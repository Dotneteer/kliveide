# UARTs: Two Serial Ports Behind One Door

A Next can fetch a web page, chat over the Internet and talk to a Raspberry Pi, yet it has no network chip
of the kind a PC has. What it has is older than the Spectrum itself: a serial port, a wire on which bits
follow one another at an agreed pace. Behind that wire sits a Wi-Fi module that does the networking, and
behind another one a Pi that can do almost anything. How does a Z80 keep up with a line that delivers a
byte every 87 microseconds, and what happens when it doesn't?

I think the serial port is the most practical piece of the Next for anyone who wants it to talk to the
world. A file from the Internet, a game played across two machines, a MIDI keyboard, a debugger on a PC:
all of them arrive as bytes on a serial line. The hardware that receives them is simple, but its buffers,
flags and timing decide whether your program gets every byte or silently loses some, and that is worth
understanding before you write the loop that reads them.

You should know two chapters first. [Interrupts: Fourteen Voices, Two
Wires](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/) explains the UARTs' interrupts and their
place in the daisy chain, which this chapter only sums up. [CTC: Four Timers in a
Ring](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-ctc/) explains the master clock and the VGA modes that the
baud rate is counted from.

## The road ahead

We start with the two UARTs and the four ports they share, then look at what actually travels on the wire:
a frame of bits. Next comes the baud rate, set by a prescaler of the master clock, and the frame register
that chooses the frame's shape. The heart of the chapter is the two buffers and the status register: how
bytes wait to be sent, how they wait to be read, and what the hardware does when a buffer fills or a frame
arrives broken; if you read only one section, read that one. After that we look at break, hardware flow
control, interrupts, the joystick port and resets.

<Callout type="info">
The facts are for **core 3.02.01**. The master clock is **28 MHz**, as in VGA mode 0, and the examples use
**115,200 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, one stop bit**, the setting after power-on. Every fact was checked
against the Next's hardware design.
</Callout>

## Two UARTs, four ports

A **UART**, a universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter, turns bytes into a stream of bits on one wire and
a stream of bits on another wire back into bytes. The Next has two of them, identical in every way, and I
call them **the twins**:

- **UART 0** is wired to the **Wi-Fi module**, an ESP chip on the board, which does the networking and
  talks to the Next through text commands on this line.
- **UART 1** is wired to the **Raspberry Pi's connector**, on GPIO 14 and 15, once bit 5 of the **PI
  Peripheral Enable register, NextReg `$A0`**, gives those pins to the UART.

The twins share one door: four ports, and a switch that says which twin is behind it.

- **Port `$153B`, UART select.** Bit 6 chooses the twin: 0 for UART 0, 1 for UART 1. Every other port talks
  to the chosen one. Bit 4 and bits 2–0 are part of the baud rate, as the next sections explain.
- **Port `$163B`, UART frame**: the shape of the frame, and three switches.
- **Port `$133B`, UART TX**: a write sends a byte; a read gives the **status**.
- **Port `$143B`, UART RX**: a read takes a received byte; a write sets part of the baud rate.

Bit 4 of NextReg `$83` switches all four ports off: they then read `$FF` and ignore writes.

1. The **start bit**, a 0, ends the rest and tells the receiver that a frame begins.
2. The **data bits**, 5 to 8 of them, **least significant first**.
3. A **parity bit**, if the two ends agreed on one: it makes the number of 1s even or odd, so that one
   flipped bit is noticed.
4. One or two **stop bits**, 1s, before the next start bit can come.

The receiver's part is the clever one. It sees the falling edge of the start bit, waits half a bit, checks
that the line is still low, and then takes each bit one bit period later, in its middle, as far as possible
from the edges where the level changes. The Next's receiver does better still: every time the line changes
level, it moves its sampling moment back to the middle of the bit, so a sender whose clock is a little fast
or slow is followed through the whole frame. It also ignores pulses shorter than 4 master clocks, 143 ns,
which are noise, not bits. I find this a lovely piece of engineering for so small a block.

With 8 data bits, no parity and one stop bit, written **8N1**, a frame is 10 bits, so 115,200 baud carries
11,520 bytes a second, one every 86.8 µs. That is the answer to the opening puzzle: at 3.5 MHz the CPU has
about 300 T-states per byte, which is plenty for a loop, and nothing like enough if the loop has to wait for
the screen or a disk.

## The baud rate

Each twin times its bits with a **prescaler**: a bit lasts exactly the prescaler's number of master clocks,
so the baud rate is 28,000,000 ÷ prescaler. The prescaler has 17 bits, written in three pieces:

```text
port $143B, bit 7 = 0:  bits 6-0 -> prescaler bits 6-0
port $143B, bit 7 = 1:  bits 6-0 -> prescaler bits 13-7
port $153B, bit 4 = 1:  bits 2-0 -> prescaler bits 16-14
```

The `$143B` writes go to the twin that is selected. The `$153B` write is a little different: its bits 2–0
go to the twin that **bit 6 of the same byte** selects, so one write can choose a twin and set its top bits
together. A read of `$153B` gives bit 6, which twin is selected, and that twin's bits 16–14; the lower 14
bits cannot be read back.

The prescaler is a whole number, so the rate is rarely exact. Here are some common ones at 28 MHz:

- **9,600 baud**: prescaler 2,917, 9,599 baud.
- **31,250 baud**, MIDI's rate: prescaler 896, exact.
- **115,200 baud**: prescaler **243**, 115,226 baud, 0.02% fast. This is the value after power-on.
- **230,400 baud**: prescaler 122, 229,508 baud, 0.4% slow.
- **921,600 baud**: prescaler 30, 933,333 baud, 1.3% fast, about as far as I would trust a serial line.
- **2,000,000 baud**: prescaler 14, exact.

The largest prescaler, 131,071, gives about 214 baud. A UART at the other end is happy with an error of a
couple of per cent, and the re-centring receiver tolerates more. Two more facts matter in practice. The
prescaler counts the **master clock**, not the CPU's, so the baud rate does not change with the CPU speed;
but it does change with the VGA mode, which runs the master clock faster, so a program that cares reads
NextReg `$11` and computes its prescaler from that mode's clock. And both ends of a frame take the prescaler
when the frame starts, so a new one applies from the next frame, never to the middle of one.

Here is UART 0 set to 230,400 baud:

```z80klive
        ld bc,$153b
        ld a,%00010000         ; UART 0, bits 16-14 follow:
        out (c),a              ; 000
        ld b,$14               ; $143B
        ld a,122               ; bit 7 = 0: bits 6-0
        out (c),a
        ld a,%10000000         ; bit 7 = 1: bits 13-7 = 0
        out (c),a
```

## The frame register

Port `$163B` holds the frame's shape for the selected twin, and three switches. It reads back all eight
bits:

```text
$163B:  R B F N N P O S
  R   = 1: reset the twin and hold it while the bit is set
  B   = 1: send a break once the transmitter is idle
  F   = 1: hardware flow control
  N N = data bits: 11 8, 10 7, 01 6, 00 5
  P   = 1: a parity bit
  O   = parity: 0 even, 1 odd
  S   = stop bits: 0 one, 1 two
```

The value after power-on is `$18`: 8 data bits, no parity, one stop bit, everything else off.

Bit 7 deserves a warning, because it does more than its name suggests. While it is 1, both buffers are
empty and **both state machines are held in reset**: a byte being sent stops in the middle, nothing is
received and nothing can be sent. It is not a pulse. Write the register with bit 7 set to clear the twin,
then write it again with bit 7 clear, or the twin stays dead. My habit: `$98`, then `$18`.

## Two buffers and the status

Between the CPU and each wire sits a buffer, a **FIFO**, first in, first out:

- **The transmit buffer holds 64 bytes.** A write to `$133B` puts a byte in it, and the transmitter takes
  them out one by one, as fast as the baud rate allows. A byte written while the buffer is full is lost.
- **The receive buffer holds 512 bytes**, and one more waits behind it while it is full. A read of `$143B`
  takes the oldest; a read of an empty buffer gives 0, which could also be a byte, so check the status
  first.

The **status**, read from `$133B`, says how both buffers and the line are doing:

- **Bit 0**: the receive buffer has at least one byte.
- **Bit 1**: the transmit buffer is full. Wait before writing.
- **Bit 2**: the receive buffer **overflowed**: a byte arrived while the 512 bytes and the one waiting behind
  them were all there, and it was lost.
- **Bit 3**: the receive buffer is **near full**, at least 384 bytes, three quarters.
- **Bit 4**: the transmit buffer is empty **and** the transmitter has finished its last frame: the line is
  quiet. This is the bit to wait for before changing the baud rate or the frame.
- **Bit 5**: the byte at the head of the receive buffer arrived after an error, an overflow or a broken
  frame. Bytes are stored with this mark, so you can tell exactly where data went missing.
- **Bit 6**: a **framing error** (a stop bit that was 0) or a **parity error** happened.
- **Bit 7**: the line is in **break**, as the next section explains.

Bits 6 and 2 are cleared by reading the status: each read reports what happened since the last one. And
here is the rule that surprises people: **a frame with an error is thrown away**. A byte whose stop bit or
parity is wrong never reaches the buffer. Bit 6 tells you it existed, and bit 5 marks the next byte that
made it through.

Two loops do most of the work. This one sends a text, waiting whenever the transmit buffer is full:

```z80klive
; send the zero-terminated text at HL on UART 0
Send:   ld bc,$153b
        xor a                  ; UART 0, prescaler untouched
        out (c),a
        ld b,$13               ; $133B: TX and status
Next:   ld a,(hl)
        or a
        ret z                  ; end of the text
Full:   in a,(c)               ; status
        bit 1,a                ; TX buffer full?
        jr nz,Full
        ld a,(hl)
        out (c),a              ; into the TX buffer
        inc hl
        jr Next
```

And this one empties the receive buffer into memory at DE, as long as it has bytes:

```z80klive
; copy what the selected UART has received to (DE)
Drain:  ld bc,$133b
Loop:   in a,(c)               ; status
        rra                    ; bit 0 into carry
        ret nc                 ; the buffer is empty
        inc b                  ; $143B: RX
        in a,(c)               ; the oldest byte
        dec b
        ld (de),a
        inc de
        jr Loop
```

My advice for receiving is to drain the whole buffer every time you look, not to take one byte. At 115,200
baud the 512 bytes fill in 44 ms, a little over two frames, so a program that reads the UART once a frame has
time to spare, and one that stops reading while it loads a level or waits for a key does not.

## Break

A **break** is the line held low for longer than a frame: no start bit, data and stop bit can look like
that, so both ends know it is a signal, not data. It is how some devices are reset or woken.

- **Sending a break**: set bit 6 of the frame register. The transmitter finishes the frame it is sending,
  then holds the line low and sends nothing more until the bit is cleared.
- **Receiving a break**: the frame fails its stop bit, a framing error that sets status bit 6, and when the
  line stays low for eight more bit periods, status bit 7 says **break** for as long as it stays low.

## Hardware flow control

The receive buffer can fill, so a fast sender needs a way to be told to wait. **Hardware flow control**,
bit 5 of the frame register, adds two signals beside the data lines, both active low:

- **CTS**, clear to send, is an input. The twin starts a frame only while the other end holds it low; while
  it is high, the transmitter takes the next byte from its buffer and waits with it in hand.
- **RTR**, ready to receive, is an output. The twin drops it when its receive buffer holds **510 bytes**, two
  short of full, so that a sender who stops at once loses nothing.

With flow control, a program can stop reading for as long as it likes, and no byte is lost: the sender is
paused instead. For the Pi, the two signals are GPIO 16 and 17. The Wi-Fi module takes part in flow control
only on issue 4 boards, so on an earlier board a program must read fast enough.

## Interrupts

Each twin can ring the CPU, and the [interrupts
chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/#the-sources-up-close) tells the whole story.
In short, from the UART's side:

- **Receive**: when the buffer gets its first byte, or, with the "near full only" bit, when it reaches 384.
  The enables are bits 0 and 1 of NextReg `$C6` for UART 0, bits 4 and 5 for UART 1.
- **Transmit**: when the transmit buffer becomes empty, which is a frame before the line falls silent,
  because the last byte is still being sent. The enables are bit 2 for UART 0 and bit 6 for UART 1.
- The receivers stand high in the daisy chain, right after the line interrupt, and the transmitters last,
  after the ULA: a byte arriving is urgent, a buffer to refill is not.
- **The requests are edges**: a byte arriving into a buffer that is not empty raises nothing new. That is the
  second reason to drain the whole buffer in the handler.

## The joystick port

Either twin can leave its own pins and use a joystick port instead, through the **Joystick I/O Mode
register, NextReg `$0B`**: bit 7 turns the I/O mode on, bits 5–4 = 10 choose the left port and 11 the right
one, and bit 0 chooses the twin, 0 for UART 0 and 1 for UART 1. The twin then sends on pin 7 and receives on
pin 9, and with flow control it takes CTS from pin 6; there is no pin for RTR. Its own lines rest high
meanwhile. A cable from a joystick port to another machine is then a serial link with no expansion at all.

## Resets

A **soft reset** selects UART 0, empties both buffers in both twins, clears their errors and stops whatever
was being sent. It keeps each twin's prescaler and frame register. Only loading the core, which a hard
reset through NextReg `$02` does, sets them back to 115,200 baud and `$18`. So a program that changed the
baud rate leaves it changed for the next program after a soft reset, and here is my last piece of advice:
set the prescaler and the frame yourself, even when you want the defaults.

## What to take home

The Next has two identical UARTs, the twins: UART 0 to the Wi-Fi module and UART 1 to the Pi's connector,
behind one set of ports. Port `$153B` bit 6 chooses the twin; `$163B` is its frame register; `$133B` sends
a byte and reads the status; `$143B` takes a received byte. A byte travels as a frame: a start bit, 5 to 8
data bits with the least significant first, an optional parity bit and one or two stop bits, sampled in the
middle of each bit by a receiver that re-centres on every edge and ignores short glitches.

The baud rate is the master clock divided by a 17-bit prescaler, written in pieces to `$143B` and `$153B`:
243 gives 115,200 baud at 28 MHz, the power-on value. It does not follow the CPU's speed, but it does follow
the VGA mode. The frame register's bit 7 holds the twin in reset for as long as it is set, bit 6 sends a
break and bit 5 switches on hardware flow control.

The transmit buffer holds 64 bytes; the receive buffer 512 and one more, near full at 384, with RTR raised
at 510. A frame with an error is thrown away, and the status says so: bit 6 for the error, bit 5 for the next
good byte, bit 2 for an overflow, bit 7 for a break. Interrupts come on the first byte or at near full, and
when the transmit buffer empties; they are edges, so a handler drains everything. Either twin can move to a
joystick port. A soft reset empties the buffers but keeps the baud rate. Read fast, or let flow control make
