# Real-Time Clock: A Watchman on Two Wires

Switch your Next off, put it in a drawer for a month, and switch it on again: it knows the date. The FPGA
forgot everything the moment the power went, the SRAM is blank, the core has been loaded from flash all
over again. So who was counting the seconds in the dark? The answer is the Next's **Real-Time Clock**, the
**RTC**: a clock that keeps the time and the date whether the machine is running or not.

A clock that survives the power switch matters more than it seems. An operating system that writes a file
wants to stamp it with the date. A game can offer a puzzle of the day, a logger wants to say when
something happened, and a program that needs a random seed has one ticking right there. None of this works
with a clock that starts at midnight on 1 January every time you switch on, and the Spectrum never had any
other kind.

You should know one chapter first. [Memory, Ports and NextRegs: How the Z80N Reaches the
Machine](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-memory-io/) explains how the Next decodes its ports and
how a NextReg switches a group of them off. [Storage: an SD Card on a Serial
Bus](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-storage/) is worth a look too: it shows a serial bus that the
hardware clocks for you, and this chapter shows one where you do the clocking yourself.

## The road ahead

We start with the clock chip itself, which lives outside the FPGA on its own battery, and the two wires that
connect it to the machine. Then come the two ports that hold those wires, and the rules of the conversation
on them: how a message starts and stops, how bits travel and how the listener says "got it". The heart of
the chapter is the part where your program speaks those rules itself, one wire at a time, and the speed
limit it must respect; if you read only one section, read that one. After that we open the clock's
registers, read the time through a snapshot, set it, use its spare memory, and see what a reset does to it.

<Callout type="info">
The facts are for **core 3.02.01** and the **DS1307** real-time clock on the Next's bus. The listings run at
**3.5 MHz**, the CPU's speed after a reset, and every time given for them is for that speed.
</Callout>

## A clock outside the machine

The Next's clock is a small chip of its own, a **DS1307**, sitting on the board beside the FPGA. I like to
call it **the watchman**: it stays awake on its own lamp while the house sleeps, and when you come down in
the morning it tells you what time it is. It is not part of the core and no reset of the FPGA reaches it.

Three things keep it going:

- **A 32,768 Hz crystal.** The number is 2<sup>15</sup>, so fifteen halvings make exactly one pulse a
  second. Many quartz watches count from the same frequency for the same reason, which keeps the crystal
  cheap and easy to find, though some watches use other frequencies.
- **A battery input** for a 3 V lithium cell, between 2.0 and 3.5 V. When the board's own supply falls
  below the battery's voltage, the chip switches over and keeps counting on less than half a microamp. A
  48 mAh cell keeps it alive for more than ten years.
- **A guard at the door.** While the supply is below 1.25 times the battery's voltage, the chip ignores the
  bus altogether, so a machine that is powering up or down cannot scribble garbage over the time.

The watchman is only as good as his crystal. A crystal that is 20 parts in a million off gains or loses
about 1.7 seconds a day, nearly a minute a month, so do not be surprised if the clock wants setting now and
then.

## Two wires, and nobody pushes

The watchman talks over **I2C**, the inter-integrated circuit bus: two wires that many chips can share. One
wire, **SCL**, is the clock, which says *when* a bit is valid. The other, **SDA**, is the data, which says
*what* the bit is.

The trick that makes sharing possible is that no chip ever drives a wire high. Each wire has a **pull-up**, a
resistor to the supply, that holds it high when it is left alone. A chip can only do two things to a wire:
pull it low, or let go. This is called **open drain**, and it means two chips can never fight, one pushing
up while the other pulls down. If anybody pulls, the wire is low; it is high only when nobody pulls. An
engineer would say the wire is the AND of everyone's wishes.

Three things share the bus on the board: the FPGA, the watchman and an internal connector for whatever an
owner wants to add. The Raspberry Pi's connector can join in too, though in a cleverer way than it looks.
Bit 3 of the **PI Peripheral Enable register, NextReg `$A0`**, gives the Pi's GPIO 2 (data) and GPIO 3
(clock) to the bus. The FPGA then pulls the Pi's pins low whenever it pulls its own, and the lines your
program reads are the AND of both sides. The Pi's pins are not wired to the watchman, though: the FPGA
stands between two buses and runs them both at once.

There is one more rule: **the Next is always the master**. It makes the clock, it starts every
conversation and it ends it. The watchman only answers.

## Two ports, one bit each

Each wire has its own port, and only bit 0 of each counts:

- **Port `$103B`** is SCL, the clock.
- **Port `$113B`** is SDA, the data.

A write of bit 0 = 0 makes the FPGA pull the wire low; bit 0 = 1 lets it go. A read gives `$FE` or `$FF`:
bits 7–1 are always 1, and bit 0 is **the wire itself**, not what you wrote. That difference is the whole
point. Write 1 to `$113B` and read it back, and you read 0 when the watchman is pulling the wire low: that is
how you hear him. The same goes for the clock wire, so a program can see if another chip holds SCL low to
ask for more time.

A reset, soft or hard, lets go of both wires. Bit 2 of the **Internal Port Decoding Enables register,
NextReg `$83`**, switches both ports off: they then read `$FF` and ignore writes.

I find these two ports a lovely piece of minimalism. Many I2C controllers in other machines are whole
chips, with shift registers, state machines and a page of registers. The Next gives you two wires and
says: the protocol is your business.

## The rules of the conversation

Since both wires rest high and anyone may pull, the chips need rules about who touches what, and when.
There is one simple rule and two exceptions.

**The rule: SDA changes only while SCL is low.** The master puts a bit on SDA, raises SCL, and the
listener reads SDA while SCL is high. Then the master lowers SCL and may change SDA for the next bit.

**The exceptions are the brackets around a message**, and they break the rule on purpose:

- **START**: SDA falls while SCL is high. Every chip on the bus wakes up and listens.
- **STOP**: SDA rises while SCL is high. The message is over and the bus is free.

Because a normal bit never changes SDA while SCL is high, nobody can mistake data for a bracket. A START
inside a message, before any STOP, is a **repeated START**: it begins a new message without letting
anybody else grab the bus in between.

Bytes go **most significant bit first**, and each byte gets a ninth clock pulse, the **acknowledge**. For
that pulse the sender lets go of SDA and the receiver answers: pulled low is an **ACK**, "got it"; left
high is a **NACK**, "no", or "no more". The first byte after a START is the **address**: seven bits saying
which chip the master wants, and an eighth saying the direction, 0 for write and 1 for read. The watchman's
address is `$68`, so `$D0` means "watchman, listen" and `$D1` means "watchman, talk".

A chip that is not addressed answers nothing, and nothing means a high SDA on the ninth pulse, a NACK. So
the address byte doubles as a roll call: if nobody acknowledges `$D0`, there is no watchman on your bus.
I always check that ACK before believing anything else the bus tells me.

## Speaking the protocol yourself

The Next has no hardware that sends an I2C byte for you. Your program raises and lowers the two wires one
`OUT` at a time, a technique called **bit-banging**. It sounds primitive, and it is, but it takes a page of
code, and here it is. The routines use BC, DE, A and the flags, and keep HL.

START and STOP are three or four writes each. Both begin with the wires in a known state:

```z80klive
; START (also a repeated START): SDA, SCL high,
; SDA low while SCL is high, then SCL low
I2cStart:
        ld bc,$113b            ; SDA
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; let SDA go
        ld b,$10               ; SCL
        out (c),a              ; let SCL go
        ld b,$11
        xor a
        out (c),a              ; SDA low: START
        ld b,$10
        out (c),a              ; SCL low
        ret

; STOP: SDA low, SCL high, then SDA high
I2cStop:
        ld bc,$113b
        xor a
        out (c),a              ; SDA low
        ld b,$10
        inc a
        out (c),a              ; let SCL go
        ld b,$11
        out (c),a              ; SDA high: STOP
        ret
```

Sending a byte is eight bits and a listen:

```z80klive
; send A, MSB first; A = the ACK bit (0 = ACK)
I2cWrite:
        ld e,a
        ld d,8                 ; eight bits
WrBit:  ld bc,$113b
        xor a
        rl e                   ; next bit into carry
        rla                    ; and into bit 0
        out (c),a              ; SDA = the bit
        ld b,$10
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; SCL high: read me
        xor a
        out (c),a              ; SCL low
        dec d
        jr nz,WrBit
        ld bc,$113b
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; let SDA go
        ld b,$10
        out (c),a              ; the ninth pulse
        ld b,$11
        in a,(c)               ; the answer
        and 1
        ld e,a
        ld b,$10
        xor a
        out (c),a              ; SCL low
        ld a,e
        ret
```

And receiving one is the mirror image: you let go of SDA, clock eight times and read the wire at every
pulse, then answer yourself.

```z80klive
; receive a byte into A; answer NACK if carry is
; set, ACK otherwise
I2cRead:
        push af                ; keep the carry
        ld bc,$113b
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; let SDA go
        ld d,8
        ld e,0
RdBit:  ld b,$10
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; SCL high
        ld b,$11
        in a,(c)               ; read SDA
        rra                    ; bit 0 into carry
        rl e                   ; and into E
        ld b,$10
        xor a
        out (c),a              ; SCL low
        dec d
        jr nz,RdBit
        pop af
        ld a,0
        rla                    ; A = carry: 0 ACK, 1 NACK
        ld b,$11
        out (c),a              ; our answer on SDA
        ld b,$10
        ld a,1
        out (c),a              ; the ninth pulse
        xor a
        out (c),a
        ld b,$11
        inc a
        out (c),a              ; let SDA go
        ld a,e
        ret
```

How it works, the parts worth a second look:

- **Every bit of `I2cWrite` sets SDA first, then pulses SCL.** The data is steady before the clock rises and
  stays steady until it falls: the rule, kept by the order of the `OUT`s.
- **For the acknowledge, the sender lets go.** `I2cWrite` writes 1 to SDA before the ninth pulse, which does
  not make the wire high: it only stops pulling. The `IN` then reads whatever the watchman does.
- **`I2cRead` writes 1 to SDA once, at the start**, and never touches it during the eight bits. The watchman
  pulls the wire for every 0 he sends; the 1s are the pull-up's work.
- **The carry chooses the answer.** Clear for ACK, "go on"; set for NACK, "that was the last one". The
  watchman stops talking after a NACK and leaves SDA to you for the STOP.

## How fast is too fast

The watchman is a 100 kHz chip: it runs I2C's standard mode only, never the faster ones. For every pulse
it asks for at least **4.0 µs with SCL high** and **4.7 µs with SCL low**. A START needs SCL high for 4.7 µs
before SDA falls and 4.0 µs after; a STOP needs 4.7 µs of SCL high before SDA rises; and between a STOP
and the next START the bus must rest for 4.7 µs.

Now look at `I2cWrite` at 3.5 MHz. A bit takes 96 T-states, 27.4 µs, which is about 36,500 bits a second,
well under the limit. The tight spot is the clock's high time: from the `OUT` that raises SCL to the one that
lowers it there is an `XOR A` and an `OUT`, 16 T-states, **4.6 µs**. That clears the 4.0 µs, but not by
much. The ACK pulse of `I2cRead` is as short; no gap in these routines is shorter.

Here is the catch: the same 16 T-states last 2.3 µs at 7 MHz and **0.57 µs at 28 MHz**, seven times too
short. A Next running at full speed outruns the watchman with the very same code. My advice is the boring
one: switch the CPU to 3.5 MHz with the **CPU Speed register, NextReg `$07`**, before you talk to the bus,
and switch it back afterwards. A read of the whole time takes under 3 ms, so you lose almost nothing. If
you would rather keep the speed, keep SCL high for at least 112 T-states and low for at least 132 at
28 MHz, and measure what you wrote.

## The watchman's registers

The watchman has 64 bytes, numbered `$00` to `$3F`. The first eight hold the time and a control byte; the
other **56 bytes are RAM**, kept by the battery with the time.

The time is in **BCD**, binary-coded decimal: each 4-bit half of a byte holds one decimal digit, so 45
seconds is `$45`, not `$2D`. It is wasteful, but a program that prints the time only has to print two hex
digits, and I suspect that was the whole idea. The spare bits carry switches:

```text
reg   7    6    5    4    3    2    1    0
$00   CH  [ tens of sec ][ seconds          ]
$01   0   [ tens of min ][ minutes          ]
$02   0   12   PM/20 10h [ hours            ]
$03   0    0    0    0    0  [ day 1-7      ]
$04   0    0  [ tens   ][ date             ]
$05   0    0    0   10m  [ month            ]
$06  [ tens of year     ][ year             ]
$07   OUT  0    0   SQWE  0    0   RS1  RS0
```

- **CH, clock halt, bit 7 of the seconds.** When it is 1 the crystal stops and so does the time. A fresh
  chip comes up with CH set, so the first thing any program that sets the clock does is clear it.
- **The hours, bit 6, choose 12- or 24-hour mode.** With bit 6 = 0, bit 5 is the second tens digit, for
  20 to 23. With bit 6 = 1, bit 5 is **PM**, and the hours count 12, 1, 2 to 11; PM flips when 11 becomes
  12, and the date moves on when 11 PM becomes 12 AM. After changing bit 6, write the hours again: the chip
  does not convert them.
- **The day of the week, 1 to 7**, steps at midnight. Which day is 1 is up to the software: the watchman
  only counts, so pick a convention (Sunday = 1 is common) and stick to it.
- **The date, month and year** roll over as a calendar should: 30 or 31 days, February 28 or 29, and the
  year from 99 to 00. The year is two digits, and every year divisible by four is a leap year, which is
  right for every year from 2000 to 2099. Somebody in 2100 will have to fix it.
- **The control register** drives the chip's square-wave pin: SQWE = 1 makes a square wave of 1 Hz,
  4,096 Hz, 8,192 Hz or 32,768 Hz by RS1–RS0 (00 to 11); with SQWE = 0, the pin follows OUT. On the Next the
  watchman reaches the FPGA only through the two bus wires, so that pin cannot interrupt the Z80.

## Reading the time

Reading a clock has a classic trap. Suppose it is 12:59:59 and you read the seconds, then the minutes, then
the hours, and between the minutes and the hours the clock ticks: you get 13:59:59, an hour out. Every
programmer of an old PC clock has met this bug.

The watchman avoids it with a **snapshot**. His counters run on their own, and on every START he copies them
into a second set of registers. Everything you read over the bus comes from the snapshot, so all seven bytes
belong to the same moment, however slowly you read them, while the real counters keep running underneath.

Here it is as code, on the routines above. It reads the seconds to the year into `Time`, in the
registers' order:

```z80klive
; read registers $00-$06 into Time (7 bytes)
ReadTime:
        call I2cStart
        ld a,$d0               ; watchman, listen
        call I2cWrite
        xor a                  ; the pointer: $00
        call I2cWrite
        call I2cStart          ; repeated START
        ld a,$d1               ; watchman, talk
        call I2cWrite
        ld hl,Time
        ld b,6                 ; six bytes with ACK
RdNext: push bc
        or a                   ; carry clear: ACK
        call I2cRead
        ld (hl),a
        inc hl
        pop bc
        djnz RdNext
        scf                    ; carry set: NACK
        call I2cRead           ; the year
        ld (hl),a
        jp I2cStop
```

At 3.5 MHz the whole read takes 10,058 T-states, 2.9 ms, about a seventh of a frame. On Friday, 18 September
2026 at 12:34:56, in 24-hour mode with Sunday as day 1, `Time` holds:

```text
$56 $34 $12 $06 $18 $09 $26
sec min hr  day dt  mon yr
```

Notice the snapshot at work in a slower read. Start at 12:59:59, take the seconds and the minutes, then
pause for over a second before the hours. You still get `$12`, because the hours came from the snapshot
taken at the START; the next read, with its own START, gets 13:00:00.

## Setting the clock

Setting is one message: START, `$D0`, the pointer, and then the bytes, each written to the register at the
pointer before the pointer steps. With `Time` holding the seven bytes in the same order:

```z80klive
; write Time (7 bytes) into registers $00-$06
SetTime:
        call I2cStart
        ld a,$d0               ; watchman, listen
        call I2cWrite
        xor a                  ; from register $00
        call I2cWrite
        ld hl,Time
        ld b,7
WrNext: push bc
        ld a,(hl)
        call I2cWrite
        inc hl
        pop bc
        djnz WrNext
        jp I2cStop
```

It takes 8,711 T-states, 2.5 ms. Two details make this the right way to do it:

- **Writing the seconds restarts the second.** The divider that halves the crystal fifteen times is cleared,
  so the next tick comes a whole second after the write. Set the clock at 12:34:56 and it says 12:34:57
  exactly one second later, not at some random moment within the second.
- **Write everything within one second of the seconds.** A tick between your seconds and your hours could
  carry into the minutes you have not written yet. One message from register `$00` up takes 2.5 ms, which
  settles it. The bytes are stored as each one is acknowledged, so a message broken off halfway keeps what it
  wrote.

And remember CH: the seconds byte you write has bit 7 clear, so the clock runs. Write `$80` or more and it
stops, which is also how you would freeze the time on purpose.

## Fifty-six bytes that remember

Registers `$08` to `$3F` are plain RAM, 56 bytes, read and written like the time and kept by the same
battery. It is not much, but it is the only memory in the Next that survives the power switch without a
file system: a high score, a setting, the date a program last ran. I would keep a byte or two of it as a
signature, so your program can tell its own data from another program's.

The pointer has two habits worth knowing:

- **It wraps from `$3F` to `$00`.** Read four bytes from `$3E` and you get `$3E`, `$3F`, then the seconds
  and the minutes.
- **It stays where it was between messages.** A read that starts with `$D1` straight after the START, with
  no pointer written first, continues from the last pointer: a **current address read**. Useful, but I
  prefer writing the pointer every time; it costs one byte and guesses nothing.

## Resets

No Next reset touches the watchman. A soft reset, a hard reset, loading a new core: the time keeps running
and the RAM keeps its bytes, because the chip is not part of the FPGA and has its own power.

The FPGA's side is another matter. A reset lets go of both wires, so the bus goes high, and it does not care
what the watchman was doing at that moment. If he was in the middle of sending a 0, he is still holding SDA
low, waiting for clock pulses that the reset will not send. He is not broken, only patient. Here is my
advice for the first thing a program does on the bus: if SDA reads 0, pulse SCL until it reads 1 (nine
pulses are always enough to finish any byte and its acknowledge), then send a STOP. After that the
watchman is listening again, and your START will be heard.

## What to take home

The Next's real-time clock is a DS1307, the watchman, a chip outside the FPGA with a 32,768 Hz crystal and
its own battery, so it keeps counting through power-off and every kind of reset. It talks over I2C: two
open-drain wires, SCL and SDA, held high by pull-ups and pulled low by anyone who wants to, so a wire is
the AND of everyone on it. Port `$103B` is SCL and port `$113B` is SDA; bit 0 of a write pulls or lets go,
and bit 0 of a read is the wire itself. The Raspberry Pi's GPIO 2 and 3 can join the bus through NextReg
`$A0` bit 3, and NextReg `$83` bit 2 switches the ports off.

The Next is always the master, and the CPU does every step itself. SDA changes only while SCL is low,
except for START and STOP, which change it while SCL is high. Bytes go most significant bit first, and a
ninth pulse carries the acknowledge, low for yes. The watchman's address is `$68`: `$D0` to write, `$D1`
to read. He is a 100 kHz chip that wants 4.0 µs of SCL high and 4.7 µs low, which the routines here meet at
3.5 MHz and miss by a factor of seven at 28 MHz, so slow the CPU down or slow the code.

The registers are BCD: seconds with the clock-halt bit, minutes, hours with a 12/24-hour switch, a day of
the week from 1 to 7, date, month, a two-digit year whose leap years are right until 2099, and a control
byte for the square-wave pin. A read writes the pointer, then a repeated START reads on from it; every
START takes a snapshot, so one read never mixes two moments. A write of the seconds restarts the second,
and a whole time written in one message takes 2.5 ms. The other 56 bytes are RAM that survives everything,
and the pointer wraps from `$3F` to `$00`. Clear CH, write the time in one go, read it in one go, and the
