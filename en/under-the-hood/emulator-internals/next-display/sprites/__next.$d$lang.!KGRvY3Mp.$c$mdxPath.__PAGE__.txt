# ZX Spectrum Next: Sprites

Almost everything that moves on a screen is a sprite: the player, the enemies, the
bullets and explosions of a game, and the pointer, cursor and dragged icon of an application. A machine
without them, like the 48K Spectrum, makes the CPU draw every moving thing into screen memory itself,
then put back what was behind it before drawing it again one step further on. That work ate most of a
Spectrum game's frame, and drawing into the bitmap is why moving objects took on the colours of the
cells they crossed. Hardware sprites take all of it off the CPU, which is left free for the game itself.

A sprite is a picture that moves on its own: the program says "put this 16 × 16 picture at X 140,
Y 90", and the hardware draws it there, in front of the background, without the program touching a
byte of screen memory. Move it, and the program changes two numbers. The Next has **128** of them,
each up to 16 × 16 dots, enlarged up to eight times in either direction, turned and mirrored, and
linked into objects far bigger than one sprite.

Here is the surprising part. A screen of 128 sprites would be a picture of its own, 320 × 256 dots, and
yet the sprite hardware never holds one. It holds **two lines**. While the beam draws one line from the
first, the hardware draws the *next* line's sprites into the second, sprite by sprite, dot by dot; then
the two swap. Everything follows from that: why sprites are drawn in a fixed order, why one can be in
front of another, and why a line with too many sprites on it simply runs out of time and loses the rest.

This article explains the sprite hardware from the bytes a program uploads to the dots on the screen.
It assumes the [big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) and
[colours and palettes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/).

## Part 1: Sprite space

A sprite's position is two **9-bit** numbers, X and Y, each 0–511. They place the sprite's top-left
dot in a square of 512 × 512 positions, **sprite space**, of which only a corner is ever shown: the
320 × 256 **wide area**, X 0–319 and Y 0–255. The Spectrum's display area sits inside it at
X 32, Y 32, so a sprite at (32, 32) starts exactly at the top-left corner of the ULA's picture, and one
at (0, 0) at the top-left corner of the border.

Why 512 positions for a 320-dot screen? So that a sprite can be **partly off** any edge. A 16-dot
sprite moving left from X 0 goes to X 511, 510, …: positions wrap round at 512, so at X 500 its first
12 dots are past the right of sprite space, out of sight, and its last 4 come back in at X 0–3. To the
program it is simply X −12. The same happens at the top with Y. With 8 bits, X could not even reach the
right-hand side of a 320-dot screen; the ninth bit reaches it and leaves 192 hidden positions across and
256 down, more than the widest sprite.

## Part 2: Patterns

A sprite's picture is a **pattern**, and the patterns live apart from the sprites, in **16K** of memory
of their own inside the FPGA. Many sprites can show the same pattern, as many cells of the tilemap can
show the same tile.

A pattern is **16 × 16 dots**, stored row after row, the left dot first, in one of two forms:

- **8-bit**: a byte a dot, **256 bytes** a pattern, so 16K holds **64** patterns. The byte is the
  palette index, and the sprite's 4-bit palette offset is added to its upper four bits.
- **4-bit**: a nibble a dot, two dots in a byte, the upper nibble on the left: **128 bytes** a pattern,
  **128** patterns in the same 16K. The nibble is the low four bits of the index, and the palette offset
  becomes the upper four.

So the two forms share one memory, and a program mixes them freely: 8-bit pattern 5 and 4-bit
patterns 10 and 11 are the same bytes. A sprite names its pattern with six bits, **N5–N0**, which pick
the 256-byte slot; that is the whole number of an 8-bit pattern. A 4-bit pattern needs one more bit, **N6**,
which picks the half of the slot: 0 the first 128 bytes, 1 the second. So the 4-bit pattern's number is
2 × slot + N6, and N6 is its *lowest* bit, even though it is the seventh one added. Which form a sprite uses is a bit of its own (the H bit of
Part 3), not a setting of the memory.

A dot is **transparent** when its value, before the palette offset, equals the Sprite Transparency
Index register, **NextReg `$4B`** (`$E3` after a reset): the whole byte for an 8-bit pattern, the low
four bits of NextReg `$4B` for a 4-bit one. The colours come from one of the two sprite palettes,
chosen by bit 3 of the Palette Control register, NextReg `$43`. The
[palette article](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/#the-other-layers-a-dot-and-an-offset)
explains the offset and why the test comes before it.

Byte 4 bits 7 and 6 together have one more meaning: `01` (H clear, so an 8-bit pattern, but N6 set,
the half-slot bit that only a 4-bit pattern uses) makes the sprite a **relative** sprite, whose bytes then mean something else (Part 5).

### Uploading: three ports

The pattern memory and the attribute table are not in the CPU's memory map. A program writes them
through three ports, each with an index that steps on by itself:

| Port | Name | What a write does |
|---|---|---|
| port `$303B` | Sprite slot select | bits 6–0 pick the sprite whose attributes the next port `$57` write sets, from its byte 0; bits 5–0 pick the 256-byte pattern slot the next port `$5B` write fills, and bit 7 starts it 128 bytes in, at the second 4-bit pattern |
| port `$57` | Sprite attributes | writes one attribute byte and steps to the next; after byte 4, or after byte 3 when its E bit is 0, it steps to the next sprite's byte 0 |
| port `$5B` | Sprite pattern | writes one pattern byte and steps to the next, through all 16K |

So a program uploads the whole table in one stream, four or five bytes a sprite, and a 4-byte sprite
simply takes four. Its byte 4 is not written, and keeps whatever it held, but no part of the hardware
reads it while E is 0.

<figure>
<svg viewBox="0 0 700 200" role="img" aria-label="One stream of bytes written to port 57. The attributes can also be written through NextRegs, which the Copper can do in the middle of a frame.
The Sprite Number register, **NextReg `$34`**, picks the sprite (bits 6–0), and the Sprite Attribute 0–4
registers, **NextRegs `$35`–`$39`**, write its bytes 0–4 directly, in any order. NextRegs **`$75`–`$79`**
write the same bytes and then step NextReg `$34` to the next sprite, so a program ends each sprite's
bytes with one of them. Normally NextReg `$34` and port `$303B` pick their sprites independently; bit 4
of the Peripheral 4 Setting register, NextReg `$09`, ties them, so that either one sets both (and then bit
7 of NextReg `$34` is port `$303B`'s 128-byte bit).

dots, two fifths of the screen's width, drawn from the same 16 × 16 pattern:

## Part 5: Anchors and relative sprites

One sprite is at most 16 × 16 dots of detail. A boss, a spaceship or a big character is several sprites
moved together, and moving them one by one means rewriting every one's position each frame. **Relative
sprites** let the hardware do it: a sprite can be placed relative to an **anchor**, the last ordinary
sprite before it in the table, and moves when the anchor moves.

A sprite is relative when its bytes 3 and 4 say so: E set (five bytes) and byte 4 bits 7–6 `01`. Every
other sprite, visible or not, is an anchor, and the relative sprites that follow it in the table, up to
the next anchor, belong to it. A relative sprite's bytes mean:

| Byte | In a relative sprite |
|---|---|
| 0, 1 | X and Y **offsets** from the anchor, signed: −128 to 127 |
| 2 | palette offset, X mirror, Y mirror, rotate; bit 0: add the anchor's palette offset to its own |
| 3 | visible, E = 1, pattern N5–N0 |
| 4 | `01` in bits 7–6; bit 5: N6, the half-slot bit of a 4-bit pattern; bits 4–1: its own scale (composite only); bit 0: add the anchor's pattern number to its own |

The anchor's T bit (byte 4 bit 5) chooses how its relative sprites follow it:

- **Composite** (T = 0): the relatives take only the anchor's **position**. Each keeps its own mirror,
  rotation and scale, so when the anchor turns, they do not. This is for objects made of separate parts:
  a ship whose turret turns on its own.
- **Unified** (T = 1): the group is **one big sprite**. The anchor's rotation, mirroring and scale apply
  to the relatives' offsets too; its rotation and mirroring are combined with each relative's own, and its
  scale replaces theirs, so the whole object turns, mirrors and grows round the anchor as one picture.

In both kinds, a relative sprite is **visible only if its anchor is**, so hiding an object takes one bit,
and it uses the anchor's 4-bit or 8-bit form (H). On the machine, four formations of three ships, all
drawn from the same pattern: an anchor (grey hull) and two relatives at (+16, 0) (blue) and (0, +16)
(green), each coloured by its own palette offset:

The unified group turns round the anchor because of how the offset is transformed. Rotation takes an
offset (*x*, *y*) to (−*y*, *x*), a quarter turn clockwise; X mirror negates *x*, Y mirror negates *y*;
then the scale multiplies both by 2, 4 or 8. Since every sprite is the same 16 × 16 and turns round its own
middle, moving each corner like this keeps the pieces together: the blue sprite at (16, 0) goes to
(0, 16), below the anchor, and the green one at (0, 16) to (−16, 0), to its left.

The two "add the anchor's" bits make objects cheap to animate and recolour: with pattern-relative set,
an object's frames can be consecutive patterns, and changing the anchor's pattern number moves every
part to its next frame; with palette-relative set, one write to the anchor recolours the whole object.

## Part 6: Two lines, not a screen

The sprites do not have a picture memory. What they have is two **line buffers**, each 320 entries
long, one for every dot of a line of the wide area. An entry is **9 bits**: the 8-bit palette index of a
sprite dot, and a ninth bit that says "a sprite dot is here".

While the beam draws line *v*, one buffer is being **shown**: read out dot by dot at the 7 MHz dot rate,
each entry cleared as soon as it has been read. At the same time the **sprite engine** fills the other
buffer with line *v* + 1: it goes through the sprite table, sprite 0 first, and for every sprite that has
a row on that line, writes the row's dots into the buffer at the sprite's X, one dot per 28 MHz clock.
When the line ends, the two buffers swap, and the buffer just shown, empty again, is the next one filled.

The order of the table has nothing to do with the order of the dots. The engine can write any of the 320
entries at any time, so a sprite drawn later can land **to the left** of one drawn earlier: sprite 5 at
X = 200 and sprite 6 at X = 40 are drawn in that order, and the line still comes out right, because
nothing reads the buffer until the whole line is in it. Sprites never have to be sorted by X, or by
anything else; their numbers only decide how long the line takes (Part 7) and which one is in front where
two overlap.

The swap happens when the wide area's horizontal counter reaches 511, the dot just before the wide
area's first dot: the end of one line's sprites is the start of the next's. A line's sprites are drawn in
the line before it is shown, from the attribute table **as it is at that moment**. So a change to the table
shows from the next line the engine draws: a sprite moved while the beam is on it shows its upper rows in
the old place and the rest in the new, and a program that rewrites a sprite below the beam can use it
again further down the same frame.

The buffer's ninth bit is also what the rest of the machine sees. The sprite layer answers "opaque" for a
dot where it is set and "transparent" where it is not, and its index goes through the sprite palette to
the [composition stage](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#part-4-six-orders),
where the layer order decides whether a sprite is in front of Layer 2 or behind it. Bit 0 of the Sprite
and Layers System register, **NextReg `$15`**, which switches the sprites on, only gates this output:
the engine keeps drawing into the buffers when the sprites are switched off, and Part 9's collision flag
keeps counting.

### Which sprite is in front

When two sprites cover the same dot, both write it into the buffer, and the order of the writes decides.
The engine always works through the table from sprite 0 to sprite 127, so normally the **later write
wins**: sprite 127 is in front of sprite 126, and sprite 1 in front of sprite 0. NextReg `$15` bit 6 turns
it round: a dot is then written only where no sprite has written yet, so the **first write wins** and
sprite 0 is in front.

## Part 7: The time budget

Part 6 showed that the engine draws a line's sprites while the beam is showing the line before it. Its
time for a line is fixed: from one swap of the line buffers to the next. This part counts how many sprites
fit into that time, and what the engine does when they do not.

### The engine's clock and the beam

The engine runs at 28 MHz, four clocks for every dot the beam moves. A 128K/+3 line is 456 dots, so the
engine has **1,824 clocks** from one swap to the next; at 48K or Pentagon timing, 448 dots and 1,792
clocks. All the numbers below are for 128K/+3 timing.

**The beam** is the dot the machine is showing at a given moment. Remember from Part 6 that the beam
shows line *v* while the engine draws line *v* + 1 into the other buffer. So when this part says "the
beam is at dot 316", it means: at that moment the machine is showing dot 316 of line *v*. It does not mean
anything about the line the engine is drawing. The beam is simply the engine's clock face, the way to tell
how far through its line's time the engine is.

The engine's clock and the beam move together, so every clock has a beam position. The swap comes one dot
before dot 0, so at clock *c* the beam is at dot *c* ÷ 4 − 1, rounded down:

| Engine clock | Beam position |
|---|---|
| 0 | the swap, one dot before dot 0 |
| 1,156 | dot 288 |
| 1,268 | dot 316 |
| 1,284 | dot 320, the first dot after the wide area |
| 1,824 | the next swap |

### What a sprite costs

The engine goes through the sprite table in order, from sprite 0 to sprite 127, starting 3 clocks after
the swap:

- A sprite that is **not on the line**, because it is hidden or has no row there, costs **1 clock**: the
  engine checks it and moves on.
- A sprite that is **on the line** costs 1 clock for the check and then **1 clock for every dot it
  walks**, which is its width: 16, 32, 64 or 128. Transparent dots cost a clock like any other, and so do
  dots that fall off the left of the line. Only a sprite that runs off the **right** of the line stops
  early, at dot 320, unless it is far enough right to wrap round to the left edge. Parked wholly off the
  right, it costs 2.

So a 1× sprite on the line costs **17 clocks**, and an 8× one 129. Dividing 1,824 by 17 suggests that
about 107 1× sprites fit on a line. In fact only **75** do when every sprite is on it, because of one more
rule.

### The cut-off window: when the engine gives up on a line

Near the end of every line there is a short stretch of time in which the engine is not allowed to begin
drawing a sprite. This article calls it the sprite's **cut-off window**: if the engine gets to a sprite
during it, that sprite is not drawn at all, and the line is cut off there. There are four of them, one for
each sprite scale, all ending just before the beam reaches dot 320:

The cut-off window belongs to the sprite the engine is about to draw: its length is that sprite's own
drawing time, so the wider the sprite, the longer its window, and the earlier it opens:

| Sprite scale | Cut-off window: the beam at | The engine's clocks | Its length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1× | dots 316–319 | 1,268–1,283 | 16 clocks |
| 2× | dots 312–319 | 1,252–1,283 | 32 clocks |
| 4× | dots 304–319 | 1,220–1,283 | 64 clocks |
| 8× | dots 288–319 | 1,156–1,283 | 128 clocks |

Nothing about it is uncertain: the same table always gives the same result. And it says nothing about where
the sprite is on the screen: a 1× sprite at X = 10 and one at X = 300 have the same cut-off window. What
decides is how late in the line the engine gets to the sprite, and that depends on how much work the
sprites before it in the table took.

The rule is: each time the engine gets to a sprite that is on the line, it looks at the beam before
drawing it. If the beam is in that sprite's cut-off window, the engine **gives up** on the line: it draws
neither this sprite nor any after it, and sets the "too many sprites" flag.

Three things follow from the rule, and the two examples below show each of them:

- What counts is the moment the engine gets to a sprite. A sprite it got to just before its cut-off window is
  drawn to its end, even though the beam moves into that window while it is being drawn.
- A sprite that is not on the line never makes the engine give up: its check does not look at the beam.
- Once the beam is past dot 319, the cut-off window is behind it, and the engine draws sprites again until
  the next swap. The cut-off window is not a deadline.

### The next swap: the hard stop

The swap is the one real deadline. Wherever the engine is when it comes, it stops and starts the next
line, even in the middle of a sprite. The dots it has already written stay, so a wide sprite late in the
table can appear cut short on its right. That also sets the flag.

### What a program can count on

- The flag is bit 1 of port `$303B` when read (written, the same port selects a sprite slot). It is **set** the moment a
  line loses sprites: when the engine gives up in a cut-off window, or when the next swap stops it in the
  middle of a sprite. Once set, it **stays set**: later lines that lose nothing do not clear it.
- **Reading** port `$303B` returns the flag and **clears** it, together with the collision flag, bit 0
  (Part 9), in the same read. A flag raised just after a read waits for the next one. A reset clears both
  as well. So the flag says that some line lost sprites since the last read; it does not say which line,
  or how many. (Klive works the flag out once a frame rather than as each line is drawn: Part 10.)
- Sprites low in the table are safe: the engine always starts at sprite 0, so the sprites that matter
  most, the player, the bullets, go first.
- Every sprite in the table costs at least one clock on every line, visible or not. A line on which all
  the work is done before clock 1,156, when the beam reaches dot 288, can never lose a sprite, whatever
  the scales.
- Wide sprites are expensive: one 8× sprite costs as much as seven and a half 1× ones. Scaling a sprite to
  fill the screen is cheap in memory and dear in time.

What is shown is decided as the buffer is read out, not as it is written: the engine draws every sprite
across the whole 320-dot line, and a window then says which dots of the line may show. The window comes
from the Clip Window Sprites register, **NextReg `$19`** (four writes, X1, X2, Y1, Y2, as the
[big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#the-clip-window-a-rectangle-for-each-layer)
describes), and its meaning depends on two bits of NextReg `$15`:

| NextReg `$15` bit 1 (over the border) | bit 5 (clip over the border) | Where sprite dots show |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | either | inside the clip window, in display-area units: dots 32 + X1 to 32 + X2, lines 32 + Y1 to 32 + Y2, and never below the display area's last line, 223 |
| 1 | 0 | the whole wide area, 320 × 256; the clip window is ignored |
| 1 | 1 | inside the clip window, in wide-area units, X doubled: dots 2 × X1 to 2 × X2 + 1, lines Y1 to Y2 |

After a reset the window is X 0–255, Y 0–191, the display area exactly. Without "over the border" a sprite
can therefore never reach the border, however the window is set; with it, the clip bit decides whether the
window still applies, now measured over the whole wide area in steps of two dots.

Over the border, sprites show even where the layer order puts the ULA in front of them: the
[composition article](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#sprites-over-the-border)
explains the exception that keeps the border from hiding them.

**What counts as a touch.** Two sprites touch only where both of them write the **same dot** of the
same line. Each of these conditions must hold:

- **Both dots are opaque.** A dot whose value equals the transparency index, NextReg `$4B` (Part 2), is
  never written into the buffer: it does not set the flag, and it leaves no mark for a later sprite to hit.
  So two sprites whose 16 × 16 squares overlap only where at least one of them is transparent do **not**
  collide. The flag is exact to the dot; it is not a test of the sprites' boxes.
- **It is the same dot.** Sprites side by side, even with no gap between them, do not collide.
- **Both sprites are drawn on that line.** A sprite the engine gave up on (Part 7) writes nothing, so it
  collides with nothing.
- **Any two sprites count**, including the parts of one object. An anchor and its relatives (Part 5)
  that overlap set the flag on every frame.

Because the check is made in the line buffer, it follows the buffer's rules, not the screen's: dots
outside the clip window collide, overlaps hidden by "sprite 0 on top" collide, and sprites switched off
with NextReg `$15` bit 0 still collide. Only dots inside the 320-dot line count. Both flags of port
`$303B` stay set until the port is read, and reading it clears both.

The flag says *that* two sprites touched somewhere, in this dot-exact sense, not *which* two or where. A game reads it once a frame as a cheap "something hit
something", and only then compares the positions of the sprites it cares about.

## Part 10: How Klive does it

### The sprite table and the patterns

Klive's Next core keeps the 128 × 5 attribute bytes and follows the hardware's port and NextReg rules for
writing them: the four-or-five-byte stream of port `$57`, the stepping of NextRegs `$75`–`$79`, and the tie
of NextReg `$09` bit 4. Pattern memory is kept a little differently: every byte written to port `$5B` is
stored eight times, once in each orientation of its pattern, and in both the 8-bit and 4-bit views, so the
renderer reads a turned or mirrored sprite as if it were stored that way. The cost is memory; the gain is
that the renderer never transforms a coordinate.

### Whole frames, with the hardware's line budget

Klive does not run the engine line by line. Before it draws the sprites, it **resolves** the table as the
hardware's engine would see it: every relative sprite composed onto its anchor (position, orientation,
scale, palette offset, pattern, visibility), by the rules of Part 5. It then works out, for each of the
256 lines, where the hardware would run out of time: the first sprite it would not finish, and how many
dots of that sprite it would have written. That is the arithmetic of Part 7, done per line in a few
sums per sprite, and it was checked against the hardware's sprite engine simulated clock by clock
on random sprite tables at both line lengths, dot for dot. Then it draws each sprite, row by row, skipping
the rows a line's budget does not reach, and stops a cut sprite at its last dot.

Drawing whole sprites instead of whole lines gives the same picture: within a line the drawing order and
the "first write" or "later write" rule are the same, so the same sprite wins every dot, and a write to the
sprite table during the frame makes the raster draw the lines the beam has passed with the old table first.
Where such a change lands can differ from the hardware by about a line, because the hardware's engine works a
line ahead of the beam. Two things happen at
a different moment than on the hardware. The collision flag and the "too many sprites" flag are worked out
once per frame, when the frame is complete, rather than as each line is drawn, so a program that reads port
`$303B` in the middle of a frame sees only what the frames before it raised.

### In the IDE

The [Sprite Inspector](/en/guides/next/sprite-inspector/) shows the attribute table, resolved the same way
(relative sprites on their anchors), and the patterns; the [Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view shows the
sprite layer alone. Sprite palettes and pattern files open in the
[sprite and palette editors](/en/guides/next/sprite-palette-editors/).

## Summary

- **128 sprites**, each placed by a 9-bit X and Y in a **512 × 512 sprite space**, of which the 320 × 256
  wide area is shown; positions wrap, so a sprite can be partly off any edge.
- **16K of patterns**: 64 8-bit patterns of 256 bytes or 128 4-bit ones of 128, mixed freely; a dot equal
  to NextReg **`$4B`** is transparent; the palette offset recolours.
- **Four or five attribute bytes** per sprite, uploaded through ports **`$303B`**, **`$57`** and **`$5B`**,
  or through NextRegs **`$34`–`$39`** and **`$75`–`$79`**.
- **Eight orientations** (rotate clockwise, then mirror) and **four sizes**, 1× to 8×, across and down
  separately.
- **Relative sprites** follow an anchor: **composite** ones take its position, **unified** ones its
  position, rotation, mirroring and scale, as one picture.
- The hardware holds **two line buffers**, not a screen: it draws the next line's sprites into one while
  the other is shown, in table order, the later write winning unless NextReg `$15` bit 6 says the first.
- A line has **1,824 clocks** at 128K timing; a 1× sprite costs **17**. If the engine gets to a sprite in its
  **cut-off window**, the last stretch before dot 320 as long as that sprite's own drawing takes, it gives
  up on the line: **75** 1× sprites fit when all are on the line. The next swap stops the engine outright. Both set port `$303B` bit 1.
- Sprites show over the border only with NextReg `$15` bit 1; the clip window, NextReg **`$19`**, is
  applied as the buffer is read.
- A **collision**, port `$303B` bit 0, is two opaque writes to one buffer entry, counted before clipping.
