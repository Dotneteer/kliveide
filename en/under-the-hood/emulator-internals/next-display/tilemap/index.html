ZX Spectrum Next: The Tilemap

Game consoles were built on one idea: most of a game screen is the same few graphics repeated, so store each graphic once, and the screen as a list of which goes where. The idea saves time as much as memory. When the scenery scrolls, the hardware moves the whole picture, and the program only adds what comes into view: a new entry in the list for each cell, instead of every dot of the new graphics.

The Next’s tilemap is that idea. A 320 × 256 picture would take 80K as a Layer 2 bitmap; the tilemap draws one in 2.5K: a grid of 40 × 32 small numbers, each naming an 8 × 8 tile to draw there. Each tile can be mirrored, rotated, given its own 16 colours and put above or below the Spectrum’s screen, tile by tile, and the whole grid scrolls by the dot. Bringing a new column of scenery onto the screen means writing 32 map entries, 64 bytes; drawing the same 8-dot column into a 320 × 256 Layer 2 picture means writing 2,048 bytes, 32 times as many.

This article explains the tilemap from the bytes in memory to the dots on screen. It assumes the big picture and colours and palettes.

How this article is organised

  • The map and the tiles (Part 1): what is stored where, and how much of bank 5 it takes.
  • The attribute byte (Part 2) and a tile’s dots (Part 3).
  • Eight orientations (Part 4): mirror and rotate, on the machine. If you read only one part, read this one.
  • Text mode (Part 5), 40 or 80 columns (Part 6), above or below the ULA (Part 7).
  • Scrolling and clipping (Part 8), fetching (Part 9) and the emulator (Part 10).

The pictures are Klive’s Next with the tilemap alone on screen (the ULA switched off, the fallback colour black), at 128K/+3 timing and 50 Hz. Each picture is written by a test that first checks the dots this article describes.

Part 1: The map and the tiles

The tilemap covers the 320 × 256 wide area with a grid of 40 columns by 32 rows of 8 × 8 cells. What it draws comes from two separate areas of memory, each with a start address of its own:

  1. The map says which tile goes where. It has one entry per cell, row after row, 40 entries to a row: the number of the tile to draw in the cell and, in a second byte, how to draw it (Part 2). The map holds no dots at all.
  2. The tile definitions say what each tile looks like. They hold the tiles’ dots, 32 bytes for each tile, tile 0 first, then tile 1, and so on. They say nothing about where a tile appears.

The map refers to the tile definitions only by number, so any number of cells can name the same tile, and changing one tile’s 32 bytes changes every cell that shows it, while changing one map entry changes one cell.

Where the two areas can be: bank 5 and bank 7

The map and the tile definitions cannot be anywhere in the Next’s memory. The tilemap reads its bytes through the same memory port as the ULA, and that port reaches only one kind of memory: the block RAM inside the FPGA, which holds exactly 24K: all 16K of bank 5, and the first 8K of bank 7. The rest of the Next’s RAM, the main memory outside the FPGA, is out of the tilemap’s reach; that is why its areas are so small. (The ULA article explains why the screen memory is kept inside the FPGA.)

For the CPU, this block RAM simply is banks 5 and 7. Counted in the 8K pages of the Next’s MMU, bank 5 is pages 10 and 11, and bank 7’s first half is page 14. Whenever the CPU reads or writes one of those three pages, wherever it has paged them in, it uses the block RAM’s other port; there is no copy of these pages in the main memory. Bank 7’s second half, page 15, is ordinary main memory, and the tilemap cannot use it.

Each area is placed on its own. The Tilemap Base Address register, NextReg $6E, says where the map starts, and the Tile Definitions Base Address register, NextReg $6F, where the tile definitions start. Both have the same layout:

Bits of NextReg $6E / $6FMeaning
70: the area is in bank 5; 1: in bank 7 (its first 8K)
6not used (0)
5–0where in the bank the area starts, in steps of 256 bytes: 0–63 in bank 5, 0–31 in bank 7

So an area always starts at a multiple of 256 bytes. As the CPU normally sees bank 5 at $4000, a value of $20 puts an area at $6000; with bit 7 set, $80 puts it at the start of bank 7, which is $C000 when port $7FFD pages bank 7 in at the top of memory, or wherever an MMU slot shows page 14. The two areas choose their bank independently: the map can be in bank 7 and the tiles in bank 5, or the other way round. An area that runs past the end of its bank continues from the bank’s start: past 16K in bank 5, past 8K in bank 7.

How big the two areas are

The size of each area is not fixed: it follows from four choices a program makes in the Tilemap Control register, NextReg $6B. Each is explained in its own part below; here is what each does to the memory.

  • A map entry is 2 bytes or 1. Normally an entry is two bytes: the tile number, and an attribute byte that says how to draw the tile (its colours, mirroring, rotation). When every tile can be drawn the same way, a program can drop the attribute byte and use one shared attribute for all tiles; each entry is then 1 byte, the tile number alone (Part 2).
  • The grid is 40 or 80 columns. 40 × 32 is 1,280 cells. In 80-column mode the cells are half as wide and there are 80 × 32 = 2,560 of them, so the map holds twice as many entries (Part 6).
  • A tile is 32 bytes or 8. A normal tile has 8 × 8 dots of 4 bits each, two dots in a byte: 8 rows of 4 bytes, 32 bytes. In text mode a dot is a single bit, as in the Spectrum’s font: 8 rows of 1 byte, 8 bytes a tile (Part 5).
  • There are 256 tiles or 512. A tile number is a byte, 0–255; in 512-tile mode the attribute lends a ninth bit, and the tile definitions can hold twice as many tiles (Part 2).

The size of each area is then a product:

AreaOne itemHow manyTotal
The mapan entry: 2 bytes, or 1a cell: 1,280, or 2,560 in 80 columns1,280 to 5,120 bytes
The tile definitionsa tile: 32 bytes, or 8 in text mode256 tiles, or 5122,048 to 16,384 bytes

That has to fit in bank 5, which is only 16K, and the ULA’s screen already uses 6.75K of it:

The sums are tight. A map of 2,560 bytes and 256 tiles of 8,192 come to 10,752 bytes, and with the ULA’s 6,912 that is 17,664: more than bank 5 holds. This is where bank 7 earns its place: its 8K is exactly the size of 256 tiles.

Layouts that fit

Here are four common ways to share the 24K, each with the values for NextRegs $6E and $6F. Bank 5’s addresses are those the CPU normally sees; bank 7’s assume it is paged in at $C000. The bytes right after the ULA’s screen, from $5B00, are where 48K BASIC keeps its system variables, so these layouts suit a program that does not return to BASIC.

LayoutThe mapThe tile definitionsNextRegs $6E, $6FThe ULA’s screen
256 tiles beside the ULAbank 5, $5B00–$64FF (40 × 32, 2-byte entries)bank 7, $C000–$DFFF (256 tiles)$1B, $80kept
All in bank 5, fewer tilesbank 5, $5B00–$64FFbank 5, $6500–$7FFF (216 tiles)$1B, $25kept
512 tilesbank 7, $C000–$C9FFall of bank 5, $4000–$7FFF (512 tiles)$80, $00gone: switch the ULA off
80 columns of textbank 5, $5B00–$6EFF (80 × 32, 2-byte entries)bank 5, base $6F00: characters 32–127 at $7000–$72FF$1B, $2Fkept

Two of these give something up. The first uses bank 7’s first half, which is also where a 128K program keeps its shadow screen (port $7FFD bit 3): the two cannot be used at once. The third fills bank 5 with tiles, so the ULA, which reads bank 5 for its own screen, would show tile bytes as a scrambled picture; a program switches it off with bit 7 of the ULA Control register, NextReg $68. The text layout’s tile definitions start at $6F00 but hold only characters 32–127: a character’s tile number is its code, so tiles 0–31 are simply never used, and their 256 bytes may hold anything.

A trap: moving a setup from bank 5 to bank 7

The 8K limit of bank 7 is easy to forget, because to the CPU bank 7 looks like any other 16K bank. Here is how it catches a program out.

Suppose a program has the map at $4000 and the tile definitions at $6000, both in bank 5: NextReg $6E is $00 (bank 5, offset 0) and NextReg $6F is $20 (bank 5, offset 32 × 256 = 8K). It works. Now the program copies all of bank 5 into bank 7, byte for byte, to free bank 5 for something else, and switches both areas to bank 7 with the same offsets: $6E = $80 and $6F = $A0.

The map still works: offset 0 in bank 7 is the start of page 14, where the map’s copy now is. The tiles do not. The copy put bank 5’s first 8K, the map, into page 14, the block RAM, but bank 5’s second 8K, the tiles, into page 15, the main memory the tilemap cannot see. And asking for offset 32, 8K into bank 7, does not reach page 15 anyway: only the low 13 bits of the address are used in bank 7, so 8K wraps round to 0, the start of page 14. The tilemap reads its tile definitions from the map’s bytes: tiles 0–79 are made of the 2,560 bytes of the map, and tiles 80–255 of whatever else was copied from bank 5’s $4A00–$5FFF, often the ULA’s screen. The screen shows the right arrangement of cells, each drawn as noise. (Klive’s Next draws exactly this: with $A0, the picture is identical to the one with $80.)

What works instead is to put everything the tilemap reads into bank 5 or page 14, never page 15:

  • The tiles in bank 7, the map in bank 5. Copy the 8K of tile definitions to the start of bank 7 and set NextReg $6F to $80; leave the map in bank 5 with NextReg $6E at $00. This is the “256 tiles beside the ULA” layout above.
  • Both in bank 7, if they fit in 8K together. A map of 2,560 bytes and 176 tiles of 32 bytes come to exactly 8,192: for instance the map at offset 0 ($6E = $80) and the tiles at offset 10, right after the map’s 2,560 bytes ($6F = $8A).

After a reset, NextReg $6E is $2C and NextReg $6F is $0C: the map at $6C00 and the tiles at $4C00, inside the ULA’s bitmap. Programs set both before they switch the tilemap on.

Part 2: The attribute byte

A map entry is two bytes: the tile number, 0–255, and an attribute byte that says how to draw it:

When every tile can be drawn the same way, the attribute is wasted space. Bit 5 of the Tilemap Control register, NextReg $6B, removes it: each entry is then one byte, the map halves to 1,280 bytes, and every tile uses the Default Tilemap Attribute register, NextReg $6C.

512 tiles. Bit 1 of NextReg $6B doubles the tile numbers: bit 0 of the attribute becomes the ninth bit of the tile number, 0–511, and 512 tiles take the whole of bank 5. The bit no longer says “ULA over this tile”, so in 512-tile mode every tile is below the ULA unless bit 0 of NextReg $6B puts the whole tilemap on top (Part 7).

Part 3: A tile’s dots

A tile is 8 × 8 dots of 4 bits: 32 bytes, 4 bytes for each row, two dots in a byte, the high nibble on the left. The dot at column x of row y of tile t is in byte t × 32 + y × 4 + x ÷ 2 of the tile definitions.

A 4-bit dot is a number 0–15, and the attribute’s palette offset becomes the high four bits of the index into the tilemap palette, as the palette article explains. So one tile can be drawn in any of 16 sets of 16 colours: a red and a blue enemy, or a lit and an unlit window, are the same 32 bytes with a different offset. Bit 4 of NextReg $6B chooses which of the two tilemap palettes the tilemap uses.

The tilemap’s transparency is decided on the 4-bit dot before the palette: a dot equal to bits 3–0 of the Tilemap Transparency Index register, NextReg $4C, $F after a reset, is transparent, whatever the offset. So the empty parts of a tile let the layers below it show.

Part 4: Eight orientations

Bits 3–1 of the attribute turn a tile into any of eight versions of itself: the eight ways an 8 × 8 square can be laid down, four turns and their mirror images. One tile drawn eight times, with attributes $00 to $0E; the red dot is the tile’s top-left dot:

The rotate bit always turns the tile 90 degrees clockwise: there is no setting for the other direction or for half a turn, because the mirrors provide those. The rule is: rotate 90 degrees clockwise first, then mirror. Rotation moves the tile’s dot (u, v) to (7 − v, u); then X mirror takes the column x to 7 − x, and Y mirror the row y to 7 − y. The two mirrors together are a half turn, 180 degrees, so rotate plus X and Y ($0E) is 90 + 180 = 270 degrees clockwise: 90 degrees anticlockwise.

The hardware does it without moving a single dot. To draw the dot at column x, row y of the cell, it simply reads a different dot of the tile: it turns x into 7 − x for X mirror (and, because a rotation also flips across, for rotate too, the two cancelling when both are set), turns y into 7 − y for Y mirror, and for rotate swaps the two before it forms the address. Three bits of the attribute change three bits of the address: the transformation costs nothing.

Why eight? Because game graphics are full of symmetry. A pipe, a wall, a corner, a ladder, a character facing left or right: one tile per shape covers every direction, and the tile set, which is the expensive part of bank 5, shrinks by up to eight times.

Part 5: Text mode

Bit 3 of NextReg $6B switches the tilemap to text mode. A tile becomes a character in the way the Spectrum’s font is: 1 bit a dot, 8 bytes a tile, one byte a row, bit 7 on the left. Two hundred and fifty-six characters take 2K instead of 8K, and a copy of the Spectrum’s font fits in 768 bytes.

A dot of one bit cannot pick a colour by itself, so the attribute does more of the work: its bits 7–1 become a 7-bit palette offset, and the dot is bit 0 of the index. So every character picks its own pair of palette entries, even for paper and odd for ink, from 128 pairs. The price is the other attribute bits: in text mode there is no mirroring and no rotation, and bit 0 of the attribute is colour too.

Transparency changes with it: a text-mode dot is transparent when its colour, after the palette, equals the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14, the rule the ULA and Layer 2 use, and not when its index matches NextReg $4C. Part 6: 40 or 80 columns

Bit 6 of NextReg $6B doubles the columns to 80 × 32. The cells keep their 8 dots, but the dots are 14 MHz dots, half as wide, so 80 cells fill the same 320-dot width as 40 did, 640 dots across, like Layer 2’s 640 mode. The map doubles to 80 entries a row, 5,120 bytes with attributes.

Eighty columns are for text: with text mode, an 80 × 32 screen of characters, the size of a terminal or a word processor, from 5K of map and a font. With 4-bit tiles they work as well, at half the width per dot.

Part 7: Above or below the ULA

The tilemap and the ULA are mixed into one layer before the layer order is applied, and the attribute’s bit 0 decides, tile by tile, which of the two is in front: 0 puts the tile above the ULA, 1 puts the ULA above the tile. Bit 0 of NextReg $6B overrides every tile and puts the whole tilemap on top. The composition article explains the mix, and its stencil mode.

This is how a game puts a tile-based playfield behind a Spectrum-style status panel, or a text window in front of the Spectrum’s screen, and why the tilemap and the ULA share one memory: they are, in the end, one layer.

Part 8: Scrolling and clipping

The tilemap has its own scroll: an X offset of 10 bits, bits 1–0 of the Tilemap X Scroll MSB register, NextReg $2F, above the Tilemap X Scroll LSB register, NextReg $30, and a Y offset in the Tilemap Offset Y register, NextReg $31. The picture wraps round at its own size:

ModeX offsetWraps across atY offsetWraps down at
40 columns0–319 dots3200–255256
80 columns0–639 dots of 14 MHz6400–255256

The grid covers exactly the wide area, so a scroll never shows anything but the map: what leaves on one side comes back on the other. A game that scrolls endlessly writes the next column of tiles into the map column that has just left the screen, one byte or two per row, 32 rows: 64 bytes for a whole new column.

The tilemap’s clip window, the Clip Window Tilemap register, NextReg $1B, works in the wide area: X in units of two 7 MHz dots (four dots in 80 columns), so 0–159 covers the width, and Y one unit a line. It starts as 0–159, 0–255, the whole grid, as the big picture describes.

Part 9: Fetching a character ahead

The tilemap reads bank 5 through the same port of the block RAM as the ULA. The ULA, which needs its bytes at exact moments, goes first; the tilemap takes every cycle the ULA does not use. The block RAM runs at 28 MHz, and the ULA uses only a few of its cycles, so there is plenty left.

To be ready in time, the tilemap works one character ahead. While the dots of one cell are being drawn from a small buffer, it fetches the next cell into the buffer’s other half: the map entry, one or two bytes, then the tile’s row, a read for each dot. When the beam reaches the next cell, its dots are waiting, and the halves swap.

A scrolled screen does not line its cells up with the fetch: then a stretch of 8 dots on screen is the end of one tile and the start of the next, and the tilemap reads the second map entry part-way. Its settings, the base addresses, the attribute mode, text mode, 512 tiles and the on-top bit, are taken again for every cell it fetches, so a program or the Copper can change them between two cells of the same line.

Part 10: How Klive does it

One renderer

Klive’s Next core draws the tilemap with one function, zxnextUlaRenderTilemapScreen, which works through the wide area dot by dot. For each dot it applies the scroll and the wrap at 320 or 640, finds the map entry at row × 40 (or 80) + column, reads the tile number and attribute (or uses NextReg $6C), and reads the dot: in standard mode through the orientation rule of Part 4, the high or low nibble of byte t × 32 + y × 4 + x ÷ 2, transparent when it equals NextReg $4C; in text mode bit 7 − x of byte t × 8 + y, transparent by its colour. It writes the colour with the below flag into the tilemap’s buffer for stage 2. Bank 7’s 8K wraps as on the hardware.

The fetch of Part 9 is not modelled: on the hardware it never delays anything, so there is nothing to imitate.

The Tilemap Inspector shows the map, the tile definitions and the registers, and the Layers view shows the tilemap alone.

Summary

  • The tilemap draws the 320 × 256 wide area from a grid of 40 × 32 (or 80 × 32) cells of 8 × 8 dots: a map, one entry per cell, and tile definitions, both in bank 5 or the 8K of bank 7, at the multiples of 256 bytes set in NextRegs $6E and $6F.
  • An entry is a tile number and an attribute: palette offset, X mirror, Y mirror, rotate, and the ULA-over-tile bit (or the ninth tile bit in 512-tile mode). NextReg $6B bit 5 drops the attribute for the one in NextReg $6C.
  • A tile is 32 bytes, 4 bits a dot; the palette offset adds the high four bits, and a dot equal to NextReg $4C is transparent.
  • Rotate (90 degrees clockwise) then mirror gives eight orientations of each tile, at no cost: the hardware changes which dot it reads.
  • Text mode makes tiles 1 bit a dot, 8 bytes each, with a 7-bit palette offset for a colour pair per character and no mirroring; 80 columns halves the width of a cell.
  • The tilemap scrolls by its own offsets (NextRegs $2F, $30, $31), wrapping at its own size, and clips in wide-area units (NextReg $1B).
  • It reads bank 5 after the ULA, one character ahead, and takes its settings afresh for every cell.
  • Klive draws it with one renderer, dot by dot, by the same rules.
