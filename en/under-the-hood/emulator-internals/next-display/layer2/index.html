ZX Spectrum Next: Layer 2

Layer 2 is the simplest idea on the Next: one byte, one dot, any colour. No attributes, no colour clash, no scrambled addresses. Yet it has the most surprising memory layout of all the layers: in two of its three resolutions the bytes do not run across the screen, line after line, as every other bitmap does. They run down it, column after column.

This article explains Layer 2 from its memory up: where its bytes are, why they are ordered as they are, how they become dots in three resolutions, and how the hardware reads them fast enough. It assumes the big picture (the layers, the wide area, clip windows and scrolling in general) and colours and palettes (how a byte becomes a colour, and when it is transparent).

How this article is organised

  • The idea (Part 1): one byte for each dot, and what that costs.
  • Where it lives (Part 2): 16K banks, the active and the shadow picture.
  • Three resolutions, two orders (Part 3): across in 256 × 192, down in 320 × 256. If you read only one part, read this one.
  • 640 × 256 (Part 4): two 4-bit dots in a byte.
  • The CPU’s view (Part 5): port $123B and the MMU.
  • Scrolling, clipping, colours (Part 6), fetching (Part 7) and the emulator (Part 8).

The pictures are Klive’s Next with Layer 2 alone on screen (the ULA switched off, the fallback colour black), at 128K/+3 timing and 50 Hz. Each picture is written by a test that first checks the dots this article describes.

Part 1: One byte, one dot

The ULA spends one bit on each dot and one attribute byte on every 8 × 8 cell, so 6,912 bytes paint the whole Spectrum screen, at the price of two colours per cell. Layer 2 spends a whole byte on each dot. The byte is a palette index, so every dot can have any of the 256 colours of the Layer 2 palette, which are any 256 of the Next’s 512.

The price is seven times the memory, and seven times the work for a CPU that redraws the picture. That is why Layer 2 could not simply be one more ULA mode. The ULA’s memory is bank 5, 16K kept inside the FPGA, as the ULA article explains; a 48K or 80K picture does not fit there. Layer 2 is a layer of its own, with its own way into the Next’s main memory.

Part 2: Where Layer 2 lives

Layer 2 lives in the Next’s ordinary RAM, counted in 16K banks: bank 5 is the ULA’s screen, bank 0 the Spectrum’s usual top 16K, and a 2 MB Next has 112 of them. Bits 6–0 of the Layer 2 Active RAM bank register, NextReg $12, name the bank where the picture starts; it continues in the banks after it. A 256 × 192 picture takes 3 banks (48K), the 320 × 256 and 640 × 256 pictures 5 banks (80K).

Beside it, the Layer 2 Shadow RAM bank register, NextReg $13, names a second picture: the shadow. Layer 2 always shows the picture of NextReg $12; the shadow is never shown, and exists only for the CPU, which can map either one into memory (Part 5). A program draws the next frame into the shadow while the active picture is on screen, then swaps the two numbers: double buffering, with no byte copied.

Two details. The defaults, banks 8 and 11, suit the 256 × 192 mode: in the wide modes the active picture runs into the shadow’s first two banks, and a program that wants double buffering sets NextReg $13 to 13 or beyond. And a picture placed so that it runs past the end of the memory the Next can address has no dots there: those parts of Layer 2 are transparent.

Part 3: Three resolutions, two orders

Bits 5–4 of the Layer 2 Control register, NextReg $70, choose the resolution:

NextReg $70 bits 5–4ResolutionBits a dotWhereMemoryByte order
00256 × 1928the display area48K, 3 banksacross: line after line
01320 × 2568the wide area80K, 5 banksdown: column after column
10640 × 2564the wide area80K, 5 banksdown: column after column

The 256 × 192 mode covers the ULA’s display area exactly. The two wide modes cover the 320 × 256 wide area, the ULA’s display area with 32 dots of border on every side.

Why down?

Look at how the hardware turns a position into an address. In 256 × 192, the line y and the dot x are each a byte, 0–255 (the line only up to 191), and the address of the dot within the picture is the two bytes side by side: y as the high byte, x as the low byte. No arithmetic at all, and the dots run across the screen, because x is the part that counts up first.

Now try the same with 320 dots across. The dot no longer fits in a byte, it needs 9 bits, 0–319, so “line high, dot low” would leave a gap: the address of line 1 would have to start at 320, which takes a multiplication, y × 320 + x. Turn the picture on its side and the problem disappears. The wide modes have exactly 256 lines, so the line is the coordinate that fills a byte, and the address is again two coordinates side by side: the column x as the high part, the line y as the low byte. The dots now run down the screen, and each column of the picture is one 256-byte page of memory.

So the rule is the same in every mode: whichever coordinate is 256 long goes in the low byte. On the machine, with the same 80K of bytes in both modes: eight colours, each painted over 2,048 bytes in a row of memory.

For a program, the column order has its own advantages. Moving down a column is INC L, and moving across is INC H; an 8K memory page holds exactly 32 whole columns. Vertical lines, sprites and scrolling text that walk down the screen become the cheap direction, where on the ULA screen, with its scrambled lines, moving down was the expensive one.

Part 4: 640 × 256: two dots in a byte

The third mode doubles the dots across, to 640, each one a 14 MHz dot, half the width of a standard dot. The memory stays at 80K and the order stays column after column: what changes is the byte. It now holds two dots, four bits each, the high nibble on the left:

Why four bits, and not a byte for each of the 640 dots? Because of Part 7: Layer 2 gets one byte for every 7 MHz dot from memory, and that is the same in all three modes. At 320 across a byte is a dot; at 640 across the same byte has to serve two dots, so each gets half of it. The 640 mode is the 320 mode’s memory, read at the same pace, split differently.

Four bits give each dot 16 colours, and the palette offset, bits 3–0 of NextReg $70, picks which 16: it becomes the high four bits of every index, as the palette article explains. On the machine, three lines of an 80-column text, ink nibble $F and paper nibble $0, with two offsets:

Part 5: The CPU’s view

Layer 2 reads its picture from banks the CPU does not see unless it asks to. There are two ways to ask.

Port $123B

The Layer 2 control port, port $123B, can lay the picture over the bottom of the CPU’s memory:

Bits of port $123BMeaning
7–6which part: 00, 01, 10 the first, second or third 16K at $0000–$3FFF; 11 the first 48K at $0000–$BFFF
5not used (0)
40 for this layout; 1 for the bank offset below
30 maps the active picture (NextReg $12), 1 the shadow (NextReg $13)
21: CPU reads come from Layer 2
11: Layer 2 is shown (also NextReg $69 bit 7)
01: CPU writes go to Layer 2

A write with bit 4 set stores a bank offset in bits 2–0 instead, 0–7 banks, added to the part chosen in bits 7–6. The 48K window shows only three of a wide picture’s five banks; with an offset, any of them can be reached.

Four ways to use it

The separate switches for reads and writes are what make the port useful: with writes only, the CPU still reads, and runs, whatever is normally at those addresses, the ROM at $0000–$3FFF for a start, while every write there lands in Layer 2. Here are four common uses, each with the port value that sets it up.

1. Plot a dot, one third at a time (layout 1, writes only, 256 × 192). The three thirds of a 256 × 192 picture are 64 lines each, and bits 7–6 of the line number are the third’s number. So the line’s top two bits go straight into the port, and the rest of the line and the dot become the address, H the line within the third, L the dot:

; Plot x = E, y = D (0-191) in the colour at L2Ink
Plot:   ld a,d
        and $c0                 ; y's bits 7-6 pick the third: port bits 7-6
        or $03                  ; bit 1: Layer 2 shown; bit 0: writes mapped
        ld bc,$123b
        out (c),a
        ld a,d
        and $3f                 ; the line within the third
        ld h,a
        ld l,e                  ; the dot
        ld a,(L2Ink)
        ld (hl),a               ; into Layer 2; reads of $0000-$3FFF still see the ROM
        ret

The routine can live anywhere, even in the area it draws through, because only its writes are redirected, and only those below $4000: its stack can be anywhere from $4000 up.

2. Work on the whole picture at once (layout 2, reads and writes, 256 × 192). With port value $C7 the whole 48K picture sits at $0000–$BFFF with no gaps: H is the line and L the dot, and a program can read a dot, change it and write it back. This replaces one colour with another over the whole screen:

        di                      ; the ROM's interrupt handler at $0038 is not there any more
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$c7                ; layout 2, reads (bit 2) and writes (bit 0) mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ld hl,$0000
Next:   ld a,(hl)
        cp $e0                  ; red...
        jr nz,Skip
        ld (hl),$1c             ; ...becomes green
Skip:   inc hl
        ld a,h
        cp $c0
        jr nz,Next
        ld a,$02                ; unmap again, Layer 2 still shown
        out (c),a

With reads mapped, everything the CPU fetches below $C000 comes from Layer 2, its own instructions included, so the code, its stack and its data must be at $C000 or above, and interrupts off.

3. Reach all 80K of a wide picture (layout 2 with a bank offset, 320 × 256). In the wide modes the 48K window holds columns 0–191, again with no gaps, but now H is the column and L the line: moving down a column is INC L. For the columns from 192 on, a write with bit 4 set moves the window: with bank offset 2 it shows banks 2–4, columns 128–319, and column x is at H = x − 128. This draws the whole of column 300 in green:

        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$c3                ; layout 2, writes mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ld a,$12                ; bit 4 set: bank offset 2
        out (c),a
        ld h,300-128            ; column 300
        ld l,0                  ; from line 0...
        ld b,0
Col:    ld (hl),$1c
        inc l                   ; ...down all 256 lines
        djnz Col
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$10                ; the offset back to 0
        out (c),a
        ld a,$02                ; unmap
        out (c),a

Port value $C3 maps writes only, so this code can run anywhere, below $C000 too: the CPU’s instruction fetches and other reads are not redirected and still see the normal memory. What moves is every write below $C000: while the port is set, those go to Layer 2, so the stack, which CALL, PUSH and an interrupt write to, must be at $C000 or above, and so must any variable the code updates.

4. Draw off screen, then show (bit 3, writes only). Port value $CB maps the shadow picture, NextReg $13, while NextReg $12’s stays on screen, untouched. The program draws the whole next frame into the shadow, then swaps the two bank numbers at the frame interrupt, and the new frame appears at once, never half drawn:

        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$cb                ; layout 2, the shadow (bit 3), writes mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ; ... draw the next frame with ordinary writes to $0000-$BFFF ...
        ld a,$02
        out (c),a               ; unmap
        halt                    ; the frame interrupt: the beam is past the picture
        nextreg $12,11          ; the shadow becomes the picture shown...
        nextreg $13,8           ; ...and the old picture the next shadow

With writes mapped, every write below $C000 goes to Layer 2, the CPU’s own pushes included, so the stack must be at $C000 or above while the port is set.

The MMU

The other way is the Next’s MMU, the eight MMU slot registers, NextRegs $50–$57, which page any 8K page of RAM into any 8K slot of the CPU’s 64K. A 16K bank b is 8K pages 2b and 2b + 1, so the default picture, banks 8–12, is pages 16–25. In the wide modes an 8K page is exactly 32 columns, so a program can page in the columns it is drawing and leave the rest of memory alone.

Part 6: Scrolling, clipping and colours

Layer 2 scrolls by its own offsets: the Layer2 X Scroll LSB register, NextReg $16, with bit 0 of the Layer 2 X Scroll MSB register, NextReg $71, as a ninth bit, and the Layer2 Y Scroll register, NextReg $17. The picture wraps round at its own size:

ModeX offsetWraps across atY offsetWraps down at
256 × 1920–255 dots (NextReg $71 is not used)2560–191192
320 × 2560–319 dots3200–255256
640 × 2560–319, in steps of two dots6400–255256

Its clip window, the Clip Window Layer 2 register, NextReg $18, counts in the area the mode covers: in 256 × 192, the display area, one unit a dot; in the wide modes, the wide area, X in units of two 7 MHz dots (four dots in 640 × 256) so that 0–159 spans the 320 positions, and Y one unit a line. The clip window starts as 0–255, 0–191, which in a wide mode leaves the bottom 64 lines clipped until a program writes 0–159, 0–255. The big picture has the details of both.

Its colours come through the two Layer 2 palettes; the palette offset of NextReg $70 is added to the high four bits of an 8-bit dot and becomes the high four bits of a 4-bit one. Layer 2 is transparent where its colour, after the palette, equals the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14, $E3 after a reset. And a palette entry can carry the priority bit that lifts its dots above every other layer, as the composition article shows.

Every one of these settings, and the bank and the resolution, is taken again for every dot, so a change made in the middle of a line, by the CPU or the Copper, shows from the next dot.

Part 7: Fetching a byte for every dot

The ULA reads block RAM inside the FPGA. Layer 2 reads the Next’s main memory, an external static RAM shared with the CPU and the DMA. It needs one byte for every 7 MHz dot while it draws, a read about every 140 nanoseconds, and the CPU at 28 MHz wants the same memory too. How do they not get in each other’s way?

The memory is fast enough to be shared by time. It completes one access in every tick of the 28 MHz clock, four accesses per dot, and each tick goes to whoever asks, in a fixed order of priority: the CPU first, Layer 2 second, and the DMA with whatever is left.

Layer 2 can afford second place because it is never in a hurry: it asks for each byte a dot ahead of the dot it draws, so a request that waits a tick still arrives in time. The CPU never waits for Layer 2 at all, at any speed; unlike the ULA’s memory on a Spectrum, Layer 2’s has no contention. The one byte per dot is also what sets the 640 mode’s four bits, Part 4’s answer.

Part 8: How Klive does it

Three renderers

Klive’s Next core draws Layer 2 in C with one renderer for each resolution: zxnextUlaRenderLayer2_256x192Screen, ..._320x256Screen and ..._640x256Screen. Each works through its area dot by dot, forms the address as Part 3 describes, line high in 256 × 192 and column high in the wide modes, applies the scroll with the same wrap at 192, 256 or 320, reads the byte from the banks of NextReg $12, and turns it into a colour through the Layer 2 palette with the offset. It then writes either nothing, for a transparent dot, or the colour with the priority bit into Layer 2’s buffer, for stage 2 to compose. A bank past the end of memory gives no dot, as on the hardware. The 640 renderer reads one byte per 7 MHz position and writes two dots from it.

Klive does not model the memory sharing of Part 7: on the hardware it never delays the CPU or the picture, so there is nothing to imitate.

The CPU’s view and the IDE

Port $123B’s mapping is part of Klive’s memory decoding, zxnextMemoryResolveLayer2Offset, which lays the chosen bank over the CPU’s view for reads, writes or both, as Part 5 describes. The IDE’s Layer 2 Inspector shows the picture’s banks, registers and palette, and the Layers view shows Layer 2 alone.

Summary

  • Layer 2 is a bitmap with one byte for every dot, a palette index, in the Next’s main memory: 48K or 80K in 16K banks from the bank in NextReg $12. NextReg $13 names a shadow picture for double buffering; only NextReg $12’s is shown.
  • NextReg $70 bits 5–4 choose 256 × 192 (the display area), 320 × 256 or 640 × 256 (the wide area). The address of a dot is its two coordinates side by side, the one that is 256 long in the low byte: in 256 × 192 the bytes run across, in the wide modes down, a column per 256-byte page.
  • In 640 × 256 a byte holds two 4-bit dots, high nibble first, because Layer 2 gets one byte per 7 MHz dot in every mode; the palette offset gives them their high four bits.
  • The CPU reaches the picture through port $123B, one 16K bank at $0000 or 48K at $0000–$BFFF, for reads, writes or both, with a bank offset, or through the MMU.
  • Layer 2 has its own scroll (NextRegs $16, $71, $17), wrapping at its own size, and its own clip window (NextReg $18) in the units of its area; it is transparent where its colour equals the value in NextReg $14.
  • Its bytes come from the main memory, which makes four accesses per dot: the CPU first, Layer 2 one of them, so Layer 2 never slows the CPU down.
  • Klive draws each resolution with its own renderer, rule for rule as above.
