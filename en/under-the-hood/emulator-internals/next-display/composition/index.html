ZX Spectrum Next: Putting It Together

Four candidates for every dot, and one winner. The rules that pick it fit on a page, yet they give the Next layers that can swap places on every line, stencils that cut one picture to the shape of another, colours that add up across layers, and a single Layer 2 colour that can climb above everything else on the screen.

This article is about those rules: stage 2 of the video pipeline, the step that turns four answers into one colour. It comes after the articles on the big picture, whose Part 4 and Part 5 introduce the answers and the pipeline, and on colours and palettes, which explains how each layer’s colour and transparency are made. Here we take those answers as given and follow what stage 2 does with them.

How this article is organised

  • Stage 2 at a glance (Part 1): what goes in, what comes out.
  • Transparent or not (Part 2): the four reasons a layer has nothing to show at a dot.
  • The ULA and the tilemap become one (Part 3): the below flag, and stencil mode.
  • Six orders (Part 4): the heart of it, on the machine. If you read only one part, read this one.
  • Two exceptions (Part 5): the Layer 2 priority bit, and sprites over the border.
  • Blending (Part 6): adding colours instead of choosing one.
  • Nothing opaque (Part 7) and the emulator (Part 8).

The pictures are Klive’s Next running the scene of the first article, with Layer 2 and the tilemap scrolled so that every pair of layers overlaps somewhere. Each picture is checked by a test before it is written: the colour of each marked dot is the one this article predicts.

Part 1: Stage 2 at a glance

Stage 2 gets, for one dot, four colours, one from each layer: the ULA (or LoRes), the tilemap, Layer 2 and the sprites. With each comes the layer’s answer to “am I transparent here?” and a few flags: the ULA says whether its dot is border, the tilemap whether the tile is below the ULA, and Layer 2 whether its palette entry carries the priority bit.

From these it makes one 9-bit colour in two steps. First the ULA and the tilemap are mixed into one layer, called U. Then the three remaining layers, S (sprites), L (Layer 2) and U, are put in order, and the first one that is not transparent wins. Two settings replace the ordering with blending, and when nothing at all is opaque, the fallback colour fills the dot.

All the settings stage 2 uses, the order, the stencil and blend bits, the transparency and fallback colours, are taken once for each dot as it enters the pipeline and travel with it. So a program, or the Copper, can change them between two dots of the same line, and the change takes effect from the next dot on, never half-way through one.

A layer is transparent at a dot for one of four reasons, which stage 2 does not tell apart:

Two remarks. The ULA’s colour test applies to its border as much as to its picture: a border whose colour equals the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14, is transparent too. And switching the ULA off with bit 7 of the ULA Control register, NextReg $68, removes it as a layer, but not as a source for blending (Part 6): a program can hide the ULA and still blend Layer 2 with it.

Part 3: The ULA and the tilemap become one

The ULA and the tilemap are mixed first, into the single layer U that takes part in the ordering. They are a pair in the hardware too: they read bank 5 through the same memory port, and their palettes share one palette memory. A program often uses the tilemap as a text or tile screen in front of the Spectrum picture, or behind it.

Which of the two is in front is decided tile by tile. Bit 0 of a tile’s attribute byte puts the tile below the ULA; bit 0 of the Tilemap Control register, NextReg $6B, overrides every tile and puts the whole tilemap on top. (In 512-tile mode bit 0 of the attribute is the ninth bit of the tile number, so there every tile is below the ULA unless NextReg $6B bit 0 says otherwise.)

The rule is then the same as for any two layers: the upper one shows where it is opaque, the lower one where the upper is transparent, and U is transparent only where both are.

Stencil mode

Bit 0 of the ULA Control register, NextReg $68, turns the mix into something else: a stencil. With both the ULA and the tilemap switched on, U is opaque only where both are opaque, and its colour is their two colours combined bit by bit with AND. Each layer cuts the other to its own shape: a tilemap of solid colour shows only through the ULA’s ink, or the ULA’s picture only inside the tiles.

The AND also changes the colours. A white dot (all ones) lets the other layer’s colour through unchanged, a black one (all zeros) gives black, and anything between keeps only the bits both share, so a stencil can tint as well as cut.

Part 4: Six orders

Three layers are left, S, L and U, and bits 4–2 of the Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg $15, choose how they are stacked. The numbers fit neatly. Three bits make 8 combinations. Three layers can be stacked in 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 orders: any of the three on top, either of the other two in the middle, and the last one at the bottom. So six of the eight values, 000 to 101, are the six orders, and the two left over, 110 and 111, are not orders at all: they are the two blend modes of Part 6.

In each order, stage 2 looks at the layers top first and takes the first that is not transparent:

Why six, and not one fixed order? Because games want different things. A game with a Layer 2 backdrop and a Spectrum-style status bar wants the ULA in front of Layer 2 (SUL, USL); a game with a tile playfield and Layer 2 effects on top of it wants Layer 2 in front of the tilemap (SLU, LSU); a game whose sprites walk behind scenery wants the sprites below (LSU, LUS, ULS). And because the order is taken for every dot, a program can change it at a chosen line, with the line interrupt or the Copper, and have different orders in different bands of the screen.

Part 5: Two exceptions

The Layer 2 priority bit

A Layer 2 palette entry can carry a priority bit (bit 7 of the second write to the 9-bit Palette Value register, NextReg $44, as the palette article explains). A Layer 2 dot whose entry has it, and which is not transparent, goes above every other layer, whatever the order says. It works in every order where Layer 2 is not on top already: SLU, SUL, USL and ULS, and in the blend modes. In LSU and LUS it changes nothing, because Layer 2 is on top anyway.

The bit belongs to the palette entry, not to the dots, which makes it precise: one colour of a Layer 2 picture can be put in front of the sprites, a window frame or a foreground column, while the rest of the picture stays behind them, without splitting the picture into two layers.

Sprites over the border

In the three orders where U is above the sprites, LUS, USL and ULS, the ULA would hide every sprite behind its border, because the border is an opaque ULA dot. Stage 2 makes one exception: where U’s dot is the border, the tilemap is transparent and a sprite is opaque, the sprite wins. So sprites that are allowed over the border (bit 1 of the Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg $15) stay visible there in every order.

Part 6: Blending

Two more values of bits 4–2 of the Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg $15, 110 and 111, do not order Layer 2 and U at all: they add their colours. Each channel of the two colours, red, green and blue, levels 0 to 7, is added, and the sum, 0 to 14, is brought back into 0 to 7:

  • 110: add and clamp. A sum above 7 becomes 7. Adding brightens: a light source makes Layer 2 lighter, a black one leaves it as it is.
  • 111: add, then subtract 5. A sum up to 4 becomes 0, a sum of 12 or more becomes 7, and anything between loses 5. With a source at level 5, Layer 2 is unchanged; a lighter source lightens it, a darker one darkens it. One layer can shade another both ways.

What Layer 2 is added to, the blend source, is chosen by bits 6–5 of the ULA Control register, NextReg $68: the ULA, the tilemap, or the two already mixed. Whichever of the ULA and the tilemap is not the source is still drawn on its own, above or below the sprites by the tiles’ below flag. So in the blend modes the order is a fixed one of its own:

Two things follow from this order, and both surprise programmers who expect a blend mode to be “SLU with adding”:

  • The blend source shows only through Layer 2. It is at the bottom, added to Layer 2, and it has no place in the order of its own. Where Layer 2 is transparent, the source is not shown at all: the dot comes from the layers above or from the fallback colour. With the ULA as the source, the Spectrum screen and even its border disappear wherever Layer 2 has nothing.
  • The tilemap can rise above the sprites while Layer 2 stays below them. With the ULA as the source, a tile in front of the ULA is drawn above the sprites, and the blended Layer 2 underneath both: a split the six orders cannot make, since there the tilemap and the ULA move together.

Where the source itself is transparent, Layer 2 is shown as it is, in both modes. And a Layer 2 dot with the priority bit shows its blended colour on top of everything.

What blending is good for

Blending is the Next’s way of lighting a picture. Layer 2 holds the detailed picture, 8 bits a dot, and the blend source holds the light: something coarse and cheap to change, laid over it.

  • Light and shadow. Make the ULA the source, with an empty bitmap, and give its attribute cells paper colours. In mode 111 a cell at level 5 in all three channels, the Spectrum’s normal white, leaves the picture under it as it is; BRIGHT white lights it up, and darker colours shade it: a blue cell, levels 0, 0 and 5, darkens red and green by five levels and leaves blue as it is. A torch in a dark cave, a spotlight following the player, the shadow of a cloud: each is a handful of attribute bytes, 768 for the whole screen, instead of a redrawn 48K picture. The tilemap works the same way, with a light level for each 8 × 8 tile.
  • Fades and tints of the whole screen. If every cell of the source shows the same palette entry, changing that one entry recolours everything at once. Raise it step by step in mode 110 and the picture fades to white; lower it in mode 111 and it darkens, until at black every level has dropped by five and only the brightest parts keep a glimmer; give it a little red and the whole picture flushes red, the classic flash when the player is hit. One palette write per frame.
  • Bands of colour. Change the source’s colour between lines, with the line interrupt or, better, the Copper, and the picture gets a sunset tint at the top and deep-water blue at the bottom, while Layer 2 itself is untouched.
  • Glow and haze. Adding light to a picture is what glow, fog and mist look like: a pale source over part of the screen in mode 110 makes it look seen through haze.

The limits come with the format: each channel has only eight levels, so steps are coarse, and the clamps at 0 and 7 lose detail in the darkest and brightest parts. But for the cost of a few register or attribute writes, the CPU never touching the 48K or 80K of Layer 2, nothing else on the Next comes close.

Part 7: Nothing opaque

When every layer is transparent at a dot, in any mode, stage 2 shows the fallback colour, held in the Fallback Colour register, NextReg $4A: an 8-bit colour made into nine bits like a write to the 8-bit Palette Value register, NextReg $41. The machine starts with $E3 there; programs usually set it to black, or, as the pictures of this series do, to a sky blue that then needs no layer at all.

Part 8: How Klive does it

Stage 2 in C

Klive’s Next core has stage 2 as one function, zxnextComposePixel, written from the hardware’s rules branch for branch: the ULA and tilemap mix with its below flag and stencil mode, the six orders with the priority bit and the border exception, both blend modes with their sources and order, and the fallback. It takes the four values of a dot from the layer buffers of the first article’s Part 9, and the settings of NextRegs $15, $68, $6B, $14 and $4A, and gives one 9-bit colour.

The settings are recorded each time they change during a frame, so a frame whose order or blend mode the Copper changes between bands is composed with the right settings in each band.

Why this colour?

The same function can also say which rule chose a dot’s colour: one of ten, the fallback, the ULA or the tilemap through U, Layer 2 by the order or by its priority bit, the sprites by the order or by the border exception, the stencil, or one of the two blends. That answer is what the IDE’s Layers view and its pixel probe show when you point at a dot: each layer’s value there, the settings in force on that line, and the rule that won.

Summary

  • Stage 2 takes four colours with their transparency answers and flags, and makes one colour.
  • A layer is transparent at a dot when it is switched off, clipped, or fails its own test: the colour against the value in NextReg $14 for the ULA and Layer 2, the dot against the values in NextRegs $4B and $4C for the sprites and the tilemap.
  • The ULA and the tilemap are first mixed into one layer, U, the tile on top unless its attribute or 512-tile mode puts it below; stencil mode (NextReg $68 bit 0) makes U the AND of both, where both are opaque.
  • NextReg $15 bits 4–2 put S, L and U in one of six orders; the first opaque layer wins. The setting is taken for every dot, so it can change in the middle of a frame.
  • A Layer 2 palette entry with the priority bit lifts its dots above everything. In the orders with U above S, sprites still show over the border.
  • Blending (NextReg $15 = 110 or 111) adds Layer 2 to a source chosen by NextReg $68 bits 6–5, clamped or minus 5. The source shows only through Layer 2, and the tilemap can rise above the sprites.
  • When nothing is opaque, the fallback colour (NextReg $4A) shows.
  • Klive composes every dot with a C copy of these rules and can tell, for any dot, which rule chose its colour.
