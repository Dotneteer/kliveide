ZX Spectrum Next: The Big Picture

Load a modern game on a ZX Spectrum Next and look at one frame. A playfield built from tiles scrolls smoothly from right to left. Behind it a background in a few hundred colours scrolls more slowly, for depth. Dozens of characters move over both. At the bottom sits a score panel in the unmistakable Spectrum font and colours. And the same machine, switched to another mode, runs a 1982 48K game exactly as the 48K did, down to the T-state.

On the original Spectrum, one chip produced every dot. On the Next, five producers of dots run side by side, each reading its own memory. They feed four layers: two of the producers, the ULA and LoRes, share one layer and take turns at it. So for every position on the screen there are four candidate dots, and something has to pick one of them, about fourteen million times a second. This article is the map of that machinery: what the producers are, where each can draw, what each tells the rest of the machine about every dot, and how one colour comes out at the end.

It is the first article of a series. The ones that follow take each piece apart; this one shows how the pieces fit. You should know the Spectrum’s own screen first: ZX Spectrum: How the Screen Is Drawn explains the ULA, the bitmap and its attributes, and contention, and this series re-explains only what the Next changes. How a Television Draws a Picture explains the beam and the lines, and The ZX Spectrum Next Core and the FPGA explains why the Next’s hardware lives in an FPGA, and how Klive’s emulator is checked against it.

How this article is organised

  • From one chip to a pipeline (Part 1): what the Next kept from the Spectrum and what it added beside it.
  • The cast (Part 2): the five producers, the four layers they make, the border and the fallback colour, one at a time.
  • Where each layer can draw (Part 3): the 256 × 192 display area, the 320 × 256 wide area and the frame around them, the clip window that limits each layer, and scrolling.
  • Two answers for every dot (Part 4): the one idea that carries the whole series. If you read only one part, read this one.
  • The pipeline (Part 5): two dots on the screen for every position of the beam, the stages that turn the answers into colours, and one dot followed through them.
  • Clocks and colours (Part 6): 28, 14 and 7 MHz, and the 512 colours everything ends as.
  • What stays the same (Part 7): the Spectrum inside the Next.
  • The series (Part 8) and the emulator (Part 9): the articles that follow, and how Klive composes the picture.

The numbers in this series are for the Next’s 128K/+3 display timing at 50 Hz, with the master clock at exactly 28 MHz (VGA mode 0, or HDMI). The other timings change the size of the frame, not the way it is drawn; the frame article of the series lists them. Every fact here was checked against the Next’s hardware design, and Klive emulates the machine the same way.

Part 1: From one chip to a pipeline

The ZX Spectrum’s picture has a single source. The ULA reads the screen memory, turns bytes into dots and sends them to the television; nothing else in the machine takes part. The Next keeps that ULA, and keeps it compatible to the bit: the same bitmap, the same attributes, the same order of reads, the same contention. Every 48K or 128K program that writes to $4000 sees exactly the screen it expects.

What the Next adds is not a better ULA but more producers beside it. Each is a separate piece of hardware with its own memory and its own idea of what a picture is: a bitmap with a byte per dot, a grid of tiles, a set of sprites. They all run at the same time, in step with the same beam. For each position the beam reaches, they offer four candidate dots, one for each layer: the ULA and LoRes share a layer, and only one of them fills it (Part 2). A compositor after them picks the winner.

Notice the memories on the left. The producers do not queue for one memory, as the CPU and the ULA do on the Spectrum. The FPGA keeps bank 5 (and the first 8K of bank 7, the 128K’s second screen) in its own block RAM, which has two ports: one is shared by the CPU and LoRes, the other by the ULA and the tilemap. Layer 2 reads the external SRAM, the Next’s main memory, where its bitmap can be large. The sprites keep their shapes in a 16K memory of their own, which the CPU can reach only through I/O ports. It is block RAM inside the FPGA, and a very fast one: the sprite hardware can read a new byte from it at every tick of the 28 MHz master clock, four times for every dot the beam draws. That speed is what lets it draw dozens of sprites into a line in the time the beam takes to draw one (the sprite article shows how). Giving each producer its own way to its data is what lets them all run at once.

Part 2: The cast

Here are the five producers and the two colours that are not producers, each with what makes it different. Five producers make four layers, because the ULA and LoRes share one. The later articles of the series explain each one; this part is the introduction.

The layers are a stack. At every dot, the top layer is shown if it has something there. Where it is transparent, the dot comes from the layer below it; where that one is transparent too, from the next one down, and so on to the bottom of the stack. Only where every layer is transparent does the fallback colour show. Part 4 explains what makes a layer transparent at a dot.

The order of the stack is not fixed. The Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg $15, picks one of six orders for the sprites, Layer 2 and the ULA (with the tilemap), so a program can put Layer 2 in front of the sprites, or the Spectrum screen in front of everything. The tilemap can sit in front of the ULA or behind it, for the whole map or tile by tile. The figures in this article use the default order, the one the machine starts with: the sprites on top, then Layer 2, then the tilemap, then the ULA. Part 5 shows where in the pipeline the order is applied, and article 9 of the series shows all six.

  • The ULA. The Spectrum’s own screen, $4000 in bank 5 or the shadow screen in bank 7, with all of its rules. The Next lets it do more when a program asks: the Timex modes (a colour for every 8 × 1 dots, or 512 dots across), ULANext and ULA+ (more colours from the same attribute byte), hardware scrolling and a clip window. Old software never asks, so it never notices.
  • LoRes. A 128 × 96 screen with a byte, any of 256 colours, for every 2 × 2 dots. It is not a layer of its own: when it is switched on, it replaces the ULA’s picture and takes the ULA’s place in everything that follows, its priority, its clip window, its transparency. The two are never shown together: the ULA layer shows the ULA’s screen or LoRes, and only the border around it stays the ULA’s either way.
  • Layer 2. A plain bitmap with one byte for every dot, in 256 × 192, 320 × 256 or 640 × 256 (half a byte a dot). No attributes, no clash, any dot any colour, at the price of memory: 48K or 80K, which is why it lives in 16K banks of the main RAM.
  • The tilemap. A grid of 8 × 8 tiles, 40 or 80 across and 32 down, each tile a reference into a set of tile shapes and a byte saying how to draw it: mirrored, rotated, with which colours, in front of the ULA or behind it. A whole screen costs a few kilobytes.
  • The sprites. 128 sprites of 16 × 16 dots, each with its own position, shape, colours, mirroring, rotation and size, which can be linked into bigger objects. The hardware never holds a picture of them: it draws each line of them just before the beam needs it.
  • The border. On the Next, the border is not a separate thing: it is what the ULA layer shows outside its 256 × 192 area, in the colour of port $FE. It takes part in the composition like any other ULA dot, which is why sprites and Layer 2 can be drawn over it.
  • The fallback colour. When every layer says “nothing here” for a dot, the picture still needs a colour. The Fallback Colour register, NextReg $4A, holds it.

Part 3: Where each layer can draw

The Spectrum has one rectangle that matters, the 256 × 192 display area, and a border around it. The Next adds a second rectangle: the wide area, 320 × 256 dots, which is the 256 × 192 area with 32 dots added on every side. The wide area covers most of the border a television shows, and it is the canvas of the layers the Next added.

Which layer can draw where follows from what each was built to show:

The clip window: a rectangle for each layer

A layer does not have to cover the whole of its canvas. Each layer has a clip window, a rectangle on the screen. Inside it, the layer draws as usual. Outside it, the layer answers “transparent” for every dot, so the dot comes from the layers below it, exactly as if the layer had nothing to show there (Part 4 explains the answer).

Notice what a clip window is not. It does not change the layer’s memory, and it does not move or shrink the layer’s picture: it is a mask laid over the screen, which hides part of the picture and shows what is underneath. That makes it the cheapest tool on the machine for a whole family of effects: a score panel that one layer must not draw over, a playfield framed by a border, a layer switched off for a band of the screen, a letterbox. One write of four bytes does it, where clearing the same area in memory would take thousands of bytes, and putting it back would take thousands more.

There are four windows, one for each layer (LoRes, which takes the ULA’s place, uses the ULA’s):

Each window is four bytes, X1, X2, Y1, Y2, and both edges are inside it: a window of X 0–255 is 256 dots wide. A program writes all four to the same NextReg, one after the other; a small index inside the machine says which coordinate the next write sets, steps on after every write, and wraps from Y2 back to X1. The Clip Window Control register, NextReg $1C, resets the index of any of the four windows, so a program can start from X1 without counting.

The right half of the figure explains the odd numbers. A coordinate is one byte, 0 to 255, but the wide area is 320 dots across. So the windows that work in the wide area count X in units of two dots: X1 = 10 means dot 20, and X2 = 159 means dots up to 319, the right edge. The Y coordinates stay in lines, and 0–255 covers the wide area’s 256 lines exactly. One thing follows from that, and it surprises programmers: Layer 2’s window starts as 0–255, 0–191 in every mode, which in the 320 × 256 mode covers the full width but only the top 192 of the 256 lines. A program that uses the wide modes sets the window to 0–159, 0–255 first.

Scrolling: moving the picture without moving memory

Moving a picture on the Spectrum meant moving bytes. To scroll the screen one character to the left, a program had to copy almost all of the 6,912 bytes of the screen.

Scrolling by one dot is harder still. Eight dots share a byte, so no byte can simply be copied: every bitmap byte has to be shifted by one bit, and the bit that falls out of it carried into its neighbour, along every line, in the screen’s scrambled address order. Even with the tightest code, an unrolled RL (HL) and DEC L for each byte, that is 19 T-states for each of the 6,144 bytes: about 117,000 T-states, more than one and a half frames of a 48K, before the contention of the screen memory slows it down further. And that moves only the dots. The colours belong to 8 × 8 cells and can move only a whole character at a time, so the picture tears into ink that has moved and colours that have not. That is why Spectrum games that scroll smoothly scroll a small window, not the whole screen.

Bigger pictures make it worse: to move a 48K Layer 2 picture by copying, a 3.5 MHz CPU would need about fourteen frames. All of this is far too slow for anything that has to move every frame.

So the Next lets the hardware do it. All the producers except the sprites have scroll offsets: registers that say where in its picture the layer starts reading. With an offset of zero, the dot at the top-left of the layer’s area is the first dot of its picture. With an X offset of 96, it is the 97th dot of the first line, and everything shifts 96 dots to the left. One register write moves the whole picture.

And the picture wraps round. What is pushed off the left edge does not disappear; it comes back in on the right, and what leaves the top comes back at the bottom. The picture behaves like a loop, or like the surface of a doughnut:

Wrapping is what makes endless scrolling work. A game that scrolls its playfield to the left draws each new column of graphics into the column of memory that has just scrolled off the left edge, just before it comes back in on the right. The picture never needs to be bigger than the screen.

The clip window belongs to the screen, the scroll offset to the layer’s picture, so a layer can scroll inside a window that stays put, and a status panel on another layer can stay exactly where it is. The ULA article and the articles on the other layers give the details of each layer’s offsets; changing them in the middle of a frame, for a screen that is split into bands that scroll separately, is the subject of article 10.

Part 4: Two answers for every dot

Here is the idea the whole series is built on. For every position of the beam, every layer gives the compositor exactly two answers:

  1. Which colour? As an 8-bit index into a palette, not as a colour. The palette turns it into a colour later.
  2. Am I transparent here? Whether this layer has anything to show at this dot at all.

That is all the compositor ever learns about a layer. It does not know about tiles, sprites, attributes or bitmaps. Every rule the later articles describe, from the Timex modes to sprite anchors, is a way of arriving at these two answers. A few layers add a flag to them: the border says “I am border”, the tilemap says “I am behind the ULA”, and Layer 2’s palette can say “draw me in front of everything”.

Look at the red lines in the figure: there are two kinds of transparency, and which a layer uses says something about it.

  • The ULA, LoRes and Layer 2 are transparent where their dot’s colour, after the palette lookup, equals the global transparency colour, held in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14. These layers draw whole screens of colours, and “this colour means see-through” is the natural rule for a bitmap.
  • The sprites and the tilemap are transparent where their index, before the lookup, equals the value in the Sprite Transparency Index register, NextReg $4B (sprites), or in the Tilemap Transparency Index register, NextReg $4C (tilemap). These layers draw shapes out of small 4- or 8-bit patterns, and “this value in the pattern is the hole” is the natural rule for a shape. (The tilemap’s text mode, whose tiles have one bit per dot, uses the colour rule instead.)

Both registers start at the same value, $E3, and so does the fallback colour. That is why $E3, a bright magenta, is the colour of “nothing” on the Next: in the default Layer 2 palette, index $E3 is colour $E3, so a Layer 2 bitmap filled with $E3 is invisible from the start.

A layer that is switched off, or clipped by its clip window at this dot (Part 3), answers “transparent” too. To the compositor there is no difference between “off”, “outside my window” and “a hole in my picture”.

Part 5: The pipeline

The compositor is not one step but a short pipeline, the video pipeline. A dot enters it when the beam’s counters reach its position and leaves it, as a colour, a few clock ticks later. While one dot is in the last stage, the next ones are already in the stages before it, so a new dot comes out at every tick.

Two dots for every position of the beam

Start with the most important fact about the pipeline’s output. So far, a dot has been one step of the beam’s counters, one 7 MHz dot. The pipeline does not produce one colour for it but two: a left half and a right half, each one 14 MHz dot wide, side by side on the screen. Its last stages run fast enough for that: the decision (stage 2 below) is made at 14 MHz, and the palette lookups before it (stage 1) at 28 MHz, so both halves get their own.

For most layers the two halves are the same. A layer that draws 256 dots across, the ULA’s standard screen, LoRes, Layer 2 in 256 × 192 and 320 × 256, and every sprite, offers the same answers for both halves, and the screen shows its dot twice as wide as a 14 MHz dot. The layers that draw 512 or 640 dots across, the ULA’s Timex hi-res mode, the tilemap’s 80 columns and Layer 2’s 640 × 256, offer a different answer for each half, and then the two halves really differ.

The compositor decides each half on its own, with that half’s answers. So the layers can mix inside one position of the beam: a hi-res tile dot that is opaque in the left half and transparent in the right shows the tile in one half and whatever lies below it in the other.

The stages

Here is the road from the beam’s counters to those two colours, in four stages:

Stage 0: gather

The beam’s counters reach a position, and each producer offers its two answers for it. In the same tick, the pipeline latches the registers that steer everything after it: the layer order, the transparency and fallback colours, which palettes are shown, which layers are on. They are held for this dot while it travels through the stages. That is how a write to one of these registers in the middle of a line takes effect: from the next dot the beam reaches, and never half-way through one. A few settings are taken less often, the ULA’s scroll per 8-dot cell, the sprites a line ahead; article 10 of the series lists them, and is about using all of this.

Stage 1: look up

The indices go through the palettes. There are eight: a first and a second palette for each of the ULA, the tilemap, Layer 2 and the sprites, 256 entries each. But there are only two memories, each holding four of them: one for the ULA and the tilemap, one for Layer 2 and the sprites. Two numbers describe each memory: 1,024 entries of 16 bits.

Why 1,024 entries? Look at what picks an entry. Its address has ten bits. One says which of the memory’s two layers is looking up: the ULA or the tilemap in one memory, Layer 2 or the sprites in the other. One says which of that layer’s two palettes is shown, the first or the second (the Palette Control register, NextReg $43, chooses for three of the layers, and the Tilemap Control register, NextReg $6B, for the tilemap). The other eight are the layer’s own index. Two layers, two palettes, 256 indices: 2 × 2 × 256 = 1,024, four palettes one after the other in one memory. (Entries, not bytes: an entry is two bytes, so each memory holds 2K.)

Why 16 bits? An entry needs only ten: the 9-bit colour, and a priority bit written with it. Only Layer 2’s entries use that bit: it can lift a Layer 2 dot above everything, and it travels with the colour from here on. Sixteen is simply what the memory is made of. One block of the FPGA’s RAM holds 16 Kbit, and laid out as 1,024 words each word is 16 bits wide: 1,024 × 16 is one whole block, used as it comes, with six bits of every entry spare. Packed four to a block, the eight palettes take two blocks of RAM; eight memories of their own would take far more of a chip that has few to spare.

Sharing has a price in time, and the pipeline pays it with speed. Each memory is read at every tick of the 28 MHz clock, its two layers taking turns: the layer bit of the address simply says which tick it is. That is two lookups for each 14 MHz dot, one for each layer, so all four layers get their colours for every dot, even in the modes that draw 512 or 640 dots across.

This is why stage 1 is drawn as two boxes in the pipeline figure, not four. The two boxes are not two steps one after the other: they are the two memories, and they work at the same time. The taking of turns happens inside each box:

28 MHz tick:       0         1         2         3      (one 7 MHz period)
memory 1:         ULA     tilemap     ULA     tilemap
memory 2:       Layer 2   sprites   Layer 2   sprites

Four boxes would show four memories, each with a lookup of its own. That is a natural design, but not the Next’s: its four lookups are made by two memories, two at a time.

Stage 2: decide

Now the compositor has four colours with their flags, and it applies the rules:

  • Is each layer transparent here? The colour rule (against the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14) is checked now; the index rules were already applied by the sprites and the tilemap themselves.
  • The ULA and the tilemap become one layer, U. Before the main order is applied, these two are mixed: the tilemap goes in front of the ULA, or behind it where a tile asks for it. In stencil mode a dot is shown only where both are opaque.
  • The order. Three layers are left, S (sprites), L (Layer 2) and U, and the Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg $15, picks one of six orders, from SLU (sprites in front, the default) to ULS. The first opaque one, in that order, wins.
  • The exceptions. A Layer 2 dot with the priority bit wins whatever the order. Two further settings of NextReg $15 do not order Layer 2 and U at all but add their colours, channel by channel.
  • Nothing opaque? Then the fallback colour, from the Fallback Colour register, NextReg $4A.

Putting it together, article 9 of the series, follows each of these rules in full, with the machine’s own pictures.

One dot, through the pipeline

Take the dot marked in the exploded figure of Part 2, in the default settings, and follow it. Here is the figure again, with what each layer answers at that dot:

Step by step:

  1. Stage 0. The sprites have nothing at this position: transparent. Layer 2’s bitmap holds $E3 here, below its band of hills. The tilemap offers a dot of a platform tile, index $13. The ULA offers its paper colour.
  2. Stage 1. Layer 2’s $E3 becomes the colour $E3 (and a ninth bit). The tile’s $13 becomes whatever the tilemap palette holds at $13, a green.
  3. Stage 2. Layer 2’s colour equals NextReg $14: transparent. The tile is not behind the ULA, so U is the tile’s green. The order is SLU: the sprites are transparent, Layer 2 is transparent, U is not. The dot is green.

Everything in the later articles happens inside one of these three steps.

Part 6: Clocks and colours

Three numbers that make it fit

  • 28 MHz is the master clock. Everything in the video pipeline counts in its ticks.
  • 4 ticks per dot. The beam’s counters step at 7 MHz, a quarter of the master clock: one step is one dot of a 256-wide line, the same dot the Spectrum’s ULA sends at 7 MHz. Inside each step a sub-pixel counter counts the four master ticks, and the memories hand out those ticks in turn: the ULA and the tilemap take turns at bank 5, and each palette memory serves its two layers.
  • 2 dots per step for the fine layers. Layers that draw 512 or 640 dots across, the ULA’s Timex hi-res mode, the tilemap’s 80 columns and Layer 2’s 640 mode, run at 14 MHz, two dots per 7 MHz step. Stage 2 decides at 14 MHz too, so the final picture has 14 MHz resolution across, and a 256-wide layer simply offers each of its dots twice.

The VGA modes 1 to 6 (the Video Timing register, NextReg $11) raise the master clock in steps up to 33 MHz, for monitors that do not accept the standard rate. Everything above scales with it: the frame keeps its shape and only gets faster.

The CPU deserves a sentence. At the top level of the design, the CPU’s clock is selected from the video clocks: 28, 14 or 7 MHz as they are, or 3.5 MHz made from the 7 MHz clock by letting only every other tick through. Contention, at 3.5 MHz, is that clock holding back a tick. So the CPU speed changes how much a program can do in a frame, never the picture: the beam’s counters run at 7 MHz whatever the CPU does.

512 colours

Every path through the pipeline ends in the same form: a 9-bit colour, 3 bits each of red, green and blue, RGB333, 512 colours. Why 9? Three bits a channel is what the Next’s video connector puts out: a 3-bit digital-to-analogue converter per channel for VGA and RGB. HDMI carries 8 bits a channel, and the 3 bits are stretched to fill them.

Fifteen, not sixteen: the Spectrum has 16 colour codes, eight colours with and without BRIGHT, but BRIGHT black is the same black. And one of the fifteen has moved. Pure bright magenta, red 7, green 0, blue 7, is $E3 in the 8-bit form, the global transparency colour of Part 4: on the ULA layer every bright magenta dot would be a hole. So the Next’s default palette gives bright magenta one step of green, a shade the eye hardly tells apart and the compositor never mistakes for “nothing”.

The palettes are what connect the 8-bit indices of Part 4 to these 9-bit colours, and the 8-bit indices are what keep the layers cheap: a Layer 2 dot is one byte, not two. An 8-bit palette entry can also be written in the short form RRRGGGBB, which leaves out the lowest bit of blue; the palette article of the series explains how that bit is made, and where the eight palettes are selected and written.

Part 7: What stays the same

All this is added beside the Spectrum, not on top of it. Switch the Next to 48K or 128K timing and leave the new layers off, as an old program does, and what is left is the Spectrum:

  • The frame keeps the Spectrum’s shape: 448 dots by 312 lines for the 48K, 456 by 311 for the 128K and +3 (the frame of Part 3), and 448 by 320 for a Pentagon. A dot is still half a T-state of a 3.5 MHz CPU, so a line still lasts 224 or 228 T-states.
  • The ULA reads the screen the same way: a bitmap byte and an attribute for every 8 dots, in the same order and at the same moments relative to the interrupt.
  • Contention stops the CPU at the same T-states, in the same pattern. It exists only while the CPU runs at 3.5 MHz: at 7, 14 or 28 MHz the CPU is never held back (and bit 6 of the Peripheral 3 Setting register, NextReg $08, switches it off at 3.5 MHz too). That is the price of speed being paid by compatibility: a program that counts T-states against the beam works only at the speed it was written for.
  • The border is still port $FE, changed at the same moments.

The Spectrum article’s explanation of all this, from the frame to the floating bus, holds for the Next’s ULA. What the Next changes about the ULA is the subject of the ULA article of this series.

Part 9: How Klive does it

Klive’s Next core is a C program compiled to WebAssembly, written to behave exactly like the hardware (see The ZX Spectrum Next Core and the FPGA). It draws the picture in two steps that mirror the pipeline, but grouped differently: not dot by dot through every stage, but layer by layer through stages 0 and 1, and then dot by dot through stage 2.

One buffer per layer

The picture Klive shows is the 360 × 288 window of Part 3, stored as 720 × 288 pixels: one pixel for every 14 MHz dot, so a 7 MHz dot is two pixels. Each of the four layers (LoRes draws into the ULA’s) has a buffer of its own of the same size, with 16 bits per pixel:

  bit:  15        14–12     11       10        9          8–0
       OPAQUE     –       BORDER  TM_BELOW  L2_PRIORITY   9-bit RGB

A layer’s renderer does stages 0 and 1 for the whole of its buffer: it works out its dot, looks up the palette, applies its transparency rule (the colour against the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg $14, for the ULA and Layer 2, the index for the tilemap and the sprites), and writes either 0, for transparent, or OPAQUE, the colour and its flags. These are Part 4’s two answers and flags, with the palette lookup already done. The border is drawn into the ULA’s buffer first, as ULA pixels marked BORDER, and the ULA’s picture over it.

One function for stage 2

Then zxnextUlaCompose goes through the picture dot by dot and calls zxnextComposePixel with the four values of that dot. That function is the hardware’s stage 2 written in C, rule for rule: the ULA and tilemap mix, stencil mode, the six orders with the border exception, the priority bit, both blend modes and the fallback. It also returns, if asked, which rule chose the colour; that is what the IDE’s Layers view and its pixel probe show when you point at a dot (see Layers).

Most programs never switch on a layer beyond the ULA, so the compose has a fast path: when the tilemap, Layer 2 and the sprites are all off and no blend mode is set, every dot is simply the ULA’s colour or the fallback, and the general function is skipped.

Following the beam

The renderers draw from the registers and memory as they are now. If they ran once per frame, a palette change at line 100 would recolour the whole frame. So, as Klive’s Spectrum cores do, the Next core draws lazily: just before a write that could change the picture, a Copper move, a palette write, a border OUT, it renders the lines the beam has already passed with the old state. Article 10 of the series explains this raster and the one line of difference it allows.

Summary

  • The Next keeps the Spectrum’s ULA, bit-compatible, and adds four producers beside it: LoRes, Layer 2, the tilemap and the sprites. Each reads its own memory, and all of them run at once.
  • Five producers make four layers: the ULA and LoRes share one, and only one of them fills it (LoRes, when it is on, replaces the ULA’s picture; the border stays the ULA’s).
  • The ULA draws the 256 × 192 display area and the border around it; Layer 2’s wide modes, the tilemap and the sprites can draw over the wide area, 320 × 256. When no layer has a dot, the fallback colour (NextReg $4A) shows.
  • For every dot, every layer gives two answers: a colour index and “am I transparent?”, sometimes with a flag. The compositor sees nothing else.
  • Transparency comes in two kinds: by colour after the palette (NextReg $14: ULA, LoRes, Layer 2) and by index before it (NextReg $4B for the sprites, NextReg $4C for the tilemap). All three, and the fallback, start at $E3.
  • The pipeline gathers the answers and latches the registers (stage 0), looks the indices up in eight palettes held in two time-shared memories (stage 1), and decides the winner (stage 2): the ULA and tilemap mixed into one layer, then one of six orders, the priority bit, blending, the fallback.
  • It runs on a 28 MHz master clock: the beam steps at 7 MHz, the fine layers and the decision at 14 MHz. Every colour ends as RGB333, 512 colours. The CPU’s clock is picked from the same clocks and never changes the picture.
  • Klive renders each layer into a 16-bit buffer (stages 0 and 1) and composes them dot by dot with a C copy of stage 2, lazily, in step with the beam.
