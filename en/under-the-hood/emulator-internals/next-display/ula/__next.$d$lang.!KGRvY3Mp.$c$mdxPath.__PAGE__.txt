# The ULA Layer: The Spectrum's Screen, Extended

Load a 48K program on your Next, and it sees exactly the screen it expects: the same bytes at `$4000`, the
same colours, the same border stripes, the same FLASH. Yet the very same ULA can put 512 dots on a line,
give every 8 × 1 dots their own colours, show 64 colours at once, and scroll the whole screen smoothly by a
single dot. So where does all that hide, and why does old software never trip over it?

It matters because the ULA is the screen almost every Spectrum program draws on, and the one most Next
programs still use for text, menus and status lines. A new mode that broke one old game would be a bad
trade, so every extension sits behind a switch that old software never touches. Learn the switches, and you
get the familiar screen with its worst limits taken away: colour clash, eight colours per cell, and
scrolling by moving thousands of bytes.

Everything here builds on [ZX Spectrum: How the Screen Is Drawn](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/),
which explains the ULA itself: the bitmap and its attributes, the famous address order, how a line is read
and drawn, contention and the floating bus. I will not repeat any of that; we only look at what the Next
changes. You also need the series so far: the
[overview chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) for the layers and the
pipeline, and the previous chapter, [colours and palettes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/),
for how an index becomes a colour.

## The road ahead

We start with the memory behind the screen, which on the Next lives inside the FPGA, and why the Next has to
imitate contention rather than suffer it. Then we watch the ULA read every line twice over, eight reads where
the Spectrum made four, and find out what the extra four are for. The heart of the chapter comes next: the
Timex modes, a second screen, hi-colour and hi-res, each shown on the machine. After that, two new ways of
reading the attribute byte, ULANext and ULA+, and what each does to the border. Then the hardware scroll,
dot by dot with the colours moving along, down to half a dot. We end with the clip window, switching the ULA
off, and how Klive does all of this.

## The same ULA, new memory

Leave every new switch alone, and the Next's ULA is the Spectrum's: the 256 × 192 bitmap at `$4000`, the
768 attributes at `$5800`, INK, PAPER, BRIGHT and FLASH (16 frames on, 16 off), and the border from port
`$FE`. It reads the same bytes at the same moments of the frame. The Spectrum display chapter's
[The screen in memory](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/#part-2-the-screen-in-memory)
and How the ULA draws a line hold for it word for word.

What differs is the memory behind it. On a Spectrum the screen is ordinary RAM that the ULA and the CPU
take turns to use.

Why turns? Because the RAM can do only one thing at a time. The screen lives in the lower 16K, eight
dynamic RAM chips with one set of address pins and one data line each: at any moment they can be given one
address and deliver one byte, to the ULA or to the CPU, never to both. Memory that can serve two readers at
once did exist, but it cost far more for each bit than the ordinary dynamic RAM of home computers, too much
for a machine built to a price. So the ULA and the CPU share the RAM, and someone has to give way. It cannot
be the ULA: the beam does not wait, and a byte fetched late is a dot drawn wrong. So the ULA always goes
first, and the CPU is held up until it has finished. That is **contention**, and the Spectrum display
chapter's [Sharing the memory](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/#part-5-sharing-the-memory)
explains the exact pattern.

The Next has no such limit. On the Next, **bank 5**, the 16K that normally appears at `$4000`–`$7FFF`, is
kept in **block RAM inside the FPGA**, and so is the first half of **bank 7**, the 128K's shadow screen.
Block RAM is built with **two ports** at no extra cost: two independent sets of address and data lines into
the same bytes. The CPU uses one, the ULA (and the tilemap) the other, and neither ever waits for the other.
The ULA reads bank 5 directly, whichever banks the CPU has paged in.

Two consequences follow, and you will meet both of them again.

**Contention is an imitation.** The Spectrum held up the CPU because both could not use the RAM at once. The
Next has no such problem, yet old programs count T-states with the delays built in. So the ULA works out,
from the beam's position, exactly when a Spectrum's CPU would have been held up, and holds the Next's CPU up
then, for no other reason than to keep time. Notice the irony: the Next goes to some trouble to slow itself
down on purpose. It does so only while the CPU runs at 3.5 MHz, as the timing chapter's
[Dots, ticks and T-states](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#dots-ticks-and-t-states)
explains. And there is **no snow**: the Spectrum's flickering dots came from the CPU's refresh cycles
disturbing the ULA's reads of shared RAM, and here the ULA has a port of its own.

**Bank 7 is only half there.** The FPGA has enough block RAM for 16K of bank 5 but only 8K of bank 7. That is
plenty for the 128K shadow screen, 6,912 bytes, but not for a second display file at `+$2000`. So while the
shadow screen is shown, the Timex modes are switched off, and the ULA shows a plain Spectrum screen from
bank 7.

## Reading a line twice

The Spectrum's ULA reads four bytes for every 16 dots: the bitmap byte and the attribute of one character
cell, then of the next. The Next's ULA reads **eight**. For every 8 dots it fetches the bitmap byte and
attribute of the cell under the beam **and of the cell after it**.

Why the extra work? Because of the hardware scroll, which you meet in Scrolling by the dot below. Without a
scroll, the 8 dots the beam is about to draw are exactly one cell. With a scroll of, say, 3 dots, they are the
last 5 dots of one cell and the first 3 of the next:

The extra reads are invisible to your programs. The **floating bus**, the byte a read of port `$FF` returns
on a Spectrum, shows only the four reads a Spectrum would make, at the moments it would make them, and `$FF`
over the border, exactly as the Spectrum display chapter's
[The floating bus](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/#the-floating-bus) describes. They
are the bytes the ULA really reads, so with a scroll they come from the scrolled cells, and in the Timex modes
from the display file the mode reads.

The Next gives the floating bus with 48K and 128K timing; with +3 or Pentagon timing a port `$FF` read
returns `$FF`. With +3 timing the floating bus appears instead on the +3's own port, with bit 0 set, as on a
real +3. One more thing can take port `$FF`'s place: with bit 2 of the Peripheral 3 Setting register, NextReg
`$08`, a read of port `$FF` returns the Timex register of the next section instead of the floating bus.

## The Timex modes

The Timex Computer Corporation sold Spectrum-compatible machines in America whose ULA had more modes,
switched by **port `$FF`**. A small amount of software uses them, and the Next has them all. The port's bits,
as the Next has them:

| Bits of port `$FF` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 7 | not used |
| 6 | 1 switches off the frame interrupt |
| 5–3 | ink colour of the hi-res mode (the paper is its opposite) |
| 2–0 | the screen mode: `000` standard, `001` second screen, `010` hi-colour, `110` hi-res |

Bits 5–0 can also be written through the Display Control 1 register, **NextReg `$69`**, which carries two
more switches beside them: bit 7 switches Layer 2 on, and bit 6 shows the shadow screen. The three mode bits
act on their own: bit 0 moves the bitmap to the second display file, bit 1 takes the colours from there, bit 2
doubles the dots across. The four combinations in the table are the ones the Timex defined, and the only ones
software uses.

The mode is taken again for every 8 dots, at the same moment as the scroll. Your program, or the Copper, can
change it between two cells of the same line, and the change shows from the next cell.

## A second screen

Mode `001` is the plainest: the same Spectrum screen, read from **`$6000`** instead of `$4000`, with its
attributes at `$7800`. A program draws the next frame in one screen while the other is shown, and switches
with one `OUT`: **double buffering**, the 128K's shadow screen idea, but in bank 5. No more half-drawn frames
caught by the beam halfway through.

## Hi-colour: a colour for every 8 × 1 dots

On the Spectrum the attribute byte of a cell covers 8 × 8 dots. Mode `010` gives every **byte of the
bitmap** a colour byte of its own. The bitmap stays at `$4000`, and the colour bytes are a second copy of its
layout at `$6000`: the colour of the bitmap byte at `$4000 + o` is the byte at **`$6000 + o`**.

So the ULA's address for the colour is the bitmap address with one more bit set. The Spectrum's
attribute address is built differently, from the row of cells, which is what made the 8 × 8 cells in the
first place:

The price is memory: 12K for one screen instead of 6.75K, and your program must write 6,144 colour bytes
where it wrote 768. The gain is that colour clash, the Spectrum's best-known limit, is gone from the vertical
direction. A sprite can move up and down through a cell and keep its own colours; only across does it still
share 8 dots with its neighbours.

## Hi-res: 512 dots across

Mode `110` is the other way round: one colour, twice the dots. The line has **512 dots** instead of 256,
each half as wide, one 14 MHz dot instead of one 7 MHz dot, so the line is just as long on the screen.

First, what a **column** means here, because the word is easy to misread. A column is a strip of the screen
**8 dots wide**: on every line it is **one byte** of the bitmap, and with an 8 × 8 font it holds **one
character** of text.

- The **standard** Spectrum screen has **32 columns**: 256 dots ÷ 8. A line is 32 bytes, and the display file
  at `$4000` holds them all: column 0 is the line's byte 0, column 31 its byte 31.
- **Hi-res** has **64 columns**: 512 dots ÷ 8. Each column is half as wide on the screen as a standard one,
  because its dots are. A line is 64 bytes, twice what one display file holds for a line, so **both** files
  hold half each: column 0 is byte 0 of the line in the file at `$4000`, column 1 is byte 0 in the file at
  `$6000`, column 2 is byte 1 at `$4000`, column 3 byte 1 at `$6000`, and so on. The even columns come from
  the first file, the odd ones from the second.

| | Standard | Hi-res |
|---|---|---|
| Dots on a line | 256 | 512 |
| Width of a dot | one 7 MHz dot | half that: one 14 MHz dot |
| Columns: 8 dots, one byte a line | **32** | **64** |
| Characters of 8 × 8 text on a line | 32 | 64 |
| Where column *c* is | byte *c* of the line at `$4000` | byte *c* ÷ 2 of the line, at `$4000` if *c* is even, at `$6000` if odd |

This is where the eight reads of Reading a line twice pay off a second time. In hi-res the four "attribute"
reads per 8 dots fetch from the second file's bitmap instead, at the same addresses as the first file's
bitmap plus `$2000`, so the ULA gets the bytes of both files with no extra reads at all. The shift register,
which normally sends out each bit twice, once for each half of the 7 MHz dot, now sends each bit once. Neat,
isn't it? The reads were already there; hi-res only gives them a second job.

There are no attributes left to colour the dots, so the whole screen has **one ink and one paper**, set by
bits 5–3 of port `$FF`. The ink is that colour, BRIGHT; the paper is its opposite, also BRIGHT: `000` black on
white, `001` blue on yellow, and so on to `111` white on black. On the machine, three lines of text in an
8 × 8 font, one character per column, 64 columns to a line:

Hi-res is a text mode at heart, 64 columns of 8 × 8 characters where the Spectrum has 32, and it is the ULA's
only 512-dot mode: the left and right halves of a 7 MHz dot really differ here, as the overview chapter's
[Two dots for every position of the beam](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#two-dots-for-every-position-of-the-beam)
describes. It costs the same 12K as hi-colour, and its colours can still be changed for a band of the screen
by rewriting port `$FF` between lines.

## ULANext: the attribute byte split anew

The attribute byte spends its 8 bits as FLASH, BRIGHT, three bits of PAPER and three of INK: 8 inks, 8
papers, and a bright version of each. **ULANext** keeps the byte and the bitmap and changes only how the byte
is read. Bit 0 of the Palette Control register, **NextReg `$43`**, switches it on, and the ULANext Attribute
Byte Format register, **NextReg `$42`**, holds a **mask**: the bits the mask covers are the INK, the rest are
the PAPER. FLASH and BRIGHT are gone; their bits are colour now.

The rule is short. For an ink dot, the index is the attribute **AND** the mask: entries 0 upward. For a paper
dot, it is the bits above the mask, moved down, plus **128**: entries 128 upward. The mask must be a run of
ones from bit 0, `$01`, `$03`, `$07` and so on to `$FF`, and each choice trades inks for papers:

| Mask (NextReg `$42`) | Inks | Papers | The border |
|---|---|---|---|
| `$01` | 2 | 128 | entries 128–135 |
| `$03` | 4 | 64 | entries 128–135 |
| `$07` (after reset) | 8 | 32 | entries 128–135 |
| `$0F` | 16 | 16 | entries 128–135 |
| `$1F` | 32 | 8 | entries 128–135 |
| `$3F` | 64 | 4 | entries 128–135 |
| `$7F` | 128 | 2 | entries 128–135 |
| `$FF` | 256 | the fallback colour | the fallback colour |
| any other value | the attribute AND the mask | the fallback colour | entries 128–135 |

`$FF` is the **full-ink** mode: every attribute value is an ink, so a cell can have any of 256 colours, and
its paper dots show the Fallback Colour register, NextReg `$4A`, instead of a paper. The border's colour from
port `$FE` always uses the first eight paper entries, 128–135, except in the full-ink mode, where it too is
the fallback colour.

Why would you want this? The default `$07` is the Spectrum's 8 inks with **32 papers**, so a game can give its
backgrounds far more colours than its text. `$0F` gives 16 of each, a match for 16-colour graphics. And since
the palette entries are the Next's 9-bit colours, every one of those inks and papers can be any of 512
colours. What ULANext cannot do is change the bitmap: a cell is still 8 × 8 dots with one ink and one paper,
so colour clash remains. With hi-colour it does: ULANext reads the hi-colour colour bytes the same way, one
ink and paper for every 8 × 1 dots. The two together are, if you ask me, the most colourful thing the old
screen can do.

## ULA+: 64 colours

**ULA+** is not the Next's invention. It is a standard defined for Spectrum clones and add-ons, with software
and emulators that support it, and the Next implements it so that software runs. It keeps the Spectrum's
attribute layout and reuses its FLASH and BRIGHT bits: together they pick one of **four groups** of 16
colours, and each group has 8 inks and 8 papers. The colours live in entries **192–255** of the ULA palette:

Software switches ULA+ on and writes its colours through two ports of its own. **Port `$BF3B`**, the ULA+
Register, selects what **port `$FF3B`**, the ULA+ Data port, does: with bits 7–6 of the value `00`, bits 5–0
choose one of the 64 colours, and the next write to port `$FF3B` sets it; with `01`, bit 0 of the next write
to port `$FF3B` switches ULA+ on or off. ULA+ software writes its colours in its own bit order, `GGGRRRBB`; the
Next turns them into its `RRRGGGBB` and stores them in the ULA palette like any other colour, as the palettes
chapter's [The ULA: three maps of one palette](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/#the-ula-three-maps-of-one-palette)
explains. A Next program can also switch ULA+ with bit 3 of the ULA Control register, NextReg `$68`, and write
entries 192–255 through the ordinary palette registers.

With ULA+ the border uses the paper half of group 0, entries **200–207**. If both ULA+ and ULANext are
switched on, ULANext wins. And neither of them has FLASH: its bit now chooses colours, so nothing blinks.

## The border in every mode

The border is still the ULA's: three bits of port `$FE`, a colour for every dot of the frame outside the
256 × 192 display area, the wide area of the other layers included. What the Next adds is the choice of
palette entry, which follows the mode:

| Mode | The border's palette entry |
|---|---|
| Standard (and the Timex second screen and hi-colour) | 16–23: the PAPER entries, never BRIGHT |
| ULANext | 128–135; with the mask `$FF`, the fallback colour |
| ULA+ | 200–207 |
| Hi-res | the hi-res paper colour: port `$FE` is not used |

The hi-res line is worth a second look: in hi-res, the border takes the paper colour of port `$FF` bits 5–3,
so the whole screen, border and all, is one paper colour, and port `$FE`'s border is ignored. A loader that
flashes the border in hi-res flashes nothing at all.

A new border colour from port `$FE` reaches the screen at the start of the next 8-dot cell, as on the
Spectrum, so border stripes have the Spectrum's 8-dot steps; with Pentagon timing the change shows from the
very next dot, as on a Pentagon. And because the border is a ULA dot like any other, the rules of the
composition chapter's
[Transparent or not](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#when-a-layer-is-transparent)
apply to it: a border whose colour matches the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg `$14`, is
transparent, and other layers show through it.

## Scrolling by the dot

On the Spectrum, moving the screen meant moving its bytes, as the overview chapter's
[Scrolling: moving the picture without moving memory](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#scrolling-without-moving-memory)
explains. The Next's ULA has two offsets instead: the ULA X Scroll register, **NextReg `$26`**, 0–255 dots,
and the ULA Y Scroll register, **NextReg `$27`**, 0–191 lines. The ULA starts reading its picture that many
dots to the right and lines down, and the picture **wraps round**: across at 256, down at 192. (A Y offset of
192–255 counts on from 0, as 0–63.)

Now the interesting part: what happens to the **colours**? A program that scrolls the Spectrum's screen by
one dot moves the bitmap and leaves the attributes, which can only move by whole cells. The ULA's scroll moves
both. Here is how, for one 8-dot stretch of the line:

1. The high five bits of the X offset pick the cell the line starts from; the low three bits pick the dot

2. The shift register holds both cells, the one under the beam and the next one.
3. A counter started at the same dot tells the ULA when it passes from the first cell into the second.
4. At that point it switches from the first cell's attribute to the second's.

## Half a dot

One more bit makes the scroll finer still: bit 2 of the ULA Control register, **NextReg `$68`**, the
**half-dot scroll**, moves the picture half a dot further left, one 14 MHz dot. The shift register already
holds every dot twice, once for each half, so a shift by one place more is all it takes. In the standard modes
it is a smoothing step for slow scrolls. In hi-res, where every dot is a 14 MHz dot, it is what makes the
scroll exact: NextReg `$26` moves a hi-res picture two dots at a time, and the half-dot bit adds the one
between.

## When a scroll takes effect

The ULA takes both offsets, the half-dot bit and the Timex mode once for every 8 dots, at the start of the
cell it is about to fetch. A new value written in the middle of a line shows from the next cell on. That is
fine enough to scroll the bottom of the screen differently from the top, from a line interrupt or the
Copper, and even to change the offsets within a line.

## Clipped and switched off

The ULA's clip window, the Clip Window ULA register, **NextReg `$1A`**, works in the display area's own
coordinates: X 0–255 and Y 0–191, one unit per dot, both edges inside the window, as the overview chapter's
[The clip window: a rectangle for each layer](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#clip-windows-a-rectangle-for-each-layer)
explains. Two details matter for the ULA. A clipped dot is not paper, it is **transparent**: the layers below
the ULA, or the fallback colour, show there. And the **border is never clipped**: the window only cuts the
256 × 192 picture.

Bit 7 of the ULA Control register, NextReg `$68`, switches the ULA **off**. Then it is transparent everywhere,
border included, and the screen is made by the other layers and the fallback colour. It is not gone, though:
the blend modes of the composition chapter's
[Blending](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#blending-adding-instead-of-choosing) still read its
colours. [LoRes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/lores/), which takes the ULA's place,
obeys the same window and the same switch.

All of the ULA's switches in one place:

| Register | Bits | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| port `$FE` | 2–0 | the border colour |
| port `$FF` (and NextReg `$69` bits 5–0) | 5–3, 2–0 | hi-res ink; the Timex screen mode |
| port `$7FFD` (and NextReg `$69` bit 6) | 3 | show the shadow screen in bank 7 (no Timex modes) |
| NextReg `$26`, `$27` | 7–0 | the X and Y scroll |
| NextReg `$68` | 7, 3, 2 | ULA off; ULA+ on; the half-dot scroll |
| NextReg `$43` | 0 | ULANext on |
| NextReg `$42` | 7–0 | the ULANext ink mask |
| port `$BF3B`, `$FF3B` | | ULA+ colours and on/off |
| NextReg `$1A` | | the clip window |

## How Klive does it

Klive's Next core draws the ULA layer in C, with two renderers that follow the Timex modes. The four
standard-width modes, the Spectrum's screen, the second screen and hi-colour, share one:
`zxnextUlaRenderStandardScreen` reads, for every dot, a bitmap byte and a colour byte, and the mode only
changes where it reads them from, the bitmap at `$4000` or `$6000`, the colour at `$5800`, `$7800` or the
bitmap address plus `$2000`, exactly the addresses of the hi-colour figure. Hi-res has its own,
`zxnextUlaRenderHiResScreen`, which takes one bit per 14 MHz dot from the two files in turn.

Both turn an ink or paper dot into a palette index through one function, `zxnextUlaAttrPaletteIndex`: the
standard rule with BRIGHT and FLASH, ULANext's mask, or ULA+'s groups, as the ULANext and ULA+ sections
describe them, including the fallback colour for the full-ink mode and for a mask that is not a run of ones.
The scroll is applied as the address is formed, the half-dot bit by taking the second half of each dot from
the next dot along.

The emulator does not run a shift register; it renders a stretch of dots at a time. To change things where
the hardware does, it **latches** the border colour, the scroll offsets, the half-dot bit and the Timex mode:
a write is recorded with the frame position where the hardware would first use it, the start of the next
8-dot cell (every dot for the border with Pentagon timing), and the raster renders up to that position with
the old value. So a border stripe or a mid-line scroll split falls on the same dot as on the hardware.

Klive works out the floating bus the way Reading a line twice describes it: from the beam's position it knows
which of the Spectrum's four reads, if any, the ULA is making, and it forms that read's address as the ULA
does, with the scroll offsets, the Timex mode and the shadow screen in force at that moment. So a program
reading port `$FF` over a scrolled screen, or in hi-colour or hi-res, gets the bytes it would get on the
hardware.

In the IDE, the **ULA & I/O** panel shows the border colour and the beam's position (see
[Machine-state panels](/en/guides/debugging/machine-state-panels/#ula--io)); the **Next Registers** and
**Next Palettes** panels show the scroll, mode and ULANext registers and the ULA palette's entries (see
[Next Registers, Memory Mapping and Palettes](/en/guides/next/next-registers/)); and the
[Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view shows the ULA layer alone, with its clip window. And if you want to
read the code yourself, here is where it lives:

| What | Where |
|---|---|
| The renderers, the index rule, the border and the latches | `src/emu/machines/zxNext/wasm/zxnext/zxnext-ula.c` |
| Port `$FF` and port `$FE` | `zxnext-ports.c`, `zxnext-ula.c` in the same folder |
| The ULA palette and ULA+'s colour order | `zxnext-palette.c` |
| The pictures above, with the dots they check | `test/zxnext-hw/layers/next-display-scene.test.ts` |

## What to take home

Let's look back. The Next's ULA is the Spectrum's, byte for byte, but its screen memory, all of bank 5
and the first half of bank 7, is **block RAM inside the FPGA** with a port of its own. Contention is only
imitated, and only at 3.5 MHz, and there is no snow. The ULA reads **eight bytes per 16 dots**: the
Spectrum's four, which the floating bus shows, and the next cell's, which the scroll needs.

**Port `$FF`** selects the Timex modes: a **second screen** at `$6000`, **hi-colour** with a colour byte for
every 8 × 1 dots at the bitmap address plus `$2000`, and **hi-res**, 512 dots across from the two files
interleaved, in one colour pair from port `$FF` bits 5–3. The shadow screen in bank 7 has none of them.

Two new readings of the attribute byte add colour. **ULANext**, switched on by NextReg `$43` bit 0, splits the
byte by the mask in NextReg `$42`: inks from entry 0, papers from 128, and with `$FF` 256 inks with the
fallback colour as paper. **ULA+**, through ports `$BF3B` and `$FF3B` or NextReg `$68` bit 3, turns FLASH and
BRIGHT into four groups of 16 colours in entries 192–255. The border follows the mode's paper entries, and in
hi-res it is the hi-res paper.

NextRegs **`$26`** and **`$27`** scroll the picture by the dot, wrapping at 256 × 192, and the colours move
with it; NextReg `$68` bit 2 adds half a dot. Offsets and modes take effect from the next 8-dot cell, and
Klive latches each change at exactly that cell, rendering the four standard-width modes with one renderer and
hi-res with another.
