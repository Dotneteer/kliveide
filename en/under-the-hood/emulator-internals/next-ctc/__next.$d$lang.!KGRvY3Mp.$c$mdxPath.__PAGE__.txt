# CTC: Four Timers in a Ring

Switch a Next from 3.5 MHz to 28 MHz and every delay loop in your program runs eight times faster. The music
player that counted T-states between two notes now plays like a chipmunk, the sample that took a second takes
an eighth of one, and the stopwatch you wrote out of `DJNZ` measures nothing at all. Yet some things on the
Next keep their pace whatever the CPU does. Who is keeping time for them?

I think every serious Next program needs a clock it can trust, and the CPU is not one. The CPU's speed is a
setting, contention stretches it, the DMA stops it. The **CTC**, the counter/timer circuit, counts the
machine's master clock instead, and it is the piece I reach for whenever something must happen at a rate: a
sample clock, a game tick independent of the display, a timeout, or a stopwatch to see how long a routine
really takes.

You should know one chapter first. [Interrupts: Fourteen Voices, Two
Wires](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/) explains how a CTC channel's interrupt reaches
the CPU, its vector, its status bit and the daisy chain; here we look at what makes the channel ring. [The
Frame and the Beam](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/) explains the master clock the
CTC counts.

## The road ahead

We start with the chip the CTC copies and the ports of its four channels. Then we open one channel up: a
prescaler, a down-counter and the pulse that comes out when the count runs out. The control word comes next,
with the channel's four states, and then the two ways a channel can count, by time or by events. The heart of
the chapter is the ring that joins the four channels, and what you can build with it: exact rates and a
16-bit stopwatch. Last come interrupts, resets, and what a later core version changed.

<Callout type="info">
The facts are for **core 3.02.01**, except where a section says otherwise. The master clock is **28 MHz**, as
in VGA mode 0 and HDMI, and T-states are for a CPU at **3.5 MHz**. Every fact was checked against the Next's
hardware design.
</Callout>

## A Zilog chip in the FPGA

The Next's CTC is a copy of the Z80 CTC, the counter/timer chip Zilog made as a companion for the Z80, and
programs written for it on other Z80 machines speak the same language here. I call it **the metronome**, as
the interrupts chapter does: it beats a steady rate, and it does not care how fast the musician plays.

The Zilog chip has four channels, and so does the Next, at four ports:

- **Channel 0** at port `$183B`, **channel 1** at `$193B`, **channel 2** at `$1A3B` and **channel 3** at
  `$1B3B`. The low byte is `$3B`, bits 15–11 of the address are `00011`, and bits 10–8 pick the channel.
- **Ports `$1C3B` to `$1F3B`** belong to channels 4 to 7, which have no hardware: reading them gives `$00`,
  and writing them does nothing.
- **Writing** a channel's port sends it a control word or a time constant; **reading** it gives the channel's
  current count.
- The ports can be switched off with bit 3 of NextReg `$85`, and they are on after a reset. A channel that
  runs keeps running while its port is off.

There is one thing the Next leaves out. A Zilog CTC has pins for an outside clock or trigger on each
channel; the Next's channels have no outside pins. Their trigger inputs are wired to each other, in a ring,
and that ring is the most interesting thing about them.

- The **prescaler** is an 8-bit counter that counts every clock of the 28 MHz master clock. It gives a pulse
  each time its low four bits are all ones, every **16** clocks, or, when bit 5 of the control word asks for
  it, each time all eight are ones, every **256** clocks.
- The **down-counter** holds the channel's count, 8 bits. In timer mode each prescaler pulse counts it down
  by one; in counter mode each edge on the trigger input does.
- The **time constant register** holds the value the count starts from. When the count reaches zero, the
  channel sends one pulse on its output, **ZC/TO** ("zero count / time out"), and reloads the count from the
  time constant on the next clock. Then it counts down again, with no help from your program. A time
  constant of 0 means **256**, in both modes: the first tick, a prescaler pulse or a trigger edge, takes the
  count from 0 to 255, and the zero count comes after 256 ticks.

So a timer channel's period is simply the prescaler times the time constant, in master clocks, with 0
counting as 256. The same numbers in time:

- **Prescaler 16**: a count every 16 clocks, 0.571 µs, which is exactly **2 T-states** at 3.5 MHz. Periods
  from 16 clocks (time constant 1) to 4,096 clocks (time constant 0), 146 µs.
- **Prescaler 256**: a count every 256 clocks, 9.14 µs, **32 T-states** at 3.5 MHz. Periods from 256 clocks
  to 65,536 clocks, 2.34 ms.

1. The channel starts with the count at 3 and the prescaler at 0.
2. Every 16 clocks the prescaler pulses, and the count goes to 2, then 1, then 0, at clock 48.
3. For that one clock the count reads 0, and ZC/TO is high: one pulse 36 ns long.
4. On the next clock the count is 3 again. The prescaler never stopped, so the next pulse comes at clock 64,
   and the next zero count at clock 96: exactly 48 clocks after the first.

Notice the first step, because the Zilog datasheet and some books leave it vague: while a channel is not
running, its prescaler is held at 0 and its count at the time constant. So a timer's first count comes
exactly one prescaler period after it starts, and its first zero count one full period after it. There is no
random phase to average out. I like that a lot: a timer you start at a known moment beats at known moments.

And this is the answer to the opening puzzle. The CPU's clock is made from the same master clock, so whatever
its speed, it changes nothing for the metronome. A channel that beats 17,500 times a second at 3.5 MHz beats
17,500 times a second at 28 MHz.

There is one exception, and it comes from the monitor, not from your program. Some VGA monitors refuse the
Next's 50 Hz frame, so the machine has seven **VGA modes**, 0 to 6, chosen in the **Video Timing register,
NextReg `$11`**. Mode 0 runs the master clock at exactly 28 MHz; the others run it faster, in steps up to
33 MHz in mode 6, so that the whole picture comes a little faster and the monitor accepts it. The register
can be written only in config mode, so the machine's set-up picks the mode, not your program; the frame and
beam chapter's [scan doubler and VGA](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#the-scan-doubler-and-vga)
section tells the story. In a faster mode the metronome speeds up with the master clock, but so does the CPU:
one count of prescaler 16 is still exactly 2 T-states at 3.5 MHz, and only the rate in seconds changes, by
up to 18%.

## The control word

A channel is programmed with a **control word**, a byte with bit 0 set, and often a **time constant** after
it:

```text
control word:  I M P E T C R 1
  I = 1: interrupt on every zero count
  M = mode: 0 timer, 1 counter
  P = prescaler: 0 divides by 16, 1 by 256
  E = trigger edge: 0 falling, 1 rising
  T = 1: a timer waits for a trigger to start
  C = 1: a time constant follows
  R = 1: software reset
```

A byte with bit 0 clear, when no time constant is expected, is an **interrupt vector** on the Zilog chip.
The Next throws it away: its vectors come from the interrupt controller, not from the CTC. And the byte after
a control word with C set is always the time constant, whatever its bit 0.

A channel is always in one of four states, and the control word moves it between them:

1. **Reset.** After a reset, the channel holds still with its count at the time constant, which is 0. Only a
   control word with **C** set wakes it; one without C is stored, but the channel stays where it is. This
   catches people out: the first control word must announce a time constant.
2. **Waiting for a time constant.** The next byte is the constant, and the channel moves on.
3. **Waiting for a trigger.** A timer with **T** set waits here for an edge on its trigger input; any other
   channel passes straight through, on the next clock.
4. **Running.** The channel counts, as long as nothing stops it.

The **software reset**, **R**, stops a channel from any state: with C clear it goes back to state 1, with C
set to state 2, and it starts again from the new constant. Without R, a control word does not stop a running
channel. Its settings change at once, the prescaler, the mode and the interrupt enable, and the count goes
on. With C set, the next byte becomes the new time constant, and the channel uses it from its next zero
count; the period under way finishes at the old one. I find that a lovely property for a sample player: you
can change the rate between two samples without a click.

Here is channel 0 set up as a timer of prescaler 16 and time constant 100, a zero count every 1,600 clocks,
17,500 times a second, with its interrupt on:

```z80klive
        ld bc,$183b            ; channel 0
        ld a,%10000101         ; interrupt, timer, /16,
        out (c),a              ; constant follows
        ld a,100
        out (c),a              ; starts on the next clock
```

If you inherit a channel in an unknown state, my advice is to write `$03`, a software reset without C, twice.
If the channel happened to expect a time constant, the first byte is taken as one; the second is then
certainly a control word, and it leaves the channel in state 1, ready for your setup.

## Timer and counter

In **timer mode**, bit 6 clear, a channel counts the prescaler's pulses: it measures time. In **counter
mode**, bit 6 set, it ignores the prescaler and counts edges on its trigger input instead: it counts events.
On a Zilog chip the event could be anything wired to the pin; on the Next, it is always the previous
channel's zero count.

The trigger input matters in timer mode too. A timer with bit 3, **T**, set does not start when it gets its
time constant: it waits for the first edge on its trigger input, and then starts, with the prescaler at 0. So
one channel can start another at an exact moment, its own zero count.

Bit 4, **E**, chooses the edge. The ZC/TO pulse is one master clock long, so its rising edge comes as the
upstream count reaches zero and its falling edge one clock later. Either way the channel counts once per
upstream zero count; the edge only moves the moment by 36 ns. But one detail of E deserves a warning:
**writing a control word that changes E counts as an edge**. A counter counts one down, and a timer waiting
for its trigger starts. My rule: choose the edge when you set a channel up, and do not touch it afterwards.

```z80klive
        out (c),a

        out (c),a

        out (c),a
```

Only channel 1 interrupts, every 20 ms, and the display's frame rate, which is close to 50 Hz but never exactly
50 Hz, has nothing to do with it. A game whose logic runs on this tick runs at the same speed on every
display. Two counters in the chain stretch the period further still: three channels with constants of 0 and
prescaler 256 make a zero count about every 153 seconds.

Watch the ring's one trap: it is a ring. Channel 0 in counter mode counts channel 3's zero counts, so a
chain may start at any channel, but four counters and no timer count nothing. Start every chain with a
timer; a timer ignores its trigger input once it runs, so the ring is cut there.

## A stopwatch

Reading a channel gives its count at that moment, and that makes the CTC the best stopwatch the Next has.
Read before, read after, subtract: the count goes down, so the elapsed time is the first reading minus the
second. One channel with prescaler 16 and time constant 0 measures up to 256 counts of 2 T-states, 512
T-states at 3.5 MHz.

For longer code, chain two channels into a **16-bit counter**: channel 0 as a timer with prescaler 16 and
time constant 0, the low byte, and channel 1 in counter mode with time constant 0, the high byte. That counts
65,536 times 16 clocks, 37.4 ms, nearly two frames, with a resolution of 2 T-states.

There is a catch in reading it: the two bytes are two `IN` instructions, and the low byte can wrap between
them. The fix is old and simple. Read the high byte, then the low byte, then the high byte again; if the two
high bytes differ, the low byte wrapped in between, so read again. Read again too when the low byte is 0,
because that is the moment channel 0 reaches zero and channel 1 counts, and a reading taken there cannot
tell which side of the count it is on:

```z80klive
; channel 0: timer, /16, constant 0; channel 1: counter, 0
; returns the 16-bit count in HL; changes A, BC
Read:   ld bc,$193b
        in h,(c)               ; high byte: channel 1
        dec b                  ; $183B: channel 0
        in l,(c)               ; low byte
        inc b
        in a,(c)               ; high byte again
        cp h
        jr nz,Read             ; it counted: read again
        inc l
        dec l
        jr z,Read              ; low byte 0: read again
        ret
```

Call `Read` before and after the code you measure, subtract the second count from the first, and double the
difference for T-states at 3.5 MHz. The difference includes part of the reading itself, so measure an empty
block once and take that off. At 28 MHz the same count is 16 T-states, so the stopwatch is
coarser in T-states but just as exact in time; for a routine that takes less than a count, count its
T-states from the instruction tables instead.

## Interrupts

Every zero count of a channel is an interrupt request, and the [interrupts
chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/) tells the rest of the story. From the
CTC's side, a few facts to keep together:

- **The enable is bit 7 of the control word**, and also bit *n* of the **INT EN 1 register, NextReg
  `$C5`**, for channel *n*. They are the same flip-flop: whichever you wrote last wins, and a control word
  without bit 7 switches the interrupt off.
- **The status is set on every zero count**, enabled or not, in bit *n* of the **INT Status 1 register,
  NextReg `$C9`**, and writing a 1 clears it. That is the polled mode: leave the interrupt off and watch the
  bit. The status bit is only a record; one you forget to clear blocks nothing.
- **In hardware IM2 mode** channels 0 to 3 are sources 3 to 6 of the chain, after the line interrupt and the
  UART receivers and before the ULA, with vector offsets `$06` to `$0C`. **In pulse mode** a zero count
  starts the shared pulse, and the vector is `$FF`.
- **NextReg `$20` bits 3–0** request a CTC interrupt by software, without a zero count.
- **NextReg `$CD` bits 3–0** let a channel's interrupt break into a DMA transfer. The CTC cannot start or
  pace the DMA; the DMA has its own prescaler for that.

A zero count is also edges for other hardware: channel 3's can clock a joystick pin. In the joystick's I/O
mode, NextReg `$0B` with bit 7 set, bits 5–4 = 01 and bit 0 set, pin 7 of the joystick port toggles on every
zero count of channel 3: a square wave at half its rate, for a device that wants a clock.

## Resets

A reset, hard or soft, puts every channel into the reset state: control word 0, time constant 0, interrupt
off, and a count that reads 0. NextReg `$C5` reads 0 too, and the status bits in `$C9` are cleared. Nothing
runs until a program writes a control word with a time constant. So the metronome is silent after a reset,
and, as the interrupts chapter advises, a program should set up every channel it uses itself.

## What core 3.02.04 changed

Later cores rewrote the channel's state machine, and two differences can be seen from a program:

- **The count skips 0.** On the clock where a count of 1 would become 0, the channel reloads the time
  constant directly, so a channel with time constant 100 reads 100 down to 1, never 0. ZC/TO still comes on
  the same clock as before, so periods, chains and interrupts are unchanged; only a reading at that one clock
  differs. A time constant of 0 still means 256.
- **A control word with C while a timer waits for its trigger** now makes the channel wait for a new time
  constant. In 3.02.01 the channel kept waiting with its old constant, and a trigger could start it before
  the new one arrived.

Neither changes how you should program the CTC: send the time constant after every control word that
announces one, and nothing in your program will notice which core it runs on.

## What to take home

The CTC, the metronome, is a Zilog Z80 CTC with four channels at ports `$183B` to `$1B3B`; channels 4 to 7
have ports but no hardware. Each channel counts the 28 MHz master clock, not the CPU's clock, so its rates
hold at every CPU speed.

A channel is a prescaler of 16 or 256, an 8-bit down-counter and a time constant. At zero it sends a
one-clock ZC/TO pulse and reloads, so its period is the prescaler times the constant, with 0 meaning 256:
from 16 clocks, 2 T-states at 3.5 MHz, to 65,536 clocks, 2.34 ms. While it is not running, its prescaler and
count are held, so a timer beats at exact moments from the time it starts.

The control word, bit 0 set, chooses the interrupt, the mode, the prescaler, the edge, a triggered start, a
time constant to follow and a software reset; the first one after a reset must announce a constant. A
control word without a reset changes a running channel's settings at once and its constant from the next
zero count. In counter mode a channel counts the previous channel's zero counts, in a ring 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 →
0, which makes the channels a chain of dividers: exact rates such as 50 Hz, long periods, and a 16-bit
stopwatch with a resolution of 2 T-states, read high, low, high again.

Every zero count sets its bit in NextReg `$C9` and interrupts if bit 7 of the control word or NextReg `$C5`
allows. A reset silences every channel.
