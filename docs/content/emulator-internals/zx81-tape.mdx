# ZX81: How Programs Go to Tape

The ZX81 has no disk drive and no memory that survives switching off. When you pull the plug, your
program is gone. To keep it, you connect the computer to an **ordinary audio cassette recorder**,
the same kind you would play music on, type `SAVE "NAME"`, and press *Record*. Minutes later the
program is on the tape as sound. To get it back, you type `LOAD "NAME"` and press *Play*.

This article explains how that works, from the magnetic tape up to the last instruction of the ROM.
As in [How the Screen Is Drawn](/emulator-internals/zx81-display), you need to know the Z80 and have
some engineering basics. You do not need to know anything about tape recorders: we start there.

Like the picture, the tape interface is a lesson in doing a lot with very little. There is **no
tape chip**: the ULA gives the program one input bit and one output level, and **the rest is
software**, a few hundred bytes of ROM. The CPU makes every pulse of a recording with its own `IN`
and `OUT` instructions and counts their length by executing loops of known duration. Reading is the
same in reverse: the CPU samples one bit tens of thousands of times a second, and decides what it
heard by counting. The format it uses is slow, but it is remarkably hard to get wrong, and you will
see why.

## Part 1: How a cassette recorder works

### Magnetism on a ribbon

A cassette holds a long, thin ribbon of plastic, the **tape**, coated on one side with a layer of
tiny iron-oxide particles. Each particle behaves like a minute bar magnet. On a blank or erased tape
they point every which way, and their fields cancel out. Recording lines them up: wherever many
particles point the same way, that stretch of tape becomes a weak magnet, and it stays one for years.

The thing that lines them up is the **head**: a ring of soft iron with a coil of wire wound round it
and a very narrow **gap** cut into the ring where it touches the tape. A current through the coil
makes a magnetic field in the ring. At the gap the field has nowhere to go but out, so it bulges
into the tape running past and magnetizes whatever is under the gap at that instant, more strongly
the stronger the current, one way or the other depending on its direction.

So recording turns **time into position**, just as a television turns time into position across the
screen. Whatever current flows through the head now is written onto the bit of tape under the gap
now; a moment later that bit has moved on, and the next one gets the next moment.

### Reading it back: the head sees changes, not levels

To play the tape, the same kind of head is used the other way round. The magnetized tape moving past
the gap makes a magnetic field flow round the ring and through the coil. But a coil does not
produce a voltage from a field; it produces one from a **changing** field. That is Faraday's law of
induction: the voltage is proportional to how fast the field through the coil changes.

This has a consequence that every tape format has to live with. Suppose a computer records a clean
square wave: the current jumps up, stays, jumps down, stays. On the tape, that is long stretches of
"magnetized this way" and "magnetized that way". On playback, a long stretch of constant
magnetization produces **no voltage at all**. Only the boundaries, where the magnetization flips,
produce a pulse: a spike up for one direction of flip, a spike down for the other.

The spikes are also blurred. The gap has a width, so it reads a small stretch of tape at once and
smears fast changes together; the tape's particles are not perfect; and the recorder's amplifiers
are built for music, not for square waves. A cheap recorder of the time handled frequencies up to
roughly 10 kHz and filtered out the rest. What reaches the earphone socket is a wobbly analogue
wave that only resembles what was recorded.

A real recorder does more to make music sound good. It **erases** the tape with a separate head just
before the recording head, so old sound does not mix with new; and it adds a strong, inaudible
high-frequency **bias** to the recording current, which makes the particles respond more evenly. None
of that changes the picture above: a computer gets back the *changes* in what it recorded, blurred.

### Moving the tape at a steady speed

Since time is position, the tape must move past the head at a **constant speed**, or the playback
runs fast or slow. A compact cassette runs at **4.76 cm/s** (1⅞ inches a second). The reels do not
set that speed: as the tape winds from one reel to the other their diameters keep changing. A
spinning steel shaft, the **capstan**, does. A rubber **pinch roller** presses the tape against it,
and the capstan pulls the tape past the heads at its own steady speed. The take-up reel only winds up
what the capstan delivers.

No mechanism is perfect. The capstan is never exactly round, the motor's speed drifts with the
battery and the temperature, and two recorders never run at quite the same speed. Slow speed wobble
is called **wow**, fast wobble **flutter**, and a tape recorded on one machine and played on another
can easily run a few per cent fast or slow. There is one more classic trap: the head's gap must stand
exactly at right angles to the tape (its **azimuth**). If the heads of two recorders are tilted
differently, the second one reads every edge smeared across the gap, and the high frequencies fade
away. "It loads on my recorder but not on yours" was very often azimuth.

### What this means for a computer

So a cassette recorder gives a computer a channel that:

- **returns changes, not levels**: spikes at the edges rather than the square wave that was sent;
- **blurs fast detail**: anything much above 10 kHz is lost;
- **does not keep time**: the playback speed is a few per cent off, and wobbles;
- **has an unknown volume and polarity**: the user turns the volume knob, and some recorders invert
  the signal.

A tape format that works on cheap recorders has to shrug off all four. Hold on to that list: the
ZX81's format answers every item on it.

## Part 2: The players

| Player | Its job on tape |
|---|---|
| **The cassette recorder** | Any audio recorder. Its **MIC** (microphone) input records what the computer sends; its **EAR** (earphone) output plays it back. The ZX81 has no motor control, so you press *Play* and *Record* yourself. |
| **The ZX81's MIC socket** | The computer's output to the recorder. It is driven by the **same ULA pin that makes the video signal**, so whatever the CPU sends to tape also reaches the television: that is why the screen fills with stripes during `SAVE`. |
| **The ZX81's EAR socket** | The computer's input from the recorder. The ULA turns the incoming audio into one bit: **bit 7 of port `$FE`**, high while there is signal. |
| **The Z80 CPU** | Does all the work. It makes each pulse with `OUT` and `IN` instructions and times it with delay loops; it reads by sampling bit 7 of port `$FE` in loops of known length, and counts. |
| **The 8K ROM** | Holds the `SAVE` and `LOAD` routines: about 240 bytes at `$02F6`–`$03E4`, plus a few helpers. |

### One output level, one input bit

The ZX81's output to tape is not a separate signal. The display article explained that, while the
NMI generator is off, **an `IN` from an even port starts VSYNC** (the ULA's output drops to the sync
level) **and any `OUT` ends it**. During `SAVE` the NMI generator is off, so the CPU can switch the
ULA's output between two levels with nothing but `IN` and `OUT`, and that output is what reaches the
MIC socket.

The input is just as plain. Reading port `$FE` returns the keyboard in bits 0–4, and in **bit 7 the
tape signal** from the EAR socket: 1 while the audio is above the ULA's threshold, 0 while it is not.
Reading it is the same `IN` instruction that reads the keys.

So all the ZX81 offers its software is one level to switch and one bit to look at. Everything else,
the speed, the format, the error handling, is in the ROM.

<Callout type="info">
**Why the picture breaks up.** Both `SAVE` and `LOAD` need every T-state of the CPU for timing, so
the ROM switches the display off first, exactly as `FAST` does: the routine that does it (`$02E7`)
is the one the display article explains in
[Starting and stopping the loop](/emulator-internals/zx81-display#starting-and-stopping-the-loop). With the display stopped, the ULA's video
pin carries only what the tape routines do with `IN` and `OUT`. During `SAVE` that is the tape
signal itself; during `LOAD` it is the rhythm of the sampling loop, which changes when a signal
arrives. Either way, the television shows moving stripes: the sound made visible.
</Callout>

## Part 3: Bits as bursts of sound

### Bursts and silences

The ZX81 does not encode its bits in the shape or the timing of individual edges. It sends **bursts**
of a tone, separated by **silences**:

- A **wave** is one high stretch and one low stretch of the output, about **300 µs** in all: a tone
  of about 3.3 kHz, in the middle of what any recorder can handle.
- A **0 bit** is a burst of **4 waves**; a **1 bit** is a burst of **9 waves**.
- After every bit's burst comes a **silence** of about **1.5 ms**, the output held low.

A byte is its 8 bits, the most significant bit first. There is nothing between bytes but the silence
that follows every bit.

### Why bursts?

Go back to the four problems of a cassette recorder in Part 1. The ZX81's loader never looks at an
edge, a wave or a level. It asks one question, over and over: **"is there sound right now?"**, and it
measures **how long the sound lasts**. Then:

- **Changes, not levels.** The spikes that come back at every edge are sound. A burst of spikes is a
  burst of sound, just as long as the burst that was recorded.
- **Blurring.** A 3.3 kHz tone survives any recorder. Even a smeared, rounded burst is still a burst.
- **Speed.** A 1 bit's burst is more than twice as long as a 0 bit's, and the loader's dividing line
  sits between them with a wide margin (Part 6 counts it): the tape can run very fast or very slow
  and still load.
- **Volume and polarity.** An inverted signal is still sound, and so is a quiet or a loud one, as long
  as it gets above the input's threshold.

The price is speed. A 0 bit takes about 2.55 ms and a 1 bit about 4.05 ms, so the ZX81 manages
about 390 bits a second for zeros and 250 for ones, roughly **40 bytes a second** for mixed data.
These are the figures usually quoted for the machine, for example in
[Wikipedia's ZX81 article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZX81) (400 and 250 bits a second). A 16K program takes several
minutes. Sinclair chose robustness over speed, and for a machine sold with whatever recorder
the customer happened to own, that was the right choice.

## Part 4: What is on the tape

### A recording

A `SAVE` writes four things, one after the other:

1. **About 5 seconds of silence.** It gives the recorder time to get up to speed and lets you find the
   start of a program by ear. More importantly, a long silence is how the loader recognises that a
   program **starts** (Part 6).
2. **The name**, one byte per character in the ZX81's own character codes (not ASCII). Its **last
   character has bit 7 set**, which marks the end of the name. That is the only length information
   it has.
3. **The memory from `$4009` up to the byte before `E_LINE`**: most of the system variables, the
   BASIC program, the display file and the variables, exactly as they are in RAM.
4. Nothing else. There is **no header, no length, no checksum**. The end of the data is found the
   way it is found in RAM: by the value of `E_LINE` inside the data.

### A snapshot, not a file

That third item deserves a closer look, because it makes the ZX81's recording quite unlike a file
on a modern computer. The ROM does not pick out "the program". It saves a **contiguous slice of
memory**, and the ZX81 keeps everything a BASIC session needs in that slice, in this order:

| Address | What it is |
|---|---|
| `$4009`–`$407C` | The system variables from `VERSN` on: 116 bytes, among them the pointers `D_FILE`, `VARS` and `E_LINE`, the line to run next (`NXTLIN`), and the display mode (`CDFLAG`). |
| `$407D`… | The BASIC program, line by line. |
| `D_FILE`… | The **display file**: the screen. |
| `VARS`… | The variables, ending with an `$80` byte. |
| `E_LINE` | The line being edited. Not saved: it marks the end. |

Loading puts every one of those bytes back at the same address. So `LOAD` restores **the whole
state** of the session: the program, every variable with its value, the screen as it was, and the
system variables that point to all of them. Three of those system variables produce well-known
effects, which [Part 7](#part-7-living-with-the-format) explains: `NXTLIN` makes self-starting
programs, `CDFLAG` brings back FAST or SLOW mode, and the display file makes the old screen reappear.

The nine bytes below `$4009` are not saved, and for a good reason. They describe **this** machine and
this moment: the error code (`ERR_NR`), the interpreter's flags, the machine stack pointer
(`ERR_SP`), the top of the memory BASIC may use (`RAMTOP`), and the line being run (`PPC`). A program
saved on a 16K machine must not tell a 1K machine that it has 16K of RAM.

### The `.P` file

Emulators store ZX81 programs in **`.P` files** (also named `.81`), and the format is as simple as it
gets: **a `.P` file is the bytes the ROM saves after the name**, from `$4009` to `E_LINE − 1`, with
nothing added. Its first byte is `VERSN` (always 0 on a ZX81), and the word at offset 11 (address
`$4014`) is `E_LINE`, which says where the file ends: a valid file is `E_LINE − $4009` bytes long.

A `.P` file has no name. Klive puts a one-byte name in front of it when it plays one, `$80`, which is
a single inverted space with its "last character" bit set: `LOAD ""` accepts any name, so it accepts
that one.

The ZX80 has its own format, the **`.O`** (or `.80`) file. The ZX80's 4K ROM saves memory from
`$4000`, with no name at all, and its routines are different. This article stays with the ZX81.

## Part 5: SAVE

Saving is the simpler half, because the CPU is in charge of the timing: it just has to make the
right pulses at the right moments.

### The name

`SAVE` and `LOAD` start with the same helper at `$03A8`, which we call `Name`. It evaluates the
string after the command and gets it ready to go to tape:

```z80klive
; $03A8: evaluate and prepare the name of SAVE and LOAD
; Out: DE = the name's first character, HL = its last
;      carry set if the name is empty
Name:
    call $0F55        ; evaluate the expression after the command
    ld a,($4001)      ; A = FLAGS
    add a,a           ; bit 6 (string or number) to the sign,
                      ; bit 7 (checking or running) to carry
    jp m,$0D9A        ; a number, not a string: report C
    pop hl            ; HL = the caller's return address
    ret nc            ; only checking the syntax: return past the caller
    push hl           ; running: put it back
    call SetFast      ; $02E7: stop the display (as FAST does)
    call $13F8        ; DE = the string's start, BC = its length
    ld h,d
    ld l,e            ; HL = the string's start
    dec c             ; C = length - 1 ...
    ret m             ; ... negative: empty, return with carry set
    add hl,bc         ; HL = the last character (clears carry)
    set 7,(hl)        ; mark it as the last one
    ret
```

A few things in this short routine are worth a second look:

- **The same code checks syntax and runs.** The ZX81 checks each BASIC line when you enter it, by
  running the same routines with a flag saying "only check". In that case `RET NC` returns not to
  `SAVE` but to whoever called `SAVE`, because the `POP HL` has removed `SAVE`'s return address: the
  name is checked, and nothing else happens.
- **The display stops here.** The call to `SetFast` (`$02E7`, see [Starting and stopping the loop](/emulator-internals/zx81-display#starting-and-stopping-the-loop)) waits for the next border NMI and switches the NMI
  generator off, so from here on the CPU belongs to the tape routines, and the `IN`/`OUT` VSYNC
  switch of Part 2 works.
- **The end marker goes into the string itself.** `SET 7,(HL)` changes the last character of the
  name in the calculator's workspace, so that `SAVE` can send the name exactly as it is in memory.
- **Carry survives to say "empty".** `INC`/`DEC` on 8-bit registers do not touch carry, and neither
  does anything after the `ADD A,A`: when the name is empty, the carry set by "running" comes back to
  the caller unchanged. A non-empty name leaves through the `ADD HL,BC`, which clears it.

<Callout type="info">
`DEC C` / `RET M` looks only at the low byte of the length and calls it "negative" when bit 7 is
set. So a name of 129 to 256 characters is treated as an empty one, a corner the ROM's authors could
safely leave alone.
</Callout>

### The leader and the bytes

Here is `SAVE` itself:

```z80klive
; $02F6: SAVE "name"
Save:
    call Name         ; DE = the name, HL = its last character
    jr c,$02F4        ; empty name: report F
    ex de,hl          ; HL = the name's first character
    ld de,$12CB       ; 4811 rounds of the leader loop: about 5 s
Leader:
    call Break1       ; read the SPACE key (and start VSYNC)
    jr nc,$0332       ; BREAK pressed: report D
Delay:
    djnz Delay        ; 256 x 13 T-states
    dec de
    ld a,d
    or e
    jr nz,Leader
SaveName:
    call OutByte      ; send one character of the name
    bit 7,(hl)        ; was it the last one?
    inc hl
    jr z,SaveName
    ld hl,$4009       ; from VERSN on
SaveData:
    call OutByte      ; send one byte of memory
    call NextByte     ; $01FC: next byte; leave when HL reaches E_LINE
    jr SaveData
```

The report codes are a byte after a `RST $08`: at `$02F4` the ROM holds `RST $08` / `DEFB $0E`,
which prints report **F** ("no program name").

**The leader is silence, made by an `IN`.** `Break1` (`$0F46`) is the ROM's BREAK test, and it reads
the keyboard row that holds SPACE with `IN A,($FE)`. With the NMI generator off, that `IN` starts
VSYNC, and no `OUT` follows to end it, so the output to tape sits at the sync level for the whole
leader: about **5 seconds of silence**. The loop takes 3,448 T-states a round, 4,811 rounds: 16.6
million T-states, 5.1 seconds. Checking BREAK in every round means you can stop a `SAVE` at any time.

**The name and the data are sent the same way**, one byte at a time by `OutByte`. The name loop stops
after the character with bit 7 set. The data loop starts at `$4009` and leaves through `$01FC`:

```z80klive
; $01FC: step to the next byte of a SAVE or LOAD; finish at E_LINE
NextByte:
    inc hl            ; the next address
    ex de,hl
    ld hl,($4014)     ; HL = E_LINE
    scf
    sbc hl,de         ; E_LINE - address - 1
    ex de,hl          ; HL = the address again
    ret nc            ; address < E_LINE: carry on
    pop hl            ; done: drop the return into the loop ...
                      ; ... and fall into $0207, which restarts
                      ; the display in SLOW mode, then returns
                      ; from SAVE or LOAD
```

When the address reaches `E_LINE`, `POP HL` throws away the return into the byte loop, and the code
runs straight on into the routine at `$0207`, the one that starts SLOW mode (the display article
explains it in [Starting and stopping the loop](/emulator-internals/zx81-display#starting-and-stopping-the-loop)). That routine restarts the picture if the machine is in SLOW
mode and returns, and since the loop's return address is gone, it returns from the `SAVE` command
itself. The same routine ends `LOAD`.

### One byte, eight bursts

`OutByte` is the heart of `SAVE`. It sends the byte at `(HL)`, most significant bit first:

```z80klive
; $031E: send the byte at (HL)
OutByte:
    ld e,(hl)         ; E = the byte
    scf               ; a 1 that follows the bits as a marker
OutBit:
    rl e              ; next bit to carry; the marker moves in
    ret z             ; E = 0: only the marker was left, done
    sbc a,a           ; A = $FF for a 1 bit, $00 for a 0 bit
    and $05           ; 5 or 0
    add a,$04         ; 9 or 4
    ld c,a            ; C = the number of waves
OutWave:
    out ($FF),a       ; end VSYNC: the output goes high
    ld b,$23
High:
    djnz High         ; wait
    call Break1       ; IN A,($FE): VSYNC on, the output goes low
    jr nc,$03A6       ; BREAK pressed: report D
    ld b,$1E
Low:
    djnz Low          ; wait
    dec c
    jr nz,OutWave     ; the next wave of this burst
Silence:
    and a             ; clear carry for the next RL E
    djnz Silence      ; B = 0: 256 rounds, about 1.3 ms
    jr OutBit
```

**The bits come out through carry.** `SCF` followed by `RL E` moves the byte's top bit into carry and
shifts a 1 into the bottom of E. Each further `RL E` (with carry cleared by `AND A`) moves the next
bit out and the 1 further up. After eight bits, the next `RL E` pushes the marker out too, leaves E
at 0, and `RET Z` ends the byte. A marker bit instead of a loop counter saves a register.

**A bit becomes a count without a jump.** `SBC A,A` turns carry into `$FF` or `$00`; `AND $05` and
`ADD A,$04` make that 9 or 4. No branch means both kinds of bit go through exactly the same
instructions, so they differ only in the number of waves, never in the timing of each wave.

**`OUT` and `IN` make the waves.** `OUT ($FF),A` ends VSYNC, and the output to tape goes high. Then
`Break1`'s `IN A,($FE)` starts VSYNC again, and the output goes low. The BREAK test is not an extra:
it **is** the falling edge. Counted from the listing:

| Part | T-states | Time |
|---|---:|---:|
| High: `OUT`, `LD B,$23`, 35 rounds of `DJNZ`, into `Break1` up to its `IN` | 503 | 155 µs |
| Low: the rest of `Break1`, `JR NC`, `LD B,$1E`, 30 rounds of `DJNZ`, `DEC C`, `JR NZ` | 472 | 145 µs |
| **One wave** | **975** | **300 µs** |
| Silence after the last wave: its low part, then 256 rounds of `AND A` and `DJNZ`, and the next bit's setup | 4,861 | 1.50 ms |

That gives 8,289 T-states, 2.55 ms, for a 0 bit (4 waves and the silence) and 13,164 T-states,
4.05 ms, for a 1 bit (9 waves and the silence): the figures of Part 3. Notice that the silence loop
runs `AND A` and `DJNZ` with B = 0, 256 times: B is always 0 there, because the `DJNZ` before it has
just counted it down. Repeating `AND A` costs nothing but time, and time is what the loop is for.

Every one of these T-states is spent by the CPU, which is why the display has to be off. An NMI in the
middle of a wave would stretch it, and the ULA's `WAIT` would stretch it more.

## Part 6: LOAD

Loading is the harder half. The CPU no longer sets the pace; the tape does, and the CPU has to listen
for it. It does so by **sampling**: reading bit 7 of port `$FE` in a loop, again and again, and
counting.

### The plan

is wrong, and otherwise read bytes into memory until `E_LINE`. The ROM does all of that with one byte
reader, `InByte`, and three loops around it:

### The entry

```z80klive
; $0340: LOAD "name"
Load:
    call Name         ; DE = the name, carry set if it is empty
    rl d              ; carry into bit 0 of D, D's bit 7 into carry ...
    rrc d             ; ... and bit 0 back to bit 7:
                      ; D's bit 7 = 1 means "any name will do"
NextProg:
    call InByte       ; read a byte and forget it ...
    jr NextProg       ; ... until a silence (see InByte)
```

**A flag in an address.** The name's address in DE is somewhere in RAM above `$4000`, so its top bit,
bit 7 of D, is always 0, and the ROM borrows it. `RL D` / `RRC D` puts `Name`'s "empty name" carry
into it without disturbing the address. `LOAD ""` sets it, and then every name matches.

**An endless loop, on purpose.** `NextProg` calls `InByte` and throws the byte away, forever. The tape
may be in the middle of some other program, or playing noise; none of it matters until the start of a
program, and a program starts after a long silence. The way out of the loop is therefore not here
but inside `InByte`, which notices the silence. `InByte` follows in the ROM, at `$034C`.

### Reading one byte

`InByte` waits for a burst, measures it, turns it into a bit, and repeats until it has eight:

```z80klive
; $034C: read one byte from tape into C
InByte:
    ld c,$01          ; a marker bit: when it falls out, C is full
WaitBit:
    ld b,$00          ; up to 256 samples of waiting
Wait:
    ld a,$7F
    in a,($FE)        ; read the SPACE row and the tape (VSYNC on)
    out ($FF),a       ; VSYNC off again
    rra               ; bit 0, SPACE, into carry
    jr nc,BreakLoad   ; BREAK pressed
    rla               ; undo the RRA ...
    rla               ; ... and move bit 7, the tape, into carry
    jr c,GetBit       ; sound: a burst begins
    djnz Wait         ; still silent: wait some more
    ; 256 samples without sound: fall into SilenceFound ($035F)
```

While there is sound, `InByte` jumps to `GetBit`, which measures the burst and turns it into a bit
([Measuring a burst](#measuring-a-burst) shows how). Here, we follow the other way out: no sound at
all.

Each round of `Wait` takes 68 T-states, so 256 rounds without sound are 17,408 T-states, **5.4 ms**.
That is far longer than the 1.5 ms silence between bits, and far shorter than the 5 s leader. A
silence that long can mean only one thing: **the tape is between programs**.

Then `InByte` does not return. When `DJNZ` runs out, the code simply carries on with the next
instruction in the ROM, at `$035F`.

### What a silence means

```z80klive
; $035F: InByte has heard a long silence
SilenceFound:
    pop af            ; take InByte's return address off the stack
    cp d              ; A = $03, the return address's high byte;
                      ; D = the name's address ($40 or more), or 0 in the data
    jp nc,$03E5       ; D = 0: the data was cut off, start afresh
    ld h,d
    ld l,e            ; HL = the name we want: read the name
```

`InByte` was called, so its return address is still on the stack, and `POP AF` takes it off: the
call will never return. That address was `$034A` (when `NextProg` called `InByte`), `$0369` (from `InName`,
below) or `$037F` (from `InProg`, below). Its high byte, `$03`, is now in A, and `CP D` compares it
with D:

| Where `InByte` was called from | D | `CP D` | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| `NextProg`, waiting for a program | the name's address, `$40`–`$7F` (bit 7 maybe set) | `$03` < D: carry | Go on to read the name: **a program starts**. |
| `InName`, in the middle of the name | the same | carry | Start reading the name again. |
| `InProg`, in the data | 0 (`InProg` sets it, as you will see) | `$03` ≥ 0: no carry | `JP NC,$03E5`: **the machine starts afresh**. |

So one test, borrowing the high byte of a return address, gives three different behaviours. The
last one is drastic, and deliberate: when the sound stops in the middle of the data, memory from
`$4009` up holds half of the new program over half of the old one, and the system variables may point
anywhere. There is nothing safe to go back to. `$03E5` is the end of the ROM's start-up code: it keeps
`RAMTOP` and initializes everything else, as `NEW` does.

BREAK takes the same decision. `Wait` jumps here when SPACE is pressed:

```z80klive
; $03A2: SPACE pressed during LOAD
BreakLoad:
    ld a,d
    and a             ; D = 0: we are in the data ...
    jr z,$0361        ; ... JP NC,$03E5 (AND cleared carry): start afresh
    rst $08           ; otherwise report D: BREAK
    defb $0C
```

`$0361` is the `JP NC,$03E5` in `SilenceFound`. Before the data starts, BREAK stops the `LOAD` with
report D and your old program intact; once the data has started, there is no old program left, and
BREAK resets the machine.

### The name and the data

After a silence, with HL pointing at the name it wants, `LOAD` reads the name from tape, and then the
data:

```z80klive
; $0366: read and compare the name, then load the data
InName:
    call InByte       ; C = the next name character on tape
    bit 7,d           ; any name will do?
    ld a,c
    jr nz,AnyName
    cp (hl)           ; the character we want?
    jr nz,NextProg    ; no: this is not our program
AnyName:
    inc hl
    rla               ; bit 7: the last character of the name?
    jr nc,InName
    inc (iy+$15)      ; E_LINE + 256: see below
    ld hl,$4009       ; load from VERSN on
InProg:
    ld d,b            ; D = 0 (B is 0 after InByte): we are in the data
    call InByte       ; C = the next byte
    ld (hl),c         ; into memory
    call NextByte     ; $01FC: next address; leave at E_LINE
    jr InProg
```

**The name is compared as it arrives.** The loader keeps no buffer. It compares each character from
tape with the next character of the name it wants, and on the first difference it goes back to
`NextProg`, which reads and throws away bytes until the next silence: the end of that program, the
silence before the next one. So `LOAD "GAME"` skips every program on the tape until it finds `GAME`.
The ZX81 shows you nothing while it skips: you only see the stripes.

**`E_LINE` is moved out of the way.** The data loop stops when the address reaches `E_LINE`, but
`E_LINE` itself is one of the bytes being loaded, at `$4014`–`$4015`. `INC (IY+$15)` adds 256 to the
current value before the data starts, so that the end test cannot trigger while the new value
arrives over it one byte at a time. From the moment both bytes have been loaded, the test uses the
new program's `E_LINE`, and loading stops exactly at the end of the program's data.

**`D` changes its meaning.** `LD D,B` at the start of every data byte sets D to 0 (B is always 0 when
`InByte` returns). From now on, D says "we are in the data", and that is the 0 that
[What a silence means](#what-a-silence-means) tests for: a silence or BREAK now resets the machine.

### Measuring a burst

When `Wait` hears sound, `GetBit` measures how long it lasts:

```z80klive
; $0385: measure one burst; shift its bit into C
GetBit:
    push de
    ld e,$94          ; E counts all samples, down from 148
Restart:
    ld b,$1A          ; B counts silent samples: 26 in a row end the burst
Sample:
    dec e
    in a,($FE)        ; read the tape (the key row A selects does not matter)
    rla               ; bit 7, the tape, into carry
    bit 7,e           ; Z = 0 if E has gone below zero
    ld a,e
    jr c,Restart      ; sound: start counting the silence again
    djnz Sample       ; silence: one more silent sample
    pop de
    jr nz,LongBurst   ; E below zero: 149 samples or more
    cp $56
    jr nc,WaitBit     ; E >= $56, 62 samples or fewer: noise, ignore it
LongBurst:
    ccf               ; the bit: 1 for a long burst, 0 for a short one
    rl c              ; into C; the marker falls out after 8 bits
    jr nc,WaitBit     ; more bits to come
    ret               ; C is a whole byte
```

**Two counters, one test.** B counts **silent samples in a row**: every sample with sound sets it
back to 26, so the loop ends only after 26 silent samples in a row, about 400 µs. That is longer than
the low half of a wave (145 µs) and much shorter than the 1.5 ms silence after a bit, so the loop runs
through the whole burst and stops early in the silence. E counts **all samples**, down from 148.

**The decision**, with *n* the number of samples taken (one round of `Sample` is 51 T-states,
15.7 µs, and *n* includes the 26 silent ones at the end):

| Samples *n* | Sound before the silence | Meaning | How the code tells |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| up to 62 | under about 0.6 ms | **noise**: ignored, wait for the next burst | E ≥ `$56`: `JR NC,WaitBit`, with C unchanged |
| 63 to 148 | about 0.6 to 1.9 ms | a **0 bit** | `CP $56` sets carry; `CCF` makes it 0 |
| 149 or more | over about 1.9 ms | a **1 bit** | E has passed zero, bit 7 is set; carry is clear from `RLA`; `CCF` makes it 1 |

A 0 bit's burst lasts about 1.05 ms from its first edge to its last (4 waves) and a 1 bit's about
2.55 ms (9 waves), so they land near the middle of their windows. That is the margin Part 3 promised:
the 0 burst could shrink by almost half or grow by 80 per cent and still read as 0. A recorder running
a few per cent off speed does not come close.

**Three small tricks:**

- **`BIT 7,E` is a "below zero" test.** E starts at `$94`, which already has bit 7 set; decrementing
  it 26 times or more (the minimum, since B starts at 26) takes it below `$80`, and bit 7 comes back
  only when E passes zero and wraps to `$FF`. The `LD A,E` keeps a copy for the `CP $56` after the
  loop, so the loop does not have to test anything else.

  The test is made **inside** the loop, but its result is used only **after** it, by the `JR NZ`
  that follows `POP DE`. That works because nothing in between touches the Z flag. `LD A,E` and
  `POP DE` change no flags (only `POP AF` would), and neither does **`DJNZ`**, a fact many Z80
  programmers do not know. `DJNZ` decrements B and jumps on its own test of B, not on a flag, and it
  leaves every flag as it was: Zilog's [Z80 CPU User Manual](https://www.zilog.com/docs/z80/um0080.pdf)
  lists "Condition Bits Affected: None" for it. So when the loop ends, Z still holds the result of
  the last `BIT 7,E`.

  The same care keeps the carry flag alive. `RLA` puts the tape bit into carry, and `BIT` leaves
  carry alone (Zilog lists it as "C: Not affected"), so the `JR C` after `BIT 7,E` and `LD A,E` still jumps on
  the tape bit. At the end of the loop, that carry is 0, from the last silent sample. For a long
  burst it survives `DJNZ`, `POP DE` and the taken `JR NZ` to reach `CCF` in `LongBurst`, which makes
  it the 1 bit; for a shorter one, `CP $56` sets carry afresh. One flag register carries two
  results, Z from `BIT` and C from `RLA`, out of a loop whose own counter needs neither.
- **The bit is shifted in with its marker**, just as `OutByte` shifted it out: C starts as 1, and
  `RL C` pushes it out into carry after eight bits.
- **Noise costs nothing.** A short click on the tape is measured, found too short, and forgotten:
  C is left unchanged, and `WaitBit` waits for the next burst.

Note what is *not* here: no checksum, no parity, no retry. If dust on the tape turns one 1 into a 0,
`LOAD` puts the wrong byte into memory and carries on. The burst format makes errors rare, but when
one happens, nothing notices.

## Part 7: Living with the format

The design has some consequences every ZX81 owner learned, sooner or later.

**The old screen comes back.** The display file is in the saved slice of memory, so loading a
program also loads the screen as it was at the moment of `SAVE`: it appears when the display restarts
at the end of the `LOAD`.

**Programs can start themselves.** `NXTLIN` (`$4029`), the number of the line to run next, is one of
the saved system variables. When you type `SAVE "X"` as a command, there is no next line. But if a
program line does the saving, `NXTLIN` holds the line after it, and the tape keeps that. After `LOAD`,
the interpreter takes its next line from `NXTLIN` as it does after every statement, now the loaded
value, and the program carries on running from the line after its `SAVE`. That is how commercial
software started without `RUN`:

```text
9000 SAVE "GAME"
9010 GOTO 1
```

**FAST and SLOW come back too.** `CDFLAG` (`$403B`) holds the display mode, and it is saved. At the
end of `LOAD`, the routine at `$0207` restarts the display only if `CDFLAG` says SLOW, and by then it
is the loaded `CDFLAG`. A program saved in FAST mode loads in FAST mode.

**The size depends on the machine it was saved on.** A 1K ZX81 collapses its display file to as little
as 25 bytes; a 16K one keeps all 793. So the same BASIC program saves longer from a 16K machine.

**Zeros are cheap.** A 0 bit takes 2.55 ms and a 1 bit 4.05 ms, and the ZX81 space character is code
`$00`, so a screen full of spaces saves fast: a full display file of spaces is 768 bytes of eight 0
bits each.

**The tape is not checked.** Without a checksum, a bad load shows up only when the program does
something odd, or when the machine crashes. Owners learned to try again with the volume turned up or
down.

**`LOAD ""` takes the first program.** With an empty name every name matches, so `LOAD ""` loads
whatever program comes next on the tape.

## Part 8: How Klive plays and records a tape

Klive's ZX81 runs the real ROM, so `LOAD` is the ROM's own `LOAD`, sampling bit 7 of port `$FE`. All
the emulator has to provide is the signal on that bit, at the right T-state. It does so in one of two
ways.

### Real-time loading: a synthesized signal

When you load a `.P` file with *Fast Load* off, Klive puts its bytes (with the `$80` name in front,
Part 4) into the core, and the core **generates the tape signal from them** as the emulated time
passes:

| Part of the signal | Klive's timing |
|---|---|
| The leader | 1 second of silence |
| Before every bit | a 1,300 µs silence |
| Each wave | 150 µs of sound, then 150 µs of silence |
| A 0 bit / a 1 bit | 4 waves / 9 waves |
| After the last byte | silence |

These figures come from Thomas Harte's **Clock Signal** emulator, whose tape format Klive follows.
They are rounder than the ROM's own (1.5 ms of silence after each bit, a 155/145 µs wave), yet a
whole bit comes out the same length: 2.5 ms for a 0 and 4.0 ms for a 1. And as Part 6 showed,
the loader does not mind: a 0 burst still measures 1.05 ms and a 1 burst 2.55 ms, in the middle of
their windows. One second of leader is enough, because the loader needs only 5.4 ms of silence to
recognise the start of a program.

The core does not store a list of pulses: a 16K program would be millions of them. It keeps only its
place in the file (the byte, the bit, the wave, and which half of it), and works out the length of
the next pulse when the current one ends. Each instruction's T-states count down the current pulse,
and the level of the pulse is what an `IN` from port `$FE` returns in bit 7.

**The motor.** A real ZX81 has no motor control: the tape plays as long as you hold *Play*. Klive
plays it only while the CPU is inside the ROM's `LOAD` code, `$0340`–`$03C2`. Between commands, or
while you type `LOAD ""`, the tape waits. This "automatic motor" is a convenience a real ZX81 does
not have, and it means no program is lost because the tape started too early.

While a real-time load runs, Klive draws the screen exactly as the ROM's `IN`s and `OUT`s make it, so
you see the same moving stripes as on a television.

### Fast loading: skipping the sound

With *Fast Load* on (the default), Klive does not wait for the sound at all. It **traps** the three
places in the ROM where `LOAD` calls `InByte`, and answers each call at once:

| ROM address | The call | What the trap does |
|---|---|---|
| `$0347` | `NextProg`: wait for a silence | Pretend the silence has come: A = 0, continue at `$0360` (`CP D`), which goes on to the name. |
| `$0366` | `InName`: read a name character | Put the next byte from the file into C, continue at `$0369`. |
| `$037C` | `InProg`: read a data byte | Put the next byte into C, continue at `$037F` (`LD (HL),C`). |

Everything else is the ROM's own code: the name comparison, the `E_LINE` trick, the end test in
`$01FC`, and the restart of the display. A 16K program loads in a fraction of a second, and the
machine ends up exactly as if the tape had played. The trap at `$0347` continues at `$0360` rather
than at `$035F`, because no `CALL` was made, so there is no return address for the `POP AF` to drop.

When you choose *Load and Run*, Klive resets the machine, types `LOAD ""` for you, and waits for the
ROM to finish the command. Then it types `RUN`, unless the program started itself (Part 7).

### Saving: listening to OUT-BYTE

A `SAVE` in Klive writes a `.P` file. The Spectrum cores decode their `SAVE` from the pulses on the
MIC bit; the ZX81's tape signal is VSYNC, which the same ROM also toggles to draw the picture, so
Klive listens one level up, at the byte. The core follows the ROM's `SAVE` (Part 5) through four
addresses:

| ROM address | What the core does |
|---|---|
| `$02F6` | `SAVE`: starts a new file; one stopped by BREAK earlier is forgotten. |
| `$0313` | `LD HL,$4009`: the name is out, the program follows. |
| `$031E` | `OutByte`: records the byte at (HL), the name's or the program's. |
| `$02FF` | The lead-in loop (fast only, below). |

The program's bytes, from `$4009` up to `E_LINE − 1`, are exactly a `.P` file (Part 4). When the last
one is out, the core hands the file to the IDE, which names it after the tape name and writes it to
the saved-files folder. A `SAVE` stopped by BREAK never reaches its last byte, and writes nothing.

With *Fast Load* off, the ROM's own code runs: five seconds of lead-in, every burst on the screen,
the bytes recorded as `OutByte` sends them. With it on, the core skips the lead-in loop and returns
from `OutByte` at once, so the same bytes are written in a fraction of a second. Only the ROM's
`SAVE` is heard: a program with its own tape routine writes nothing.

### Where to read the code

The ZX81 core lives in `src/emu/machines/zx8081/wasm/` of the Klive repository. Its `README.md` has a
section on the tape, and:

- `zx8081/zx8081-tape.c` holds the pulse synthesizer, the automatic motor, the fast-load traps and
  the `SAVE` capture;
- `zx8081/zx8081-ula.c` puts the tape level into bit 7 of port `$FE`, and starts and ends VSYNC on
  `IN` and `OUT`;
- `src/emu/machines/zx8081/ZxPFile.ts` recognises `.P` and `.O` files and adds the `$80` name.

The tape logic is ported from Thomas Harte's **Clock Signal** emulator (MIT licence).

## The tape routines at a glance

| Routine | Address | Its job | Listed in |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Save` | `$02F6` | The `SAVE` command: 5 s of silence, the name, then memory from `$4009` to `E_LINE`. | [The leader and the bytes](#the-leader-and-the-bytes) |
| `OutByte` | `$031E` | Sends one byte, MSB first: 4 or 9 waves per bit, then 1.5 ms of silence. | [One byte, eight bursts](#one-byte-eight-bursts) |
| `Load` | `$0340` | The `LOAD` command: waits for a program, compares its name, loads its data. | [The entry](#the-entry), [The name and the data](#the-name-and-the-data) |
| `InByte` | `$034C` | Reads one byte; on a 5.4 ms silence, leaves to `SilenceFound` instead. | [Reading one byte](#reading-one-byte) |
| `SilenceFound` | `$035F` | Decides what a silence means: a program starts, a name restarts, or the data was cut off. | [What a silence means](#what-a-silence-means) |
| `GetBit` | `$0385` | Measures one burst and turns it into a bit, or ignores it as noise. | [Measuring a burst](#measuring-a-burst) |
| `BreakLoad` | `$03A2` | BREAK during `LOAD`: report D, or a fresh start once the data has begun. | [What a silence means](#what-a-silence-means) |
| `Name` | `$03A8` | Evaluates the name, stops the display, and sets bit 7 of the last character. | [The name](#the-name) |

## Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What hardware does the tape use? | One output level and one input bit. `IN` from port `$FE` drives the output low (it starts VSYNC), any `OUT` drives it high; bit 7 of port `$FE` is the input. |
| Why does the screen show stripes? | The display is off, and the ULA's video pin is also the tape output. |
| How is a bit recorded? | A burst of 4 waves (0) or 9 waves (1) of about 300 µs each, followed by about 1.5 ms of silence. |
| Why bursts? | The loader only asks "is there sound?" and "for how long?", which survive the head's spikes, blurring, speed errors, volume and polarity. |
| What does a recording hold? | About 5 s of silence, the name with bit 7 set on its last character, then memory from `$4009` to `E_LINE − 1`. |
| What is a `.P` file? | Exactly that memory, without the name. |
| How does `LOAD` find a program? | A silence of 5.4 ms means "between programs"; it then compares the name as it arrives, and skips programs that do not match. |
| How does it tell a 0 from a 1? | It counts 15.7 µs samples until 26 silent ones in a row: up to 62 is noise, up to 148 is a 0, more is a 1. |
| How does it know where the data ends? | At `E_LINE`, which is itself loaded, as the bytes at `$4014`–`$4015`. |
| What if the tape stops mid-program? | The machine starts afresh, as after `NEW`: half a program cannot be kept. |
| Is a load checked for errors? | No. There is no checksum. |
| How does Klive load a tape? | In real time, by synthesizing the signal from the file into bit 7 of port `$FE`; or at once, by trapping the ROM's three calls of `InByte`. |
| How does Klive record a `SAVE`? | It follows the ROM's `SAVE` and records each byte as `OutByte` starts to send it; the bytes from `$4009` are written out as a `.P` file. With *Fast Load* on, it skips the leader and the bursts. |
