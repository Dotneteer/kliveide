# DMA: One Bus, Two Masters

Copy 16K with `LDIR` on a Next at 3.5 MHz and the CPU spends 344,059 T-states on it, almost five frames,
reading a byte, writing it, and counting, over and over. Ask the DMA to do the same job and it is done in
98,304 T-states, less than a frame and a half. The DMA is not a faster CPU, and it does not run beside the
CPU either. So where do the other three and a half frames go?

I think the DMA is the most underused piece of the Next. Sprite patterns, a Layer 2 screen to clear, a sample
to play while the game runs, a sector to pull from the SD card: all of them are "move these bytes from here to
there", and the DMA does nothing else, but it does it at the bus's own speed. Once you know how it shares the
bus with the CPU, you can choose between a copy that stops the world for a moment and a trickle that the CPU
hardly notices.

You should know two chapters first. [Memory, Ports and NextRegs: How the Z80N Reaches the
Machine](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-memory-io/) explains the MMU and the port decoder, which
the DMA uses exactly as the CPU does. [Interrupts: Fourteen Voices, Two
Wires](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/) explains hardware IM2 mode, which lets an
interrupt break into a transfer.

## The road ahead

We start with the bus itself: how the DMA borrows it from the CPU, and why everything else in the machine
cannot tell who is driving it. Then come the two ports, `$6B` and `$0B`, and the one difference between their
dialects, the byte count. Next is the way you talk to the DMA, a stream of base bytes and their followers, and
the registers one by one: the two ports A and B, the operating mode, the flags and the commands. The heart of
the chapter is the timing: six T-states a byte, the prescaler that spaces the bytes out, and burst mode, which
gives the CPU its bus back between them; if you read only one part, read those three sections. After that we
look at auto restart, reading the DMA back, interrupts, wait states and resets.

## A second master on the bus

The Z80 has a pair of pins for exactly this situation. **`/BUSRQ`**, bus request, is an input: a device pulls
it low when it wants the bus. **`/BUSAK`**, bus acknowledge, is the CPU's answer: at the end of its current
machine cycle the CPU stops, lets go of its address, data and control lines, and pulls `/BUSAK` low. From then
on the device owns the bus, and the CPU waits, frozen in the middle of its instruction, until `/BUSRQ` goes
high again.

The Next's DMA is such a device, and I call it **the courier**, as the DAC chapter does: you hand it the parcel
and the address once, and it delivers every byte without asking you again. Inside the FPGA there is no
separate bus for it to use. The courier's address, data and control lines go through a switch, and while
`/BUSAK` is low, the switch puts them where the CPU's lines would be:

Everything behind the switch sees the DMA as if it were the CPU, and that is the reason it is so useful:

- **Memory goes through the MMU.** The DMA's addresses are 16-bit Z80 addresses, mapped by NextRegs `$50`–`$57`
  like the CPU's. To reach any bank, you page it in first, as you would for the CPU. The ROM is read-only to the
  DMA too, and the Layer 2 write mapping of port `$123B` catches its writes the same way.
- **I/O goes through the port decoder.** A DMA write to port `$5B` loads a sprite pattern byte, a write to
  `$DF` sets a DAC, a write to `$253B` writes the selected NextReg. Any port the CPU can write, the DMA can.
- **Two exceptions.** The DMA's own ports do not answer while it holds the bus, so it cannot reprogram
  itself. And the I/O traps of the **I/O Traps register, NextReg `$D8`**, do not fire for its accesses.

Notice what this means for the CPU: it does not run while the courier works. The DMA does not save time by
working in parallel; it saves time by moving a byte in 6 T-states where `LDIR` needs 21. That answers the
opening puzzle. The three and a half frames were never spent at all.

Two details of the handover matter in practice. The CPU only lets go at the end of a machine cycle, so when an
`OUT` starts the DMA, the CPU still fetches the next instruction's opcode before the DMA takes over. And giving
the bus back takes a few clocks each way, so a transfer of a few bytes costs a few T-states more than its bytes
alone.

## Two ports, two dialects

The DMA answers on two ports, **`$6B`** and **`$0B`**, and only the low byte of the port address is decoded,
so `OTIR`, which changes `B` as it goes, sends a whole table to either port. The port you use is also the
DMA's dialect: every read or write of `$6B` puts it into **zxnDMA mode**, the Next's own, and every read or
write of `$0B` into **Z80 DMA mode**, which copies the Zilog chip. The mode stays until you touch the other
port, and a reset brings back zxnDMA mode.

The two dialects differ in one thing only, where the byte counter starts. The counter counts the bytes moved,
and the transfer goes on as long as the counter is below the block length. It is checked after each byte,
never before the first, and that makes the counts come out like this:

- **zxnDMA mode**: the counter starts at 0, so a block length of *N* moves exactly *N* bytes. That is what you
  would expect, and it is the mode I recommend for every new program.
- **Z80 DMA mode**: the counter starts at `$FFFF`, so a block length of *N* moves *N* + 1 bytes, as on the
  Zilog chip. Use it for code written for a real Z80 DMA.
- **A block length of 0 moves one byte** in both modes, never none. The largest block is `$FFFF` bytes in
  zxnDMA mode and 65,536 bytes, the whole address space, in Z80 DMA mode.

Each port can be switched off: bit 5 of NextReg `$82` enables `$6B`, and bit 1 of NextReg `$85` enables `$0B`.
Both are on after a reset.

## Base bytes and their followers

The DMA has no register select port. You write a stream of bytes, and the DMA works out from each byte what
it is. A **base byte** names a register group, WR0 to WR6, by a pattern of fixed bits, and some of its other
bits announce **followers**, the parameter bytes that must come next, in a fixed order. When the followers
have arrived, the next byte is a base byte again. This is how the DMA tells the groups apart:

Two consequences are worth knowing. A byte that matches no pattern, such as `$86` (bit 7 set, bits 1–0 = 10,
but not WR5's pattern), is simply ignored. And a byte the DMA expects as a follower is taken as a follower
whatever its value, so a sequence that announces three followers and sends two swallows your next base byte.
My advice: write each group in one place, with its followers next to it, and never trust the DMA's state
between two programs: start every setup with a disable, `$83`.

## Port A and port B

Like the Zilog chip, the DMA does not speak of a source and a destination, but of **port A** and **port B**.
Each is an address with its own settings, and a direction bit says which way the bytes flow. That sounds
roundabout, but it lets you set up a buffer and a device once and run the transfer either way by changing one
bit.

**WR0** sets the direction, port A's starting address and the block length:

```text
WR0:  0 L L A A D t t
  t t = not 00 (01 for a transfer)
  D   = direction: 1 = A to B, 0 = B to A
  A A = port A address high / low follows
  L L = block length high / low follows
followers, in this order:
  port A low, port A high, length low, length high
```

Only the followers whose bits are set are sent, but always in that order: `$6D`, with bits 6, 5 and 3 set,
expects port A's low byte, then the length's low byte, then the length's high byte. The two bits that select
a transfer, a search, or both on the Zilog chip are only part of the pattern here. **There is no search**:
every WR0 is a transfer.

**WR1** describes port A and **WR2** port B, in the same layout:

```text
WR1:  0 T M M I 1 0 0      WR2:  0 T M M I 0 0 0
      I   = 1: an I/O port, 0: memory
      M M = after each byte: 00 down, 01 up, 10 or 11 fixed
      T   = a timing byte follows
timing byte:  x x P x x x c c
      c c = cycle length: 00 4, 01 3, 10 2, 11 4 clocks
      P   = one more byte follows: WR2's prescaler
```

The address modes cover every job I can think of. A copy steps both addresses up. A fill keeps a one-byte
source **fixed** and steps the destination; a device such as the sprite pattern port or a DAC keeps its port
fixed. A move between overlapping buffers steps both addresses **down**, from the end, for the same reason
`LDDR` exists. The **cycle length** is how many clocks the DMA spends on each read and each write of that
port: three after a reset. WR1's timing byte can announce a prescaler byte too, but port A has no prescaler:
that byte is accepted and thrown away. Port B's is the prescaler, which gets a section of its own below.

## The mode and port B's address

**WR4** chooses the operating mode and sets port B's starting address:

```text
WR4:  1 m m i B B 0 1
  m m = 00 byte, 01 continuous, 10 burst
  B B = port B address high / low follows
  i   = interrupt control byte follows
followers, in this order:
  port B low, port B high
```

Two modes really exist. In **continuous mode** the DMA keeps the bus from the first byte to the last. In
**burst mode** it gives the bus back while the prescaler makes it wait, and only then. **Byte mode** is
accepted, but it behaves exactly like continuous mode, and so does burst mode without a prescaler.

Leave bit 4 alone. The DMA raises no interrupts, and its handling of this bit has two traps. With port B's
address bytes, the interrupt control byte you send after them is decoded as a new base byte. Without them,
the DMA waits for an interrupt control byte it never takes: it stays deaf to every byte you write until the
machine is reset. A base byte such as `$91` is enough to lock it.

## Enables and flags

**WR3** is mostly the Zilog chip's interrupt and search register, and almost none of it is here:

```text
WR3:  1 E x H K x 0 0
  E = enable the DMA (like command $87)
  K = a mask byte follows
  H = a match byte follows (after the mask)
```

Bit 6 starts the DMA, exactly like the enable command. The mask and match bytes are taken and dropped, and
the interrupt and stop-on-match bits do nothing.

**WR5** is a single byte, `1 0 R C x 0 1 0`. Bit 5, **R**, is **auto restart**, which has a section of its own
below; bit 4, **C**, the Zilog chip's CE/WAIT setting, is stored but changes nothing on the Next. So the WR5
you write is `$82`, or `$A2` for auto restart.

## Commands

**WR6** is the command register: any base byte with bit 7 set and bits 1–0 = 11 is a command, and the DMA
acts on it at once. These are the ones that do something:

- **`$C3`, reset.** Stops any transfer, resets the status byte, sets both cycle lengths back to three clocks,
  clears the prescaler and auto restart. It does *not* touch the start addresses, the block length, the
  direction, the address modes, the operating mode or the read mask. One `$C3` does all of this; you do not
  need a series of them, as the Zilog chip wanted.
- **`$C7` and `$CB`, reset port A's or port B's timing** to three clocks.
- **`$CF`, load.** Copies the start addresses into the DMA's working source and destination, port A to the
  source for A to B, port B for B to A; sets the counter to its start, 0 or `$FFFF` by the mode; and clears
  the end-of-block flag.
- **`$D3`, continue.** Sets the counter to its start and clears the end-of-block flag, but keeps the
  addresses where they are, so the next block follows on from the last.
- **`$87`, enable.** Starts the transfer. **`$83`, disable**, stops it where it is; a later `$87` goes on from
  there.
- **`$BB`, read mask follows; `$A7`, initialise the read sequence; `$BF`, read the status byte next; `$8B`,
  reinitialise the status byte.** They belong to reading the DMA back, a section of its own below.

The Zilog chip's interrupt commands, `$AF`, `$AB`, `$A3` and `$B7`, and force ready, `$B3`, are accepted and
do nothing.

Here is the trap I would warn every newcomer about: **the addresses you write are not the addresses the DMA
uses.** WR0 and WR4 fill the start registers; only `$CF` copies them into the working addresses. Enable
without a load, and the DMA carries on from wherever its last transfer stopped. After a finished block that
means one more byte, from the address just past its end, because the counter is checked only after a byte.
So the last two bytes of every setup are `$CF`, `$87`, in that order.

## One byte, six T-states

A transfer is a loop of two bus cycles: the DMA **reads** a byte from the source and **writes** it to the
destination. Each cycle lasts the cycle length of its port, three clocks after a reset, so a byte takes **six
T-states**. During the write's first clock the DMA counts the byte and steps both addresses, and after the
write it decides whether there is another byte to move.

Put the numbers together for a 16K copy. `LDIR` takes 21 T-states for every byte but the last, which takes 16:
344,059 T-states. The DMA takes 6 × 16,384 = **98,304 T-states**, plus a few for the handover, 3.5 times
faster. With two-clock cycles, a timing byte of `$02` for both ports, a byte takes **four T-states** and
the copy 65,536. Four-clock cycles make it eight.

The clocks are the CPU's clocks, so at 28 MHz the same six clocks take an eighth of the time. Three things can
stretch a byte, all of them wait states the CPU would get too:

- **Contention.** At 3.5 MHz the ULA holds the CPU's clock while it reads the screen, and the DMA runs on that
  clock. A DMA byte that touches contended memory in the display area waits, exactly as the CPU would.
  Contention disappears at 7 MHz and above, and when NextReg `$08` bit 6 switches it off.
- **The SD card.** The data port of the SD card, `$EB`, shifts each byte out one bit at a time, and it holds
  the DMA's next access to it until the last byte has finished, 16 CPU clocks. So a DMA from `$EB` to memory
  reads a sector at the speed of the card's serial link, without a single byte lost.
- **28 MHz memory.** At 28 MHz a read from the main memory gets a wait state, the DMA's as well as the
  CPU's.

If you ask me, the two-clock cycle is the one to use for memory to memory copies, and the three-clock cycle
for anything that talks to a device. I have no evidence that a device minds; I simply like the margin.

## The prescaler: spacing the bytes out

For a copy, faster is better. For a sample, the rate *is* the sound: each byte must arrive at the DAC at
exactly the right moment, not as soon as possible. The **prescaler**, WR2's third byte, does that. It is a
Next addition; the Zilog chip has nothing like it.

The prescaler works with a timer that counts **28 MHz time**: it goes up by 8 for each CPU clock at 3.5 MHz,
4 at 7 MHz, 2 at 14 MHz and 1 at 28 MHz, and it starts again from zero at the start of every byte. After the
write, the DMA waits until the timer has reached 32 times the prescaler, *P*. So the wait ends **32 × *P*
master clocks** after the byte began, at every CPU speed, and that is where the familiar rate comes from: 28
MHz ÷ 32 ÷ *P*, **875 kHz ÷ *P***.

That is the nominal rate, and the real one is a little lower. When the wait ends, the DMA needs two more
clocks to get to the next read, and the first clock of a byte is spent before the timer starts counting. A
byte every **4 × *P* + 3 T-states** at 3.5 MHz, then, and every 32 × *P* + 3 clocks at 28 MHz:

- **Prescaler 100 at 3.5 MHz**: a byte every 403 T-states, 8,685 bytes a second, where the formula promises
  8,750.
- **Prescaler 100 at 28 MHz**: a byte every 3,203 clocks, 8,742 bytes a second. The faster the CPU, the closer
  to the formula, because the three clocks are shorter.

The rule holds whenever the wait is longer than the byte itself. At 3.5 MHz a prescaler of 1 is not, and
changes nothing; 0 switches the prescaler off at every speed. Notice too that the DMA waits after the last byte as well,
before it calls the block finished. I find the 0.7 % of a slower rate harmless for music, but if you tune
samples to a reference pitch, it is good to know where it goes.

In continuous mode the DMA **keeps the bus while it waits**. The CPU is frozen for the whole block, and a
prescaled block takes far longer than an unpaced one, so this is almost never what you want. With a
prescaler, use burst mode.

## Burst mode: the bus goes back between bytes

In burst mode the courier hands the bus back while it waits. For each byte:

1. The DMA reads and writes the byte, six T-states.
2. On the first clock of its wait it lets go of `/BUSRQ`, and a couple of clocks later the CPU picks up its
   instruction where it was frozen.
3. The CPU runs while the timer counts.
4. When the wait ends, the DMA pulls `/BUSRQ` again, and the CPU stops at the end of its current machine
   cycle.
5. The DMA takes the bus and moves the next byte.

So burst mode costs a little: each byte comes a few T-states later than the prescaler alone would say, and by
an amount that depends on what the CPU was doing, so the bytes jitter by a T-state or two. In return the CPU
gets almost all its time back. At prescaler 100 the courier holds the bus for about a dozen T-states of every

copies, burst mode with a prescaler for anything paced.

Let's play a sample. This program plays 2,048 bytes from `$C000` to the Specdrum DAC on port `$DF` at
prescaler 55, a byte about every 226 T-states, about 15.5 kHz, while the CPU goes back to its work:

```z80klive
        nextreg $08,$18        ; DACs and speaker on
        ld hl,Prog
        ld bc,$106b            ; B = 16 bytes, C = port
        otir
        ret                    ; the sample plays on
Prog:   .defb $83              ; disable
        .defb $7d,$00,$c0      ; WR0: A to B, port A $C000
        .defb $00,$08          ; ... length 2,048
        .defb $14              ; WR1: A is memory, upwards
        .defb $68,$21,55       ; WR2: B is I/O, fixed; P = 55
        .defb $cd,$df,$00      ; WR4: burst, port B $00DF
        .defb $82              ; WR5: no auto restart
        .defb $cf,$87          ; load, enable
```

How it works:

- `OTIR` sends the 16 bytes of `Prog` to port `$6B`, which also puts the DMA in zxnDMA mode, so the block
  length 2,048 means 2,048 bytes.
- WR2, `$68`, makes port B a fixed I/O port and announces a timing byte; the timing byte `$21` keeps three-clock
  cycles and announces the prescaler, 55.
- WR4, `$CD`, selects burst mode and announces port B's address, `$00DF`; only its low byte matters to the
  port decoder.
- `$CF` loads the addresses and `$87` starts the transfer. The CPU fetches its next opcode, the first byte of
  `RET`, before the first byte moves; then it carries on between the samples.

## Auto restart

With WR5 bit 5 set, the DMA does not stop at the end of a block. It sets the end-of-block flag, copies the
start addresses into its working addresses again, starts its counter again and moves the first byte of the
next round, keeping the bus if it had it. A looped sample, a ring buffer streamed to a port, a waveform that
never ends: that is what auto restart is for, and the DAC chapter's sine wave runs on it.

It has one dangerous corner. In continuous mode, with a prescaler or without, an auto-restarting DMA **never
gives the bus back**: the CPU stops for good, and it cannot even write the `$83` that would stop the DMA, because the
DMA's port does not answer while the DMA holds the bus. Only a reset, or an interrupt the DMA has been told to
step aside for, gets the CPU running again; in the latter case the interrupt's handler can write the `$83`.
Pair auto restart with burst mode and a prescaler, and let me warn you, do it every time.

## Reading the DMA back

Read the DMA's port, and you get the next entry of its **read sequence**: the status byte, the counter's low
and high bytes, port A's low and high address bytes, and port B's, seven entries in this order. Port A and B
are the working addresses, where the next byte comes from and goes to, under their port names whatever the
direction. The **read mask**, the byte after a `$BB` command, chooses the entries: bit 0 the status, bit 1 and
2 the counter, bits 3 and 4 port A, bits 5 and 6 port B. Each read gives the next enabled entry, and after
the last one the sequence starts again. The mask is `$7F` after a reset, all seven, so a program that reads
the port repeatedly without a command sees all seven in turn. `$A7` starts the sequence again from its first
enabled entry, and `$BF` makes the next read the status, whatever the mask.

The **status byte** has the form `00E1101T`:

- **E**, bit 5, is **0 once the end of a block has been reached**, and 1 before that. A load, a continue, a
  reset or `$8B` sets it back to 1.
- **T**, bit 0, is 1 when a byte has been moved in the transfer under way. It is cleared whenever the DMA is
  idle, so it reads 1 only while a transfer is running, never after it has finished.
- So `$3A` means idle and nothing done yet, `$3B` a transfer under way, and `$1A` finished.

There is a catch: the CPU can read the DMA only while it has the bus. During a continuous transfer it does not,
so your main program never sees `$3B`, only `$1A` at the end; an interrupt handler that runs while the DMA steps
aside is the exception. In burst mode, polling works:

```z80klive
Wait:   ld a,$bf               ; read the status next
        out ($6b),a
        in a,($6b)
        bit 5,a                ; end of block reached?
        jr nz,Wait
```

## Stepping aside for interrupts

The interrupts chapter's section [The DMA steps
aside](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-interrupts/#the-dma-steps-aside) explains how the **DMA INT
EN 0, 1 and 2 registers, NextRegs `$CC`, `$CD` and `$CE`**, let chosen interrupt sources break into a
transfer in hardware IM2 mode. Seen from the DMA:

- After each byte, if such a source is requesting or in service, the DMA lets go of the bus and waits until
  it is not. The CPU runs its instruction, takes the interrupt, runs the handler, and the transfer goes on
  after the `RETI`. Bit 7 of NextReg `$CC` does the same for the NMI.
- Interrupts are taken only at the end of an instruction, so every time the DMA steps aside, the interrupted
  program also makes one instruction of progress.
- Without these enables, or in pulse mode, the interrupt waits for the end of the transfer. A pulse-mode
  interrupt that comes and goes while the DMA holds the bus is lost, so a continuous transfer longer than a
  frame costs you a frame interrupt. A transfer of 16K at six T-states a byte is already nearly one and a
  half frames long.

The DMA itself raises no interrupt when a block ends. If your program must know, it polls the status byte,
or it sets up a CTC channel to ring when the block should be done.

## Resets

A reset, hard or soft, puts the DMA into zxnDMA mode and continuous mode, idle, with three-clock cycles, no
prescaler, no auto restart, the read mask `$7F` and the status `$3A`, and frees a DMA that WR4's bit 4 left
deaf. It does not clear the start addresses, the block length, the direction or the address modes. The `$C3`
command is a smaller reset still: it also keeps the operating mode and the read mask. So the
advice of the interrupts chapter goes for the courier too: write every register a transfer needs, every time.

## What to take home

The DMA, the courier, borrows the CPU's bus with `/BUSRQ` and `/BUSAK`; while it holds the bus the CPU is
frozen, and the rest of the machine sees the DMA as if it were the CPU, MMU, ROM, Layer 2 mapping and port
decoder included. It does not work beside the CPU; it simply moves a byte in 6 T-states where `LDIR` needs 21,
4 with two-clock cycles.

It answers on two ports: `$6B`, zxnDMA mode, moves *N* bytes for a block length of *N*, and `$0B`, Z80 DMA
mode, moves *N* + 1; a length of 0 moves one byte in both. You program it with base bytes, WR0 to WR6, each
recognised by its fixed bits and followed by the bytes it announces: WR0 for the direction, port A and the
length, WR1 and WR2 for the ports' memory or I/O, address step and cycle length, WR4 for the mode and port B,
WR5 for auto restart, WR6 for the commands, of which `$CF`, load, and `$87`, enable, end every setup. There is
no search, no interrupt and no mask or match, byte mode is continuous mode, and WR4's bit 4 is a trap.

The prescaler, WR2's third byte, counts 28 MHz time, so its 875 kHz ÷ *P* rate holds at every CPU speed, and
the exact period is 4 × *P* + 3 T-states at 3.5 MHz. In continuous mode the DMA keeps the bus while it waits;
in burst mode it gives the bus back and returns at the end of the CPU's machine cycle, so the CPU keeps nearly
all its time. Auto restart loops a block, and in continuous mode it never lets go.

The status byte, `00E1101T`, and the read sequence of counter and addresses can be read only while the CPU has
the bus. Interrupts enabled in NextRegs `$CC`–`$CE` make the DMA step aside in hardware IM2 mode, contention
and the SD card's port slow it down like the CPU, and a reset sets it back to zxnDMA mode, continuous and
three-clock cycles. Once you know who holds the bus and when, the courier is the fastest pair of hands in the
machine.
