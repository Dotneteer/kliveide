# HT-1080Z: How the Screen Is Drawn

The HT-1080Z's picture comes from a chain of counters that runs on its own, independently of the
program. Unlike the ZX81, the CPU does not draw the screen. Unlike the ZX Spectrum, the video
circuit never stops the CPU. The two simply share one clock and one block of memory, and that
sharing has visible consequences: a program that writes the screen while it is being drawn leaves
short black dashes in the picture, and a character changed in the middle of a frame can show half
old and half new.

This article explains the video circuit from the clock up, then shows how Klive reproduces it. You
need to know the Z80's memory cycle (T-states, MREQ). The facts come from the circuit diagrams of
the HT-1080Z/64's video board, the technical manual of the EACA Genie (the HT-1080Z's close
relative), and measurements on Klive's emulator; the few points that are inferred rather than
read off a diagram are marked as such.

## One T-state is one character

Everything follows from one fact: **the CPU's clock and the video's character clock are the same
frequency**. A crystal of about 10.66 MHz drives the dot clock, the rate at which dots leave the
video circuit. Divided by six, it gives both the character clock (one character is 6 dots wide)
and the Z80's clock:

| | Series I and II | HT-1080Z/64 |
|---|---|---|
| Crystal (dot clock) | 10.644 MHz | 10.6645 MHz |
| CPU clock = character clock | 1,774,000 Hz | 1,777,417 Hz |
| One T-state = one character | 563.7 ns | 562.6 ns |

So while the CPU executes a 4-T-state `NOP`, the beam draws four characters. There are **no wait
states and no interrupts**: the Z80's INT line is not connected, and the CPU cannot read where the
beam is. A program cannot synchronise itself to the picture. The raster runs freely under it.

## The counter chain

The video circuit is a chain of counters fed by the dot clock. Each stage divides the one before it:

| Stage | Divides by | Gives |
|---|---|---|
| Dots within a character | 6 | the character clock (and the CPU clock) |
| Characters within a line | 112 | the column, 0-111; 0-63 are shown |
| Scan lines within a character row | 12 | the row of the glyph to show |
| Character rows within a frame | 26 | the text row, 0-25; 0-15 are shown |

A line is therefore **112 T-states**: 64 characters of picture and 48 of horizontal blanking, during
which the television's beam flies back to the left edge. A frame is 26 rows of 12 scan lines,
**312 lines**: 192 of picture (16 rows of 12) and 120 of vertical blanking.

```text
frame = 112 × 312 = 34,944 T-states
        50.77 frames a second on Series I and II, 50.86 on the /64
```

The picture is not interlaced: every frame draws the same 312 lines. The sync pulses that tell the
television where a line and a frame begin come from the counters too, through small analog delays
that the trimmers on the board adjust. That is how a technician centred the picture, and why the
size of the border is not a fact of the machine. Klive draws a fixed black border of 72 dots on
each side and 48 lines above and below: the picture is 528 × 288 dots, the same share of the
television's line as the Spectrum's border.

## One line, character by character

Within a visible line the counter's column runs from 0 to 111, one step per T-state. For the first
64 steps it addresses the video memory (3C00h-3FFFh, 64 bytes a row):

| T-state in the line | What happens |
|---|---|
| *n* (0-63) | The video memory is **read** at column *n* of the current row |
| *n* + 1 | The byte is **latched**, and its 6 dots are looked up |
| *n* + 2 | The 6 dots **leave** the shift register, one per dot clock |
| 64-111 | Blanking: the counter keeps counting, but nothing is shown |

So column *n* is drawn two character times after it is read: the visible window is T-states 2 to
65 of each of lines 0 to 191. The row is line ÷ 12, and line mod 12 picks which of the glyph's 12
scan lines to draw.

## What the byte becomes

Each byte of video memory is a character or a block of graphics, chosen by bit 7.

**Characters** (bit 7 clear). Bits 0-6 and the scan line address the **character ROM**: 128 glyphs
of 16 bytes, 12 of which are used. Bits 7 to 3 of the ROM byte are the 5 dots, bit 7 the leftmost,
and the 6th dot is always dark: that is the gap between characters. The character ROMs differ by
model:

- **Series I** has the TRS-80's character set.
- **Series II** puts accented capitals and small letters in place of a few symbols: É at 40h
  (`@`), Ö Á Ü at 5Ch-5Eh, and é ö á ü among the small letters.
- **The /64** has 32 more glyphs at 00h-1Fh: all the Hungarian accented letters, capitals and small,
  and Greek and mathematical signs (λ π √ μ α γ ∫ β ω Ω Σ Ψ ∞ ■). Its capitals' accents sit in the
  glyph's top three scan lines, so the /64 uses all 12.

On Series I and II, BASIC never stores codes 40h-7Fh in video memory: its display routine moves
every character 40h-7Fh down into 00h-1Fh before it writes it (`READY` is stored as 12h 05h 01h 04h
19h). Those codes are drawn with the glyphs of 40h-5Fh. The /64's BASIC does not fold them, and its
00h-1Fh show their own glyphs.

**Block graphics** (bit 7 set). The cell is a 2 × 3 grid of blocks, each 3 dots wide and 4 scan
lines tall. Bits 0 and 1 light the top pair, bits 2 and 3 the middle pair, bits 4 and 5 the bottom
pair; the even bit is the left block. Bit 6 is ignored. BASIC's `SET`, `RESET` and `POINT` work on
this 128 × 48 grid.

## When the CPU touches video memory

The video memory has one set of address lines. While the video circuit reads it, the CPU cannot;
the designers did not make the CPU wait, they gave it priority. When the CPU addresses
3C00h-3FFFh, a signal called VID switches the memory's address lines over to the CPU **and clears
the video latch**. Every character whose latch moment falls while VID is active is latched as zero,
and a zero shows as blank: **a short black dash** on the scan line being drawn.

How long is VID active? It follows the Z80's MREQ signal, which goes active in the middle of a memory
cycle's first T-state and stays active until the middle of its third. That covers the latch moments
at the start of the second and third T-states: two characters, about 12 dots. (This is inferred from
the Z80's timing and the circuit; it has not been measured on a real machine.) In the column
numbering above, an access whose first T-state is at column *x* blanks the reads at *x* and *x* + 1,
so the dash is drawn at slots *x* + 2 and *x* + 3.

The dashes are what a real HT-1080Z shows while a program draws: a `LDIR` into video memory leaves
a trail of them, and a program that keeps reading the screen makes it flicker. Klive draws them by
default. **Machine › Show Video RAM Access Dash** turns them off.

The second effect is **tearing**. The display reads column *n* at T-state *n* of the line, and a CPU
write lands at its own T-state. If the write comes after the beam has read that cell on this scan
line, the change shows from the next scan line on. Changing a character while the beam draws its
row shows the top of the old character and the bottom of the new one, for one frame.

## VIDEO CUT and PAGE

The **VIDEO CUT** switch halves the column clock. Each character then lasts two character times and
is drawn 12 dots wide, so 32 characters fill the same window. The line and frame do not change
(the television still needs them), and neither does the CPU's timing. The **PAGE** key chooses which
32 columns of each 64-byte row are shown: the left half or the right.

With VIDEO CUT on, the slot the beam is at shows the left or right half of its column, the column
being PAGE × 32 + (slot − 2) ÷ 2, read two slots earlier.

## How Klive draws it

Klive's HT-1080Z core draws the picture the way the hardware does, against the CPU's clock, but it
does not draw it one T-state at a time. The core keeps one number: **how far the picture has been
drawn**, as a slot of the frame. Slot *s* is frame T-state *s*. It draws up to the CPU's current
T-state only when it must:

- **before the CPU touches video memory**. The memory hook sees every read and write to 3C00h-3FFFh,
  instruction fetches included. It draws up to the access's first T-state, places the dash over the
  next two reads, and lets the access happen. Reads after it see the new byte, which is the tearing;
- **before VIDEO CUT, PAGE or the dash setting changes**, so the rest of the frame is drawn the new
  way;
- **at the end of the frame**, for everything the program left alone.

A frame in which the program never touches video memory is drawn in one pass at its end, so
emulating the beam costs almost nothing, yet a program that races the beam sees exactly what the
hardware shows.

a measurement on a real machine would change only those.

<Callout type="info">
The CPU and the video share one crystal on Series I and II. The /64 divides the same crystal with two
separate counters, so the CPU and the picture are in a fixed phase that may differ by up to five
dots. At character resolution this makes no difference, and Klive does not model it.
</Callout>

## Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| Clock | crystal ÷ 6: one CPU T-state is one character |
| Line | 112 T-states: 64 shown (slots 2-65), 48 blanking |
| Frame | 312 lines (192 shown) = 34,944 T-states, not interlaced |
| Screen | 64 × 16 characters of 6 × 12 dots; 128 × 48 blocks |
| Character | bits 7-3 of the character ROM, 12 rows; codes 00h-1Fh as 40h-5Fh on Series I/II |
| CPU access to 3C00h-3FFFh | never delayed; blanks two characters at the beam |
| VIDEO CUT, PAGE | 32 columns, 12 dots each; the left or right half of each row |
