# Wi-Fi: A Telegraph Clerk on a Serial Line

WPA2 encryption, radio packets, TCP with its timers and retransmissions, DNS lookups: a modern network
connection is a lot of software, and a Z80 running at 3.5 MHz has 64K to put it in. So how does a Next
fetch a web page? The answer made me smile the first time I understood it: the Next doesn't. It hires
somebody else to do it, and talks to him in plain text.

Knowing how that arrangement works decides whether your network code is pleasant or haunted. Anything on
the Next that touches the Internet, from a chat client to a file sync tool, goes through the one narrow
door this chapter describes. The door has rules, it has a speed limit, and it has one nasty surprise about
what "reliable" means.

The previous chapter, [UARTs: Two Serial Ports Behind One
Door](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-uart/), explains the serial port itself: ports, baud rate,
buffers and status bits. I lean on it throughout and will not repeat it.

## The road ahead

We begin with the Wi-Fi module, which turns out to be a whole computer, and with the handful of wires that
tie it to the Next. Then we see how the module starts and how a program can reset it. After that comes the
language the two sides speak: first the commands, then joining a network, then a real conversation with a
server. Telling a server's data from the module's own chatter takes a small parser, which we write in Z80.
The idea I most want you to take away is in the section on the promise that stops at the module, so read
that one even if you skim the rest. We finish with how fast the link can go and with the differences
between firmware versions.

<Callout type="info">
The Next's side is described for **core 3.02.01**, with a master clock of **28 MHz** and the serial line at
**115,200 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, one stop bit**. The module is not part of the Next's own design: what
it answers depends on the firmware loaded into it, and the exchanges shown here are those of Espressif's
standard AT firmware.
</Callout>

## A computer for a peripheral

The Wi-Fi hardware is an **ESP-01**, a small board that pushes onto a header behind the VGA port, with no
soldering. On it are two things that matter: an **ESP8266** chip from Espressif and a flash memory holding
its firmware. The chip contains the radio, for 802.11 b/g/n on the 2.4 GHz band, with WPA and WPA2.

It also contains a processor, and here the story turns funny. The ESP8266's core is a 32-bit RISC running
at 80 MHz, with 160 KB of RAM on the chip. Set that beside the 8-bit CPU it serves. I find it delightful
that the humblest peripheral in the machine, the one you can buy for the price of a sandwich, has more raw
processing power than the computer it is plugged into.

I think of the module as **the telegraph clerk**. You walk up to his counter with a message written on a
slip, he does whatever mysterious things are needed to send it across the world, and when an answer arrives
he passes it through the hatch. You never touch the wires yourself, and you don't care how they work.

That is the whole design. The Next contains no network logic at all: no MAC, no packet buffers, no checksum
engine. All of it runs as firmware inside the clerk. The FPGA provides a serial port and a couple of control
lines, and I admire how little that costs.

- **The serial pair.** The transmitter of **UART 0** drives the module's RX pin, and the module's TX pin
  drives the receiver of UART 0. Every byte of the conversation goes over these two wires.
- **GPIO0.** A pin the module looks at when it starts, and the one wire the Next can both drive and read.
  The **ESP Wifi GPIO Output Enable register, NextReg `$A8`**, bit 0 lets the Next drive it, and the **ESP
  Wifi GPIO register, NextReg `$A9`**, bit 0 is the level to drive. Reading `$A9` gives the pin's real level.
- **GPIO2.** Another start-up pin, which the Next only reads, in bit 2 of NextReg `$A9`. The FPGA never drives
  it, and bit 2 of NextReg `$A8` is fixed at 0.
- **Reset.** Bit 7 of the **Reset register, NextReg `$02`** holds the module in reset for as long as it is set.

A soft reset releases GPIO0 by clearing NextReg `$A8` and sets the level in `$A9` back to 1. The FPGA also
holds weak pull-ups on both GPIO pins and on the receive line, so anything left undriven sits high, which is
the level the module wants to see for a normal start.

Two details are worth remembering. **The reset line is shared with the expansion bus**, so bit 7 of NextReg
`$02` also resets whatever is plugged in there; I would not touch it in a program that might run with a
peripheral attached. And if the **Joystick I/O Mode register, NextReg `$0B`**, moves UART 0 to a joystick port (the [UART
chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-uart/#the-joystick-port) has the details), the clerk's
receive pin rests high and he hears nothing more until UART 0 comes back.

The dashed row in the figure is hardware flow control, the two signals that let a receiver say "wait". On
issue 2 boards those lines do not exist at all: the Next's transmitter always believes it may send, and its
request to pause goes nowhere. On issue 4 boards and later the FPGA has both lines for the module, but the
eight pins of a standard ESP-01 include neither of them. My advice is to write your code as if nothing
will ever pause the clerk, and we will soon see why that matters.

## Waking the clerk

When the ESP8266 comes out of reset, its boot ROM looks at three pins to decide what to do. Two are the ones
the Next can see, and the third is held low on the module:

- **GPIO0 high and GPIO2 high**: start the firmware in flash. This is the normal case, and the pull-ups make
  it the default.
- **GPIO0 low and GPIO2 high**: wait for a new firmware on the serial line. This is **download mode**, and it
  is how a firmware update tool running on the Next rewrites the clerk's flash without taking him out of the
  machine.

Here is the whole recipe for putting the module into download mode, using the Z80N's `nextreg` instruction:

```z80klive
        nextreg $a9,%00000000  ; GPIO0 will be low
        nextreg $a8,%00000001  ; the Next drives it
        nextreg $02,%10000000  ; hold the module in reset
        ; ...wait a moment...
        nextreg $02,%00000000  ; release: GPIO0 is sampled now
        ; ...talk to the boot ROM over UART 0...
        nextreg $a8,%00000000  ; let GPIO0 float high again
```

For an ordinary restart, leave the first two lines out. Write exactly `$80` and then `$00` to NextReg `$02`:
bits 0 and 1 of that register cause a soft and a hard reset of the Next itself, and you do not want one of
those by accident. Bit 7 alone only reaches the module and the expansion bus.

A hard reset of the Next restarts the module too. A soft reset does so only when the expansion bus is enabled;
otherwise the clerk stays at his desk, with his network connection intact, while the Next reboots around him.
A program can therefore find him in the middle of a conversation that some earlier program started, and I
recommend assuming exactly that when you begin.

Now a small trap. In its first moments the boot ROM prints a diagnostic message on the serial line, and on the
usual module it does so at **74,880 baud**, a rate that falls out of its 26 MHz crystal. The Next is listening
at 115,200, so what lands in the receive buffer is a burst of nonsense. Some of those frames fail their stop bit
and are thrown away; the rest arrive as random bytes. After any module reset, wait, then clear UART 0 with the
`$98`, `$18` pair on the frame register, and only then start talking.

## Talking in AT

The clerk's language is older than the Spectrum. In 1981 Hayes sold a modem you controlled by sending it text
commands beginning with the letters **AT**, for "attention", and the format stuck for decades. Espressif's AT
firmware offers the same bargain: lines of text in, lines of text out.

A command is ASCII text ending in carriage return and line feed, written to UART 0 with a loop like the `Send`
routine in the UART chapter. The simplest possible exchange checks that anybody is home:

```text
AT
OK
```

If you see `OK`, the clerk is awake and both ends agree on the baud rate. If you see garbage or nothing, they
probably don't. The Next powers up at 115,200, which is the speed most modules use, but some leave the factory at
57,600 or 19,200. The Next's `.UART` terminal has a `-f` option that hunts for the right rate. Once found, the
command `AT+UART_DEF=115200,8,1,0,0` (plain `AT+UART=` on older firmware) stores the speed in the module's
flash, so you only do this once.

By default the module also **echoes** everything you send, which a human at a terminal likes and a program does
not. `ATE0` switches echo off, and it is the first command I would send.

## Joining a network

Getting on to Wi-Fi takes three commands, and you normally type them only once:

```text
AT+CWMODE=1
OK
AT+CWJAP="MyNetwork","MyPassword"
WIFI CONNECTED
WIFI GOT IP

OK
AT+CIFSR
+CIFSR:STAIP,"192.168.1.42"
+CIFSR:STAMAC,"5c:cf:7f:12:34:56"

OK
```

`AT+CWMODE=1` makes the module a **station**, a client of an access point, rather than an access point of its
own. `AT+CWJAP` joins the network and is slow, because this is where the radio, the password handshake and the
address request all happen. `AT+CIFSR` shows the address the router gave it.

What I like best is that the clerk **remembers**. The network name and password stay in his flash, and when he
restarts he rejoins without being asked, telling you `WIFI CONNECTED` and `WIFI GOT IP` as he does. So the
Next's `WIFI.BAS` set-up program needs to run once, and every later program simply finds the network already
there. The same fact has a darker side: your Wi-Fi password lives on that little board, so wipe it with
`AT+CWQAP` before you sell the module on.

Here is the serial traffic of that picture, in order. Lines beginning with `>>` are what your program sends and
the others are the module's answers:

```text
>> AT+CIPSTART="TCP","192.168.1.20",8080
CONNECT

OK
>> AT+CIPSEND=38
OK
>
>> GET / HTTP/1.0<CR><LF>Host: 192.168.1.20<CR><LF><CR><LF>
SEND OK

+IPD,31:HTTP/1.0 200 OK<CR><LF><CR><LF>Hello, Next!
CLOSED
```

Walk through it with me:

1. `AT+CIPSTART` asks the clerk to open a connection, and he handles the whole TCP handshake himself. `TCP` could
   have been `UDP` or `SSL`, and a host name works as well as an address, because the module does the DNS lookup too.
2. `AT+CIPSEND=38` announces exactly how many bytes are coming. The module answers `OK` and then a `>` prompt.
   Wait for the prompt: bytes sent before it are not part of the message.
3. Now the 38 bytes go out, with no line ending added; the count is the only thing that says where the message
   stops. Send too many and the module answers `busy` to the excess; send too few and it keeps waiting, taking
   your next command as part of the data.
4. `SEND OK` says the bytes have left the module. It does not say the server has read them.
5. The reply arrives unasked. It starts with `+IPD,31:`, the module's way of saying "here come 31 bytes from
   the network".
6. When the server closes the connection, the module reports `CLOSED`, right after the last byte of data.

Notice the shape of this. The line runs both ways at once, so replies and network data can arrive at any moment,
including while you are in the middle of sending something else. One `AT+CIPSEND` carries at most 2,048
bytes, so longer messages go out in several rounds.

The rule that makes it work is that **the data is counted, never searched**. A web page that happens to contain
the text `+IPD,` or `CLOSED` is fine, because once the length is known, the next that many bytes are data regardless
of what they say.

So a reader needs just three steps: look for the tag, read a number, copy that many bytes. Here it is in Z80N:

```z80klive
; read one +IPD packet from UART 0 to (DE); HL = its length
GetPacket:
        ld hl,Tag
Match:  call GetByte
        cp (hl)
        jr z,Hit
        ld hl,Tag              ; no: start over, but this byte
        cp (hl)                ; may be a new "+"
        jr nz,Match
Hit:    inc hl
        ld a,(hl)
        or a
        jr nz,Match            ; more of the tag to find
        ld hl,0                ; now the length, in decimal
Digit:  call GetByte
        cp ':'
        jr z,Body
        sub '0'
        add hl,hl              ; HL * 2
        ld b,h
        ld c,l
        add hl,hl              ; * 4
        add hl,hl              ; * 8
        add hl,bc              ; * 10
        add hl,a               ; Z80N: plus the digit
        jr Digit
Body:   push hl                ; keep the length
Copy:   ld a,h
        or l
        jr z,Done
        call GetByte
        ld (de),a
        inc de
        dec hl
        jr Copy
Done:   pop hl
        ret

GetByte:
        ld bc,$133b
Wait:   in a,(c)               ; status
        rra
        jr nc,Wait             ; nothing yet
        inc b                  ; $143B: RX
        in a,(c)               ; the oldest byte
        ret

Tag:    .defb "+IPD,",0
```

How it works:

- **`GetByte`** waits for status bit 0, then reads the receive port. It moves `B` from `$13` to `$14` rather than
  loading a whole new port number.
- **The matching loop** walks `HL` along the tag. On a mismatch it goes back to the start of the tag, but it first
  checks whether the byte that failed is itself a `+`. Without that check, the text `++IPD,5:` would slip past.
- **The digits** build the length with the usual multiply-by-ten, in which `A` survives because `add hl` leaves it
  alone. `add hl,a` is a Z80N instruction; on a plain Z80 you would load `BC` and add that.
- **The copy** counts `HL` down, with no interest at all in what the bytes are.

This is the honest minimum, and I would not ship it as it stands: `GetByte` waits forever, so a connection that dies
in mid-packet hangs the program. A real reader counts down a timeout in `Wait` and gives up.

## The promise that stops at the module

The clerk acknowledges a packet to the server the moment **he** has it. From the server's point of view the data
has been delivered, and it will never be sent again. Then the clerk pushes those bytes down the serial line at full
speed, and in the usual arrangement he neither knows nor cares whether anybody is reading.

Now do the arithmetic. A TCP packet carries up to 1,460 bytes, which takes 127 ms to cross at 115,200 baud, and the
Next's receive buffer holds 512, filled in 44 ms. If your program goes away for longer than that while a packet is
arriving, perhaps to write a sector to the SD card, the buffer overflows and bytes are lost. TCP will not save you,
because as far as TCP is concerned, everything went perfectly.

Which leaves you three defences, and I would use them in this order:

- **Never stop reading during a packet.** Read into memory, and do the slow work between packets. Status bit 2, the
  overflow flag, tells you it went wrong, which at least lets you say so.
- **Add your own check.** If the data matters, put a checksum or CRC in the protocol above TCP and ask again when it
  fails. That sounds redundant until you remember where TCP stops. NextSync, the popular file transfer tool for the
  Next, puts checksums on every packet for exactly this reason.
- **Ask for the data yourself.** Newer firmware has a **passive receive mode**, `AT+CIPRECVMODE=1`. In this mode the
  module holds what arrives, tells you how much is waiting, and hands over a portion only when you request it with
  `AT+CIPRECVDATA`. This turns the clerk from someone who shoves papers through the hatch into someone who waits
  to be asked, and as his own buffer fills, TCP's flow control quietly slows the server down. It is the only true
  end-to-end flow control available here, and I wish every module had it. Check with `AT+GMR` what yours can do.

## How fast it can go

At 115,200 baud a byte takes 86.8 µs and the line carries 11,520 bytes a second, which the framing barely dents: on
a full packet the `+IPD,1460:` header is under one per cent. That means about a tenth of a megabyte in ten seconds,
so a 200K file takes about 18 seconds at best.

You can go faster, in three moves that must happen in this order:

1. Tell the module the new speed with `AT+UART_CUR=921600,8,1,0,0`. `CUR` means it lasts until the next restart, and
   I much prefer it to `AT+UART_DEF` for this, because a machine stuck at an unusual saved speed is a pain to recover.
2. Wait for his `OK`, which still comes at the old speed.
3. Program the Next's prescaler for the new rate, here 30, which gives 933,333 baud (see the UART chapter's section on
   the baud rate). Do this promptly, or you'll miss the start of whatever he sends next.

The next limit is your code. Add up the instructions in the reader above and each data byte costs 122 T-states
when the buffer is not empty, before counting any wait states the machine inserts. So the reader manages about 28,700
bytes a second at 3.5 MHz, and 229,500 at 28 MHz. Compare that with what the line delivers:

- At **115,200 baud**, 11,523 bytes a second: comfortable at any CPU speed.
- At **921,600 baud** (really 933,333), 93,333 bytes a second, one every 10.7 µs: the reader needs a 14 MHz CPU.
- At **2,000,000 baud**, 200,000 bytes a second, one every 5 µs: it needs 28 MHz and nothing else happening, and the
  receive buffer fills in just 2.6 ms.

In practice the radio, the module and the protocol all take their cut. NextSync reports a best of about 48 KB/s for a
single large file, more than four times what 115,200 baud could carry, and that is a fair picture of what this link
can do when everything goes well. I think that is rather good for a machine whose ancestors loaded from cassette at
under 200 bytes a second.

## Firmware is the fine print

Everything above the serial line belongs to the firmware, and not all modules run the same one. AT firmware versions
0.30, 1.3, 1.5.4, 2.2.1 and 3.0.3 have all been found on modules; in a sample of about fifty bought from ordinary
shops, 1.x was the commonest. Espressif's documents for those versions differ in the details:

- Older firmware answers `AT+CIPSTART` with `OK` and `Linked` rather than `CONNECT` and `OK`.
- Sending a command while the module is busy can bring back `busy`, and the wording differs between versions.
- Passive receive does not exist in the older versions, and the speed command has changed its name over time:
  `AT+UART` on some firmware, `AT+UART_CUR` and `AT+UART_DEF` on others.
- `AT+CIPMUX=1` allows several connections at once, and it changes the tag to `+IPD,<id>,<length>:`.

My rule of thumb: match on the shortest thing that is stable, such as `OK`, `ERROR` and `+IPD,`, and treat every other
line as decoration. `AT+GMR` tells you what you are talking to.

When a clerk misbehaves, he also tells you why. After an unexpected restart the module prints `rst cause:` and a number:
0 for a normal power-on, 1 for the hardware watchdog after the firmware has hung, 2 for an uncaught exception, 3 for
the software watchdog, 4 for a software restart and 5 for waking from deep sleep. If you see this repeatedly, the module
is crashing, and the fault is rarely in your Z80 code.

The Next's distribution ships several layers built on all this, so you often need not write any of it. `UART.DRV` is
an interrupt-driven driver with a 16K buffer, which solves the overflow problem for programs that use it. The ESPAT
driver serves BASIC programs, and the `.http` dot command fetches a file. `.UART` and `TERMINAL.BAS`
are for typing AT commands by hand, which remains the best way I know to learn how your own module behaves.

## What to take home

The Next has no network hardware of its own. A small ESP-01 board behind the VGA port, with an ESP8266 chip inside, runs
the radio, WPA2, TCP and DNS in its own firmware, on a 32-bit processor faster than the machine it serves. The FPGA gives
it UART 0 and a few control lines: GPIO0 through NextRegs `$A8` and `$A9`, GPIO2 for reading, and reset through bit 7
of NextReg `$02`, which it shares with the expansion bus. GPIO0 low at reset selects download mode; the pull-ups give the
normal start, and the boot ROM's message at 74,880 baud leaves junk in the buffer to clear away.

Your program talks to the clerk in AT commands, lines of text ending in CR LF. `AT+CWJAP` joins a network once and for
all. `AT+CIPSTART` opens a connection, `AT+CIPSEND=n` sends exactly n bytes after the `>` prompt, and data comes back
as `+IPD,n:` followed by n bytes that are counted and never searched.

The lesson I would keep is that TCP's promise ends at the module. A 1,460-byte packet fills the 512-byte buffer in 44 ms
at 115,200 baud whether or not you are reading, and a byte lost there is lost for good, so keep reading during a packet,
add a checksum if the data matters, or use passive receive if your firmware has it. With `AT+UART_CUR` and a new
prescaler the link can go far faster than its default of 11,520 bytes a second, as long as your reader keeps up. Treat the
clerk politely, count what you hand him, and he will bring you the whole world through a two-wire hatch.
