# Interrupts: Fourteen Voices, Two Wires

A Next game is a busy place. The music player wants to run fifty times a second, a raster split wants its
line, a sample needs its next byte from a timer, the Wi-Fi chip has just sent a reply, and somebody pressed
the DRIVE button to browse the SD card. Fourteen pieces of hardware would like the CPU's attention, often at
the same moment. The Z80 has exactly two wires to hear them with. So who decides who goes first, how does the
CPU learn who called, and what happens to the ones that have to wait?

I think interrupts are where a Next program grows up. Polling works until it doesn't: the moment your code
must keep a tune steady while it loads a level, or split the screen while it runs a game, you need the
hardware to tap the CPU on the shoulder at the right time. The Next's answer is far richer than the
Spectrum's single frame interrupt, and it has a few traps that look like bugs until you know the rule
behind them. Know the rules, and an interrupt becomes the most reliable clock in your program.

You should know two chapters first. [Memory, Ports and NextRegs: How the Z80N Reaches the
Machine](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-memory-io/) explains NextRegs, the DivMMC and the
Multiface, which come back here as NMI devices. [The Frame and the Beam: Two Counters, Four
Timings](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/) explains where in the frame the frame
interrupt and the line interrupt happen, and I will not repeat it.

## The road ahead

We start with the two wires into the CPU, `/INT` and `/NMI`, and the cast of fourteen maskable sources in
their order of priority. Then come the two ways the Next drives `/INT`: pulse mode, the Spectrum's way, with
its short pulse that can be missed, and hardware IM2 mode, where every source has a vector of its own. The
heart of the chapter is the daisy chain behind hardware IM2 mode, how a source waits its turn and what RETI
and RETN do to the queue; if you read only one section, make it that one. After that we look at the
switches and the status bits, the sources up close, how fast the CPU answers, and how the DMA steps aside
for an interrupt. Last comes the NMI: its sources, its arbiter, the devices that answer at `$0066`, and the
stackless response that keeps the stack out of it.

## Two wires into the CPU

The Z80 has two interrupt inputs. **`/INT`**, the maskable interrupt, is a level the CPU looks at once per
instruction: if it is low when an instruction ends, and interrupts are enabled, the CPU answers instead of
fetching the next one. I call it **the doorbell**: it rings, and the CPU answers when it has finished what
it was doing, if it is willing to answer at all. **`/NMI`**, the non-maskable interrupt, works on an edge:
the CPU remembers that it fell, and answers at the end of the instruction whatever `DI` said. That one is
**the fire alarm**, and you will meet the fire brigade that answers it near the end.

A few rules of the CPU's own matter for everything that follows:

- The CPU decides at **the last T-state but one** of an instruction. `/INT` must be low at that moment.
- **Not after a prefix.** The CPU never stops between a `DD`, `FD`, `CB` or `ED` prefix and its opcode, so
  a long chain of prefixes postpones the answer.
- **`EI` takes effect one instruction late.** The instruction after `EI` always runs, which is what makes
  `EI` / `RETI` at the end of a handler safe; a run of `EI`s postpones the answer for as long as it lasts.
- **IM 1** calls `$0038`. **IM 2** reads a byte from the data bus, the **vector**, and calls the address
  stored at `I` × 256 + vector. **IM 0** executes the byte on the data bus as an instruction.

## Fourteen voices in a fixed order

The Next has fourteen maskable sources, and they stand in a fixed order of priority, the same in every
mode: the line interrupt first, then the two UARTs' receivers, then the CTC's channels, then the ULA's frame
interrupt, and last the two UARTs' transmitters. Behind them all stands the expansion bus, for a peripheral
plugged into the edge connector.

Two things in that list surprise people, and I like both of them. First, **the alarm clock beats the
veteran**: the line interrupt, index 0, outranks the ULA's frame interrupt, index 11. That is the right way
round. A raster split must land on its line or it is visibly wrong, while a once-a-frame job can start a few
microseconds late and nobody notices. Second, the CTC has eight places but only **four channels**: channels
4 to 7 are reserved, never fire, and their enable and status bits read 0.

## Pulse mode: one pulse for everybody

After a reset the Next drives `/INT` in **pulse mode**, the way the Spectrum always did: when a source asks
for attention, `/INT` goes low for a fixed time and then lets go, whatever the CPU did meanwhile. The time is
counted in CPU clocks: **32 T-states** in 48K and +3 timing, **36** in 128K and Pentagon timing, at every
CPU speed. So at 28 MHz the pulse is eight times shorter in real time than at 3.5 MHz.

The pulse is a moment, not a promise, and here is what follows from that:

- **The pulse is shared.** Every enabled source in pulse mode starts the same pulse. A second request while
  the pulse is low starts nothing new, so two sources that fire close together give the CPU one interrupt.
- **A pulse can be missed.** If interrupts stay disabled for longer than the pulse, or the DMA holds the bus
  across it, the CPU never sees it. Nothing holds the request; only its **status bit** remembers that it
  happened.
- **The CPU cannot tell who rang.** In IM 1 it calls `$0038` whatever the source. In IM 2 the data bus
  carries **`$FF`**, so the CPU reads its handler's address from `I` × 256 + `$FF`. And IM 0, which so much
  folklore warns about, is perfectly predictable on the Next: the FPGA drives `$FF` itself, and `$FF` is
  `RST $38`, so IM 0 behaves exactly like IM 1. (A device on an enabled expansion bus may put its own byte
  there instead.)

Since the vector says nothing, a pulse-mode handler that serves several sources reads the status bits to
find out who rang. I find this the most practical trick of pulse mode:

```z80klive
Isr:    push af
        push bc
        ld bc,$243b           ; select INT Status 0
        ld a,$c8
        out (c),a
        inc b
        in a,(c)              ; bit 1: line, bit 0: ULA
        nextreg $c8,a         ; write the 1s back: clears them
        ; ... test bits 1 and 0, do the work ...
        pop bc
        pop af
        ei
        reti
```

If that handler can ever run late, it can still tell a missed pulse from no pulse at all: the status bit was
set even though the doorbell rang while nobody was listening.

Bits 7 to 5 come from NextReg `$C0`, bits 4 to 1 are the source's index from the priority list, and bit 0 is
always 0, so every source gets a two-byte slot in a table of 28 bytes. Here is a setup with the table at
`$BE00`, the vectors from `$A0`, and handlers for the ULA and CTC channel 0:

```z80klive
        di
        ld a,$be              ; vector table page: $BE00
        ld i,a
        im 2
        ld hl,UlaIsr
        ld ($beb6),hl         ; ULA: $A0 + 11 x 2 = $B6
        ld hl,CtcIsr
        ld ($bea6),hl         ; CTC 0: $A0 + 3 x 2 = $A6
        nextreg $c0,$a1       ; vectors from $A0, hardware IM2
        ei
```

My advice: once a program has more than one source, use hardware IM2 mode. No 257-byte tables of identical
bytes, no guessing who rang, and no interrupt lost because a long `LDIR` happened to run under `DI`.

## The daisy chain

Behind hardware IM2 mode sits a **daisy chain**, the scheme Zilog designed for its own peripherals: the
fourteen sources stand in a row in priority order, and each one passes a permission to the next. A source
may interrupt only while the permission reaches it, and it withholds the permission from everybody behind it
while it is busy. I call it **the queue**, and it is a very polite one. Each source goes through four
states:

1. **Idle.** The source passes the permission on unchanged.
2. **Requesting.** Something happened; the source pulls `/INT` low if the permission reaches it and the CPU
   is in IM 2. From now on it withholds the permission, so the sources behind it cannot jump the queue.
3. **Acknowledged.** The CPU's acknowledge cycle reached it; it puts its vector on the bus.
4. **In service.** The CPU runs its handler. The source still withholds the permission, until a `RETI`
   releases it.

1. The ULA rings at the frame's start. The CPU acknowledges it with vector `$B6`, and the ULA is in
   service. Its handler, the music player, executes `EI` at once, because it knows it will run for a while.
2. The alarm clock rings for the split. The line interrupt stands before the ULA, so the permission still
   reaches it, and the music player's `EI` from step 1 means the CPU is willing to answer: it interrupts the
   music player with vector `$A0`. Both conditions are needed. Without that `EI`, the CPU would ignore the
   request until the music player ended, however high the line interrupt stands in the chain. The ULA's
   handler is paused, but the ULA is still in service.
3. UART 0's transmit buffer empties. Its transmitter stands behind the ULA, so the permission does not reach
   it: it waits, requesting, however long that takes.
4. The line handler's `RETI` releases the line interrupt, the highest source in service. The CPU is back in
   the music player, and the transmitter still waits, because the ULA is still in service.
5. The music player's `RETI` releases the ULA. The permission flows down the chain again, reaches the
   transmitter, and it rings at once: vector `$B8`.
6. Its `RETI` releases it, and your main program carries on.

Notice the two `RETI`s in a row in steps 4 and 5: each one releases exactly one source, the highest in
service, and the chain unwinds in the order it was built. I find that very tidy.

**Two sources at the same instant.** Say a CTC channel that plays samples and the ULA's frame interrupt
fire at the same moment. Both move to "requesting", but CTC channel 0 stands before the ULA, so it withholds
the permission even before the CPU has answered anyone. The CPU takes vector `$A6` first; the ULA waits until
the CTC handler's `RETI`, and its handler starts that much later. If your frame interrupt must be punctual,
keep the handlers of everything before it short.

**A handler that forgot `EI`.** The CPU clears its interrupt enable when it acknowledges an interrupt, so
a handler that leaves `EI` to its very end cannot be interrupted at all, not even by the alarm clock that
stands before it. The line interrupt waits, requesting, until the music player's closing `EI` / `RETI`, and
your raster split
lands wherever the music player happens to finish: a few lines too low, and a different line every frame.
My rule of thumb: a handler that runs longer than a few dozen T-states executes `EI` early.

**A timer faster than its handler.** CTC channel 0 with a prescaler of 16 and a time constant of 100 ticks
28,000,000 ÷ 16 ÷ 100 = 17,500 times a second, every 200 T-states at 3.5 MHz. If its handler takes 250
T-states, the next tick comes while the channel is still in service. The chain does not queue it: one
outstanding request per source, so the repeat is folded into the one being served, and only the status bit
records it. The sample player now runs at half its speed, and nothing crashes to tell you. Keep the handler
shorter than the timer's period, or slow the timer down.

**A handler that ends with `RET`.** This one is my favourite bug, because it looks so innocent. CTC channel
0's handler ends with `RET` instead of `RETI`. The chain never sees a `RETI`, so the channel stays in service
forever, and it withholds the permission from everybody behind it: CTC channels 1 to 3, the ULA and both
transmitters never interrupt again. The line interrupt and the UART receivers, which stand before it, still
work, which makes the symptom baffling: "the frame interrupt stopped after the first sample". The status bit
gives it away. In hardware IM2 mode a source's status bit reads 1 while it is in service, so NextReg `$C9`
bit 0 stuck at 1 tells you exactly which handler forgot its `RETI`.

## RETI releases, RETN does not

The chain learns that a handler has finished by watching the instructions the CPU fetches: when it sees
**`RETI`**, the opcode pair `ED 4D`, the highest-priority source in service goes back to idle and the
permission flows again. The rules are strict, and I would learn them by heart:

- **Only `RETI` releases.** `RETN` (`ED 45` and its aliases) releases nothing. A maskable handler that ends
  with `RETN` leaves its source in service, and that source and everything behind it are blocked for good.
- **Only in IM 2.** A `RETI` executed while the CPU is not in IM 2 releases nothing either. The chain knows
  the CPU's mode because it watches the `IM` instructions go by, and NextReg `$C0` bits 2–1 read back what
  it saw.

What if the CPU is in IM 1 while hardware IM2 mode is on? Then the chain cannot interrupt at all, with one
exception: **the veteran still rings in the old way**. The ULA's frame interrupt alone falls back to a pulse,
so a program that switches hardware IM2 mode on but stays in IM 1 still gets its fifty interrupts a second.
The other sources move to "requesting" and wait there. The moment the CPU executes `IM 2`, they all ring at
once, in priority order. I find that a lovely piece of backward compatibility, and a great way to be very
surprised.

## Switches and status bits

Every source has an **enable**, and the enables sit in three registers, the **INT EN 0, INT EN 1 and INT EN
2 registers, NextRegs `$C4`, `$C5` and `$C6`**:

- **NextReg `$C4`**: bit 0 the ULA, bit 1 the line interrupt, bit 7 the expansion bus. It reads `$81` after
  a reset: the ULA and the bus on, the line interrupt off.
- **NextReg `$C5`**: bits 3–0 the CTC channels. A channel's enable is the same flip-flop as bit 7 of its own
  control word, so writing either one overwrites the other.
- **NextReg `$C6`**: for each UART, receive (bit 0 for UART 0, bit 4 for UART 1), receive "near full only"
  (bits 1 and 5) and transmit (bits 2 and 6).

The ULA's switch is shared by three doors, all leading to one flip-flop: port `$FF` bit 6 (1 = off), bit 2
of the **Line Interrupt Control register, NextReg `$22`** (1 = off), and bit 0 of NextReg `$C4` (1 = on, the
other way round). The line interrupt's enable is bit 1 of both NextReg `$22` and NextReg `$C4`. Whichever you
wrote last wins, so pick one register and stick to it.

Every source also has a **status bit**, in the **INT Status 0, 1 and 2 registers, NextRegs `$C8`, `$C9` and
`$CA`**, laid out like the enables. A status bit is set by the source's event, and **writing a 1 clears it**;
writing a 0 leaves it alone, so a handler clears exactly what it served and nothing else. Three details
matter:

- **The CTC and the UARTs set their status even when their interrupt is disabled.** That is "polled mode":
  leave the interrupt off and watch the bit. The ULA and the line interrupt are different: switched off,
  they raise nothing, not even a status bit.
- **In hardware IM2 mode a status bit reads 1 while its source is requesting or in service**, and writing
  a 1 cannot clear it until the `RETI`.
- **NextReg `$22` bit 7 reads the pulse.** It is 1 while the pulse-mode `/INT` is low, from any source, not
  only the ULA. Writing it does nothing.

And a program can ring the doorbell itself. The **Generate Maskable Interrupt register, NextReg `$20`**,
raises a request when you write a 1: bit 7 the line interrupt, bit 6 the ULA, bits 3–0 the CTC channels.
These requests ignore the enables, set the status bit and behave like the real thing in either mode. I use
it to test a handler without waiting for the hardware.

## The sources up close

**The ULA's frame interrupt** comes once a frame at a fixed point of the beam: on line 0 in 48K timing and
at 60 Hz, on line 1 in 128K and +3 timing at 50 Hz, and on the last line in Pentagon timing, which has no
60 Hz frame. The frame-and-beam chapter's [frame
interrupt](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#the-frame-interrupt) has the details.

**The line interrupt**, the alarm clock, rings for line *n* near the end of line *n* − 1's display area, in
the line numbers the frame-and-beam chapter's [line
interrupt](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#the-line-interrupt) explains. A line
number one past the last line of the frame rings like line 0; anything higher never rings.

**The CTC**, the metronome of the DAC chapter, raises a channel's interrupt when the channel's count
reaches zero. Its timer counts the 28 MHz master clock through a prescaler of 16 or 256, so its rate does not
change with the CPU's speed. On a Zilog CTC a program writes an interrupt vector into the chip; the Next
decodes that write and throws it away, because the vector comes from the chain.

**The UARTs** have two receive conditions and one transmit condition:

- **Receive, data available**: the receive buffer holds at least one byte.
- **Receive, near full**: the 512-byte buffer holds 384 bytes, three quarters. With the "near full only"
  bit set in NextReg `$C6`, only this one interrupts.
- **Transmit**: the transmit buffer has become empty. That is about one character's time before the line
  falls silent, because the last byte is still being sent.

All requests are **edges**: a source asks once when its condition becomes true, not for as long as it stays
true. For the UART that means a byte arriving into a buffer that is not empty raises nothing new, so my
advice is to drain the whole buffer in the handler, not to take one byte per interrupt.

  raster effects chapter's line interrupt: up to 44 dots at 3.5 MHz, as long as no contention or wait state

- **The acknowledge.** In IM 1 it takes **13 T-states**: an acknowledge cycle of 7 and the two writes that
  push the return address. In IM 2 it takes **19**: the same, and two reads that fetch the handler's address.
- **`HALT` helps, but not completely.** A halted CPU executes NOP cycles of 4 T-states each and can answer
  only at the end of one, so a program waiting in `HALT` still sees 0 to 3 T of jitter.

## The DMA steps aside

The DMA can hold the bus for a long transfer, and in pulse mode an interrupt that comes and goes during it is
simply lost. In hardware IM2 mode the Next can do better: the **DMA INT EN 0, 1 and 2 registers, NextRegs
`$CC`, `$CD` and `$CE`**, laid out like the enables, choose which sources may interrupt the DMA. While such a
source is requesting or in service, the DMA stops asking for the bus between bytes, the CPU takes the
interrupt and runs the handler, and the DMA carries on after the `RETI`. Bit 7 of NextReg `$CC` does the same
for the NMI. This works only in hardware IM2 mode, and the DMA itself raises no interrupt when it finishes.
I like this arrangement: the transfer gives way to the doorbell instead of making it wait in the rain.

## The fire alarm

The NMI cannot be switched off with `DI`, and on the Next it belongs to two devices you met in the memory
chapter: **the fire brigade**, the Multiface, and **the butler**, the DivMMC. Its sources are:

- **The M1 button** (F9 on a PS/2 keyboard) asks for a Multiface NMI, allowed by bit 3 of the **Peripheral
  2 Setting register, NextReg `$06`**.
- **The DRIVE button** (F10) asks for a DivMMC NMI, allowed by bit 4 of NextReg `$06`. It works only while
  the DivMMC's port is switched on.
- **The Reset register, NextReg `$02`**: writing bit 3 asks for a Multiface NMI, bit 2 for a DivMMC NMI,
  under the same enables. The Copper can write it too.
- **An I/O trap**: with the **I/O Traps register, NextReg `$D8`**, bit 0 set, touching the +3's disk ports
  `$2FFD` and `$3FFD` asks for a Multiface NMI, so a program can emulate the +3's disk drive in software.
- **The expansion bus**, for a device on the edge connector.

A request that loses is gone; it does not wait. Once a source is taken, `/NMI` goes low at once and the
arbiter walks four states: **idle**, **fetch** until the CPU fetches the opcode at `$0066`, **hold** while
the device that answered says it is busy, and **end**, before it takes the next request. While the arbiter is
past fetch, a new request changes nothing, not even the flags of NextReg `$02`.

## Who answers at $0066

The NMI sends the CPU to `$0066`, and whoever the arbiter chose appears there to answer:

- **The fire brigade** pages itself in during that very fetch: the Multiface's ROM at `$0000`, its RAM at
  `$2000`. It leaves on a `RETN` or when the program touches its ports, which depend on the Multiface type
  chosen in the **Peripheral 5 Setting register, NextReg `$0A`**.
- **The butler** comes in through his `$0066` entry point, instant or delayed as bits 1 and 0 of the
  **Divmmc Entry Points 1 register, NextReg `$BB`**, say. It needs no ROM 3; it needs only a DivMMC NMI.
  He leaves on a `RETN` or through `$1FF8–$1FFF`.

For both, only the plain `RETN`, `ED 45`, counts as "the NMI is over"; its aliases and `RETI` do not.

- The CPU still runs both push cycles and `SP` still goes down by 2, but the memory request is held off, so
  **nothing is written**.
- The return address goes to the **NMI Return Address LSB and MSB registers, NextRegs `$C2` and `$C3`**. In
  fact they record it on **every** NMI, stackless or not, and a handler may read or rewrite them.
- **The first `RETN`** after the NMI takes its return address from NextRegs `$C2`/`$C3` instead of the stack,
  and `SP` goes up by 2 as usual. A `RETI` in the handler does not: it pops the real stack, like any return.
  Clear bit 3 inside the handler, and its `RETN` pops the stack after all.

I find this one of the Next's quietly brilliant additions. A debugger or a snapshot tool can stop any program
at any moment without leaving a single footprint in its memory.

## Resets

A reset puts the interrupt system back into the Spectrum's shape: NextReg `$C0` becomes 0 (pulse mode, the
stackless NMI off), NextReg `$C4` becomes `$81` (the ULA and the expansion bus on, the line interrupt off),
NextRegs `$C5`, `$C6` and `$CC`–`$CE` become 0, NextReg `$22` and `$23` become 0, and every status bit and
every pending request is cleared. Leaving hardware IM2 mode does the same to the chain: every request and every
handler in service is forgotten. So here is my last piece of advice: set the interrupt registers yourself at
the start of your program, and do not inherit them from whoever ran before you.

## What to take home

Two wires, then, and a well-organised crowd behind them. **`/INT`**, the doorbell, is shared by fourteen
sources in a fixed order: the line interrupt, the UART receivers, CTC channels 0 to 3 (4 to 7 have places but
no hardware), the ULA's frame interrupt and the UART transmitters, with the expansion bus behind them. **`/NMI`**,
the fire alarm, belongs to the Multiface and the DivMMC.

In **pulse mode**, the default, `/INT` is a pulse of 32 or 36 T-states that anybody can start and nobody can
hold: miss it under `DI` and it is gone, and the vector is always `$FF`, so IM 0 is just IM 1 in disguise. In
**hardware IM2 mode**, NextReg `$C0` bit 0, every source holds `/INT` until it is served and supplies its
vector: NextReg `$C0` bits 7–5, its index, and a 0. The daisy chain makes each source wait behind the ones
before it, lets higher ones nest, and releases a source only on a real `RETI` executed in IM 2; `RETN`
releases nothing, and in IM 1 only the ULA still rings.

Enables live in NextRegs `$C4`–`$C6`, status in `$C8`–`$CA` (write 1 to clear, set even when disabled for the
CTC and the UARTs), and NextReg `$20` rings the doorbell by software. The CPU answers within 0 to 22 T plus 13
or 19 T of acknowledge, and the DMA can be told to step aside.

The NMI's arbiter takes the Multiface before the DivMMC before the bus, its devices answer at `$0066`, and
the stackless NMI keeps the return address in NextRegs `$C2`/`$C3` instead of the stack. Every one of these rules
has a reason, and once you know them, the crowd behind the two wires turns into a queue that never pushes.
