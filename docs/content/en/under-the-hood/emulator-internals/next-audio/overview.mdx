# ZX Spectrum Next: The Big Picture

A 48K Spectrum had one bit of sound and a speaker the size of a coin. Play a modern game on a ZX
Spectrum Next and you hear chip music in nine voices, in stereo, with sampled drums and speech on top,
and the same machine still loads a tape by listening to it. Where does each of those sounds come from,
and how do they end up in one pair of headphones?

Sound does a lot of work in a program. Music sets the mood and the pace of a game; an effect tells you
that you jumped, fired or were hit before your eyes have found it on the screen; speech and sampled
sounds make a game feel larger than its 128K. On a Spectrum, sound had one more job: programs were
saved to tape and loaded from it as sound. And a machine with several sound sources that run by
themselves does all this without tying up the CPU, which is busy enough drawing the screen.

This article is the map of the Next's sound. It is the first article of a series; the ones that follow
take each source apart, and this one shows how they fit together. If digital sound is new to you, read
[How a Computer Makes Sound](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/) first: it explains
samples, the DAC, the filter that makes the result smooth, sound from a single bit and mixing, and this
series does not explain them again. [ZX Spectrum: How the Screen Is Drawn](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/)
explains port `$FE`, which the beeper shares with the border, and
[The ZX Spectrum Next Core and the FPGA](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-core/) explains why
the Next's hardware lives in an FPGA.

## Part 1: From one bit to four kinds of source

The ZX Spectrum 48K makes sound with one bit. Bit 4 of port `$FE`, called **EAR**, drives the speaker:
the program sets it, clears it, sets it again, and the speaker's cone follows. Bit 3, **MIC**, drives
the tape output and reaches the same speaker more weakly. Every sound a 48K program makes, from a key
click to two-voice music, is the program switching those bits on and off at the right moments. The
speaker is driven *in the program's own time*: while it plays, it can do little else.

The Spectrum 128K added a sound chip, the AY-3-8910, with three voices that play by themselves once
they are set up. The Next keeps both and adds more beside them:

That makes **four kinds of source**: the beeper, with its two bits EAR and MIC (MIC also carries the
tape input), the AY chips, the DACs and the Pi. The rest of this article and the whole series are about
these four, the mixer that adds them, and the outputs the sum leaves through.

The Spectrum's sound stays exactly as it was. A 48K program that toggles port `$FE` bit 4 hears the
same square wave; a 128K tune that writes the AY through ports `$FFFD` and `$BFFD` plays on the first AY
chip exactly as it did on the 128K. Everything new is switched off or out of the way until a program
asks for it (Part 7).

The level scale is the point of this figure: it shows how loud each source can be next to the others.
A DAC or the Pi playing at full scale is about twice as loud as the beeper, and one AY channel is half as
loud as the beeper.

The four tones are all close to 1,000 Hz, but not equal. Each source gets its pitch from its own clock,
divided by whole numbers the program chooses, and only some pitches come out of whole numbers:

- **The beeper**: 3,500,000 ÷ (2 × 1,760) = 994 Hz. The CPU's 3.5 MHz clock, divided by the length of
  the program's loop in T-states, twice per cycle; the loop's length can change only in steps of 13
  T-states.
- **The AY channel**: 218,750 ÷ (2 × 109) = 1,003 Hz. The chip's counters, divided by the channel's
  period; the next period, 110, gives 994 Hz.
- **The DAC**: 3,500,000 ÷ (218 × 16) = 1,003 Hz. The CPU's clock again, divided by the 218 T-states
  between two writes and the 16 writes of one cycle.
- **The Pi**: exactly 1,000 Hz. The Pi sends samples it has already worked out for that pitch, so the
  Next only plays them back.

Part 6 explains where these clocks come from.

### The beeper, EAR and MIC

The beeper is the 48K's one bit, unchanged: **port `$FE` bit 4**. While the bit is 1, it adds 512 units
to both sides of the mixer; while it is 0, nothing. A program makes a note by switching it at the
note's pitch: the capture above is a loop that flips the bit every 1,760 T-states, which at 3.5 MHz is a
square wave of 3,500,000 ÷ (2 × 1,760) = 994 Hz.

**Port `$FE` bit 3**, MIC, adds 128 units while it is 1: a quarter of EAR, the ratio the 48K's resistors
gave the two bits. On the Next, the MIC line is more than the port bit. Before it reaches the mixer, it
is combined with the **tape input**, the signal arriving at the EAR socket from a tape recorder, and
with an EAR bit the Pi's audio can produce: you hear a tape load at MIC's level, as on the original
machine. The same line goes out of the MIC socket, so a program saves to tape by switching bit 3.

The beeper costs the CPU all its time while it plays, and its pitch depends on how fast the CPU runs
(Part 6). The beeper article (article 2) explains the bits, the tape input and how
one bit can play two voices.

### Three AY chips

The Next has three copies of the 128K's sound chip. Each has **three channels**, A, B and C, and each
channel plays a square wave at a pitch set by a 12-bit period, mixed with noise if the program wants,
at one of 16 fixed volumes or following an envelope, a shape that rises or falls by itself. A chip can
behave as the original General Instrument **AY-3-8910** or as Yamaha's compatible **YM2149**, which has a
finer envelope.

The 128K's own ports reach them: **port `$FFFD`** selects a register, **port `$BFFD`** writes it. A
special value written to port `$FFFD` (one with bit 7 set, which no register number has) selects
*which* chip the following writes go to, and that is all a program needs to play nine channels. This is
**TurboSound Next**. One full channel adds up to 255 units to the mixer, and with every channel of all
three chips at full volume a side gets at most 2,295.

The capture above is chip 0's channel B, with period 109: a square wave of 1,003 Hz, written once and
left alone. The AY chips are explained in article 3 (one chip) and article 4 (three chips, the stereo
modes and panning).

### Four 8-bit DACs

A **DAC** channel holds an 8-bit number the CPU writes, and adds it to the mixer until the next write.
The Next has four, **A** and **B** on the left side and **C** and **D** on the right. Each side adds its
two channels and multiplies them by 4: up to 2,040 units.

The Spectrum and Pentagon world had half a dozen sound cards built on such DACs (Soundrive, Covox,
Specdrum and others), each on its own ports. The Next answers on all of their ports, so their software
plays unchanged, and adds three NextRegs, `$2C` to `$2E`, through which the Copper or a program with no
free port can write them. The capture above is a sine wave, 16 steps per cycle, written to **port `$DF`**,
Specdrum's port, which writes channels A and D at once and so reaches both sides. It is explained in
article 5.

### The Raspberry Pi's audio

Inside the Next sits a Raspberry Pi Zero, connected by, among other things, an **I2S** link: a
connection of a few wires that carries a stream of digital samples, left and right, in both directions.
The Pi can play anything a small Linux computer can (an MP3, a speech synthesiser, another computer's
sound chip in software) and send the result to the Next, which adds it to the mixer at 10 bits per side:
up to 1,023 units. The link also works the other way, sending the Next's own sound to the Pi, and the
Pi's stream can be turned into the EAR bit of the tape input, so that the Next loads a program the Pi
plays. The **PI I2S Audio Control register, NextReg `$A2`**, sets it all up.

The capture above is a 1,000 Hz sine, streamed in. It is explained in article 6.

## Part 3: Generators and level writers

The captures of Part 2 look alike, but they are made in two very different ways, and the difference
decides what a sound costs the program.

- A **generator** makes a waveform by itself from a few settings. The AY chips are generators: the
  program writes a period, a volume and which channels are on, and the chip plays that tone until it is
  told otherwise, for a second or for an hour, without any help from the CPU.
- A **level writer** holds whatever level it was given last. The beeper and the DACs are level writers:
  they make a waveform only if the CPU (or the DMA, or the Copper) writes a new level at every step of
  it, on time.

The DAC's sine costs 16 writes per cycle, 16,055 a second, each one on time: if a write comes late, the
step comes late, and the wave is distorted. A program can afford that for a drum hit or a word of
speech, or hand the job to the DMA, which can copy a sample to a DAC port at a fixed rate on its own
(article 5). The AY's tone costs nothing while it plays, which is why games of the 128K era played their
music on the AY: the program can run the game and touch the chip only when the tune moves to the next
note, typically once every frame.

In return, a level writer can play *anything*: any recorded sound, any waveform the program can compute.
A generator plays only what its counters can make: square waves, noise and envelope shapes. The Next has
both kinds, so a program chooses per sound: AY music, with DAC drums over it.

## Part 4: One mixer, two sides

Every source ends in one place: the **mixer**, which adds them. It has two sides, and every source
gives each side its own number. For the beeper the two numbers are always the same; the AY chips, the
DACs and the Pi can make them differ, which is what places a sound left or right (stereo is explained in
the background page's part
[Two speakers](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/#two-speakers-stereo-and-space)).

On each side, the mixer adds five numbers, **many million times a second**. The beeper gives two of
them, one for each of its bits, and every other source one:

- **EAR**: 512 while port `$FE` bit 4 is 1, otherwise 0.
- **MIC**: 128 while the MIC line is 1, otherwise 0.
- **The AY chips**: 0 to 2,295, the three chips' channels on that side added together.
- **The DACs**: 0 to 2,040, that side's two channels (each 0–255) added and multiplied by 4.
- **The Pi**: 0 to 1,023, the side's 10-bit sample.

The sum is a 13-bit number, 0 to 8,191. Here is how much of that range each source can take:

Two things in the figure are worth a sentence each.

**The sum always fits.** Every source at its loudest adds up to 5,998, well below 8,191, so the mixer
never has to clip: nothing is lost even when every source plays at full volume at once.

**Silence is not zero.** Every input is a number from 0 upwards; nothing is ever subtracted. A DAC at
rest holds `$80`, the middle of its range, so that a sample can swing both ways from it; the two on
each side add 1,024 units even when no program has touched them. The Pi's rest level is the
middle of its range too, 512. So a silent Next sits at 1,536, and every sound moves up and down around
whatever constant level its sources add. The background page's part on
[mixing](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/#several-sounds-at-once-are-added-together)
explains why that does no harm: a capacitor after the converters removes any constant level before the
sound reaches a speaker. Article 7, on the mixer, takes all of this apart.

- **The audio jack** gets each side through a **one-bit converter**: a single pin of the FPGA switched
  on and off at 28 MHz, more often "on" the larger the number, and a resistor and a capacitor on the
  board that smooth the pulses into a voltage. It is the converter the background page calls
  [delta-sigma](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/#sound-from-a-single-bit).
- **The internal speaker** gets one mono signal, the left and right sums added together, through one
  more converter of the same kind. The **Peripheral 3 Setting register, NextReg `$08`**, bit 4 switches
  it on (and it is on after power-on). With bit 6 of the **Peripheral 2 Setting register, NextReg
  `$06`**, also set, the speaker plays only the beeper, and EAR and MIC leave the mix: a program can
  click keys and beep alerts on the speaker without disturbing the music in the headphones.
- **HDMI audio** sends the sums as numbers, 48,000 samples a second, each a 16-bit word, beside the
  picture on the HDMI cable. Bit 2 of the **Peripheral 4 Setting register, NextReg `$09`**, silences it.
- **The Raspberry Pi** receives the sums over the I2S link, when the link is set up to send.

One line goes out separately: **the MIC line**, port `$FE` bit 3 combined with the tape input and the
Pi's EAR bit, drives the MIC socket, the tape recorder's input. That is how a program saves to tape.

The jack and the speaker have no sample rate: their converters follow the sum as it changes, 28 million
times a second. Only HDMI samples it, because HDMI carries sound as numbers. Article 7 explains each
output.

<figcaption>The clocks behind the sound. The AY chips run from a fixed fraction of the master clock; the beeper and the DACs run at whatever speed the program's loops run.</figcaption>
</figure>

**The AY chips' clock is fixed.** The master clock divided by 16 gives **1.75 MHz**, close to the 1.77 MHz
of the 128K's AY, and each chip divides that by 8 again: its counters step 218,750 times a second. A tone's
pitch is that rate divided by twice the channel's period, so period 109 gives 218,750 ÷ 218 = 1,003 Hz,
and the A of 440 Hz needs a period of 249 (439.3 Hz, as close as a whole number allows). Neither the
CPU's speed nor anything else a program does changes these clocks, so AY music plays at the same pitch at
3.5 MHz and at 28 MHz.

**The beeper and the DACs follow the CPU.** They change only when an instruction writes them, so their
timing is the program's: a loop that flips port `$FE` bit 4 every 1,760 T-states makes 994 Hz at
3.5 MHz and twice that, 1,989 Hz, at 7 MHz. The Next's CPU can run at 3.5, 7, 14 or 28 MHz
(the **CPU Speed register, NextReg `$07`**), so a beeper tune written for the 48K plays an octave higher
at 7 MHz, and a sample player plays its samples faster. Contention, which stops the CPU at certain
moments when it touches the screen memory at 3.5 MHz (see
[ZX Spectrum: How the Screen Is Drawn](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/)), also
stretches a loop that runs in that memory, and its note comes out lower. A DMA transfer timed by its own prescaler, or the Copper, does not depend on the CPU's speed
(article 5).

A third rate, **48,000 samples a second**, belongs to HDMI only: it is how the finished sum is sent over
the cable, not how any sound is made. The background page's part
[Fast enough, or it sounds wrong](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/#fast-enough-or-it-sounds-wrong)
explains why that rate is enough.

- **The Peripheral 2 Setting register, NextReg `$06`**: bit 6 sends the beeper only to the internal
  speaker (Part 5). Bits 1–0 are the AY chips' mode: `00` YM2149, `01` AY-3-8910, and `11` holds all
  three chips silent in reset. The register list also names a mode `10`, but the chips use only bit 0,
  so `10` behaves as YM2149.
- **The Peripheral 3 Setting register, NextReg `$08`**: bit 5 chooses how each chip's three channels are
  spread over the two sides (ABC or ACB), bit 4 switches the internal speaker on, bit 3 the DACs, bit 1
  TurboSound (with it off, only the selected chip plays, as on a 128K), and bit 0 the issue 2 keyboard,
  which changes what port `$FE` reads back from the MIC bit.
- **The Peripheral 4 Setting register, NextReg `$09`**: bits 7–5 put AY chips 2, 1 and 0 in mono, bit 2
  silences HDMI audio.
- **NextReg `$84`**, one byte of the four **Internal Port Decoding Enables registers, NextRegs
  `$82`–`$85`**: bit 0 enables the AY's ports, bits 1–7 the seven families of DAC ports, from Soundrive
  to Specdrum. Some of those ports collide with other devices (a joystick, the mouse); these bits decide
  who answers.
- **The PI I2S Audio Control register, NextReg `$A2`**: bits 7–6 switch the Pi's audio on (left, right
  or both), bit 4 sets which way the data travels on its two pins, bits 3–2 mute each side, and bit 0
  sends the Pi's stream to the tape input.

After power-on, the AY chips are in YM mode with TurboSound off, the internal speaker is on, the DACs are
off and held at `$80`, every port family is enabled, HDMI audio plays and the Pi's audio is off: a
machine that sounds exactly like a 128K until something asks for more.

## Part 9: How Klive does it

Klive's Next core is a C program compiled to WebAssembly, written to behave like the hardware (see
[The ZX Spectrum Next Core and the FPGA](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-core/)). Its sound
follows the hardware's plan source by source.

### One file per source

Each source is a device of its own that keeps the same state as the hardware: the beeper the EAR and
MIC bits and the moments they changed, the AY chips their registers and counters, stepped by the
1.75 MHz clock, the DACs their four channels and the port decoding, the Pi receiver the words latched
from the link. The Pi itself is not emulated: in its place, a **sample source** (a WAV file or a tone,
set with the [`pi-audio` command](/en/reference/commands/#pi-audio)) feeds samples into the Next's
receiving half of the link, frame by frame.

### One stream of samples

The hardware's mixer adds its inputs continuously. Klive adds them only at the moments it needs a
sample: while a frame runs, every 28,000,000 ÷ 48,000 ≈ 583 master clocks at the usual rate of 48,000
samples a second, it asks each source for its level at that moment, adds them as the mixer does, and
stores the result. The frame's samples go to the host's sound card, which plays them while the next
frame runs. Article 8 follows that path, and what happens to it at a breakpoint.

Klive's sum keeps the hardware's **proportions**, but not its zero:

- The DACs and the Pi are taken about their rest levels (`$80`, 512), so a silent DAC adds 0 rather than
  1,024.
- The beeper's EAR and MIC pass through a filter that removes their constant level, as the capacitor
  after the hardware's converters does.
- The AY chips stay as they are, 0 upwards.
- The sum is scaled to a signed 16-bit sample, the form a sound card takes.

You hear the same thing; a waveform drawn from Klive's samples sits at a different level than one
measured on the hardware, which is why this series' captures are drawn about their middle.

Klive makes **one** stream, the one the audio jack would play. It does not model the outputs separately:
the internal speaker, HDMI's silence bit and the converters are not emulated, and with NextReg `$06`
bit 6 set the beeper, which then belongs to the speaker alone, is not heard at all. The tape input's
sound is not added to the MIC line either.

## Summary

- The Next keeps the 48K's **beeper** (port `$FE` bit 4, EAR, and bit 3, MIC) and the 128K's **AY chip**,
  and adds two more AY chips (**TurboSound Next**), **four 8-bit DACs** and the **Raspberry Pi's audio**
  over an I2S link: four kinds of source.
- The MIC line also carries the **tape input**, so a loading tape is heard, and drives the MIC socket.
- Sources are **generators** (the AY chips play by themselves once set up) or **level writers** (the
  beeper and the DACs need a write for every step). That decides what a sound costs the CPU.
- One **mixer** adds every source on a **left** and a **right** side: EAR 512, MIC 128, the AY chips up
  to 2,295, the DACs up to 2,040, the Pi up to 1,023 units. The sum, at most 5,998, fits the 13-bit
  range of 8,191 without clipping. Silence is 1,536, not zero.
- The sums leave through the **audio jack** and the **internal speaker** (one-bit converters at 28 MHz)
  and **HDMI** (48,000 samples a second), and go to the **Pi**.
- The AY chips run from a fixed **1.75 MHz**; the beeper's and the DACs' timing is the **program's**, so
  it changes with the CPU's speed.
- NextRegs **`$06`, `$08`, `$09`, `$84` and `$A2`** switch the parts on and off; after power-on the
  Next sounds like a 128K.
- Klive emulates each source in its own file and **adds them at the moments it takes a sample**, with
  the hardware's proportions, centred and scaled to 16 bits, into one stream.
