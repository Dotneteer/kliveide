# ZX Spectrum Next: The Beeper, EAR and MIC

Manic Miner plays a two-voice tune on a machine whose sound is a single bit. The same bit's
neighbour tells the tape recorder what to save, and the same port reads back what the tape plays.
How does one bit make music, and how do music and data share one wire?

The beeper is the oldest sound on the ZX Spectrum Next, and still the one every Spectrum program
knows. A 48K game's music and effects, the click of a key in BASIC, the screech of a program
loading, and every program ever saved to cassette all go through it. A Next runs those programs
unchanged, so it has to reproduce the beeper exactly: the levels, the timing, and the small details
of what a program reads back, which some old programs depend on.

This is article 2 of the series; it follows
[The Big Picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-audio/overview/), which introduces the
four kinds of sound source. The background page
[How a Computer Makes Sound](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/computer-sound/) explains the
square wave and sound from a single bit;
[ZX Spectrum: How the Screen Is Drawn](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/)
explains port `$FE`, which the beeper shares with the border, and contention. Where the beeper's
levels go after the mixer is in [The Mixer and the Outputs](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-audio/mixer/).

- **Bit 4, EAR**, is the beeper: the bit a program switches on and off to make a sound.
- **Bit 3, MIC**, is the tape output: the bit a program switches to save data to tape. It is heard
  too, more quietly.
- **Bits 2–0** are the border colour.

Bits 7–5 of a write are ignored. A reset clears the port: EAR and MIC start low, so a machine that has
just been switched on is silent.

There are two sound bits because the 48K had two outputs with two jobs, sharing one circuit. Its ULA
has a single pin for the tape sockets and the speaker: bits 4 and 3 drive it through resistors of
different values, and the signal from the EAR socket arrives on the same pin. That is why MIC is quieter
than EAR, and why what a program writes to bits 4 and 3 shows up when it reads the port back (Part 6).
The Next has no such pin: everything is logic inside its FPGA. It keeps the two bits, their loudness
and the read-back rules, so that the programs that relied on them still work.

Reading port `$FE` returns the keyboard in bits 4–0 (a 0 for each key held in the rows the address
selects), **bit 6, the tape input**, and 1 in bits 7 and 5. With no key held and nothing on bit 6, a
read gives `$BF`.

## Part 2: EAR and MIC in the mixer

The mixer adds each bit as a fixed level, the same on the left and the right side:

- **EAR** adds **512** while port `$FE` bit 4 is 1, and 0 while it is 0.
- **MIC** adds **128** while the MIC line is 1, and 0 while it is 0. The MIC line is bit 3 combined
  with the tape input (Part 5); with no tape playing it is just bit 3.

The two bits together give four levels, 0, 128, 512 and 640. Drawn on the mixer's whole range they are
a small part of it:

EAR is four times as loud as MIC, the ratio the 48K's two resistors gave them. The beeper is not a quiet
source next to the others: its 512 is twice what one AY channel at full volume adds (255), and about a
quarter of the DACs' largest swing on one side. Nor does it rest in the middle, as the DACs do: its
silence is 0, so the first time a program sets bit 4, the sum jumps by 512, and the speaker clicks, as it
did on the 48K (the mixer article's part [Silence is 1,536](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-audio/mixer/#part-2-silence-is-1536)
explains why the other sources do not click).

## Part 3: A note from a loop

The beeper holds whatever level it was given last. To play a note, the program switches bit 4 on and off
at the note's pitch, and it has to do the switching itself, at exactly the right moments. The usual way is
a loop that counts the time between two writes in T-states. Here is the loop behind the overview's
beeper capture:

```z80klive
        di                  ; no interrupts: they would stretch the loop
        ld c,$fe
        ld a,$10            ; bit 4 set: EAR on
Loop:   out (c),a           ; 12 T-states   write EAR
        xor $10             ;  7            the next write's EAR is the opposite
        ld b,133            ;  7
Dly:    djnz Dly            ; 13 × 132 + 8  wait
        jp Loop             ; 10
```

How it works:

1. `out (c),a` writes the EAR bit; the speaker's cone moves.
2. `xor $10` flips bit 4 of A, so the next write is the opposite level.
3. `djnz` counts B down from 133. Each round that jumps back costs 13 T-states and the last one 8, so the
   wait is 13 × 132 + 8 = 1,724 T-states.
4. One pass of the loop is 12 + 7 + 7 + 1,724 + 10 = **1,760 T-states**: half a period of the note,
   since it takes two passes to go up and come back down.
5. The loop closes with `jp Loop` rather than `jr Loop`. A `jr` would be a byte shorter (2 bytes
   against 3) but slower: it takes 12 T-states where `jp` takes 10. In a timing loop the speed is what
   counts, and every T-state is part of the note: with `jr`, a pass would be 1,762 T-states and the note
   993 Hz.

At 3.5 MHz, 1,760 T-states take 1,760 ÷ 3,500,000 = 503 µs. A whole period is twice that, so the note is
3,500,000 ÷ (2 × 1,760) = **994 Hz**:

The loop's length is the only thing that sets the pitch, and it changes in steps of 13 T-states, one round
of `djnz`. B = 132 gives 1,747 T-states and 1,002 Hz, B = 134 gives 1,773 and 987 Hz: a program cannot hit
every pitch exactly, and a beeper tune is slightly out of tune wherever its notes fall between those steps.
A loop with a finer step (another instruction of 4 T-states in the wait, for example) gets closer.

### The CPU's speed and contention

Because the pitch is a count of T-states, it is only as steady as the CPU's clock. Two things change how
long the same loop takes:

- **The CPU's speed.** The Next's CPU runs at 3.5, 7, 14 or 28 MHz, set by the **CPU Speed register,
  NextReg `$07`**. A T-state at 7 MHz lasts half as long, so the same loop plays twice as high: 1,989 Hz,
  an octave up. A 48K beeper tune played with the CPU at a higher speed is too high and too fast.
- **Contention.** At 3.5 MHz, the Next stops the CPU for a few T-states when it reads memory from
  `$4000` to `$7FFF` while the screen is being drawn, as the 48K's ULA did (see
  [Contention](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/spectrum-display/#contention)). A loop that runs in that
  memory gets longer while the beam is on a line of the picture, and its note drops while it is. The
  **Peripheral 3 Setting register, NextReg `$08`**, bit 6 switches contention off, and at 7 MHz and above
  there is none.

Contention does not lower the note; it **breaks it in two**. It happens only while the beam is on one of
the picture's 192 lines, not in the border above and below, so the loop runs at two speeds in every frame:

- In the border, about 8 ms of each 20 ms frame, a pass takes its 1,760 T-states, and the note is 994 Hz.
- On the picture's lines, about 12 ms of each frame, the CPU is held up again and again, and in this capture a
  pass takes about 2,007 T-states: 872 Hz.

So the pitch drops and comes back 50 times a second, and what the ear hears is a rough, warbling tone, not a
lower note. Counting every period of a frame and dividing by 20 ms would give about 918 Hz, but that is only
arithmetic: no pass of the loop ever plays it.

How much a pass is stretched depends on the loop, not only on contention. Each of its memory accesses is held
up by up to 6 T-states, depending on where it falls in the ULA's repeating pattern of 8 T-states, and a pass
does not fall on the pattern the same way each time. Another loop, or the same loop at another address, is
stretched by a different amount; the 2,007 T-states are this loop's. What every such loop shares is the
shape: full speed in the border, slower on the picture's lines.

This is why beeper music on the Spectrum usually runs from `$8000` and up, and with interrupts off: an
interrupt would take the CPU away from the loop every 20 ms and leave a gap in the note.

## Part 4: Two voices from one bit

A square wave is one voice. Two square waves at once cannot simply be written to one bit: when one voice
wants the bit high and the other wants it low, there is no level in between to give. The trick that
beeper music uses instead is to make each voice a train of **short pulses**, one at the start of each of
its periods, and to keep the bit low the rest of the time. Pulses this short rarely need the bit at the
same moment, so the bit can carry both trains, and a train of pulses at a voice's pitch sounds at that
pitch just as a square wave does.

### A program

This program plays two voices. Every pass of its loop gives each voice one fixed **slot** of time, and
in its slot a voice either switches EAR on or leaves it off. Each voice keeps an 8-bit counter, its
**phase**, and adds its **step** to it on every pass; when the addition carries out of 8 bits, the voice
has finished a period and takes its slot with EAR on:

```z80klive
        ld hl,0             ; H, L: the two voices' phases
        ld de,$0405         ; D, E: their steps, 4 and 5
Loop:   ld a,h              ;  4
        add a,d             ;  4    voice 1's phase + its step
        ld h,a              ;  4
        sbc a,a             ;  4    $FF if that carried, 0 if not
        and $10             ;  7    ... so A is EAR on or off
        out ($fe),a         ; 11    voice 1's slot begins
        ld a,l              ;  4
        add a,e             ;  4    the same for voice 2
        ld l,a              ;  4
        sbc a,a             ;  4
        and $10             ;  7
        out ($fe),a         ; 11    voice 2's slot begins
        ld a,0              ;  7
        nop                 ;  4    four nops: voice 2's slot is
        nop                 ;  4    as long as voice 1's
        nop                 ;  4
        nop                 ;  4
        out ($fe),a         ; 11    both slots over: EAR off
        jp Loop             ; 10
```

How it works:

1. Every instruction always takes the same time, whatever the phases hold: `sbc a,a` turns the carry
   into a mask instead of a jump. One pass is always **112 T-states**, so the loop runs
   3,500,000 ÷ 112 = 31,250 times a second.
2. Voice 1's slot runs from its `out` to voice 2's `out`, 34 T-states (23 for the five instructions in
   between and 11 for the `out` itself). Voice 2's slot runs from its `out` to the last one, also 34. The
   remaining 44 T-states of the pass, from the last `out` round to the first, EAR is off.
3. Voice 1's phase gains 4 each pass, so it carries once every 256 ÷ 4 = 64 passes: its pitch is
   31,250 × 4 ÷ 256 = **488.3 Hz**. Voice 2's gains 5, and carries every 51 or 52 passes: on average
   31,250 × 5 ÷ 256 = **610.4 Hz**. The two notes are a major third apart, a ratio of exactly 5 to 4.
4. The slots of a pass follow each other and never overlap, so the bit is exactly the two pulse trains
   added together.

Each note comes with **overtones**, pitches at whole multiples of it (977 and 1,221 Hz are the second
ones). A train of pulses this short has overtones almost as strong as the note itself, many of them, up
far beyond this figure; they give the beeper's chords their buzzing, nasal colour. What lies
between the notes is close to nothing. Voice 2's small unevenness, a period of 51 passes here and 52
there, spreads its peak a little but does not move it.

### What it costs

- **Loudness.** A voice is on for 34 of every 7,168 T-states (voice 1's period of 64 passes), less than
  half a percent of the time. Short pulses are quiet: the louder a voice should be, the longer its slot,
  so a program sets the **volume of a voice by the width of its pulses**. Two voices need two slots that
  never meet, so each can have at most half the pass.
- **Pitch.** A voice's pitch is the loop's rate times its step divided by 256. Finer pitches need a
  16-bit phase, which costs more T-states per pass and lowers the rate.
- **The CPU.** While the tune plays, the CPU does nothing else: every T-state of the loop is counted.
  This is why beeper music on the 48K played on title screens and between levels, and why the 128K's AY
  chip, which plays by itself, was such a step forward.

## Part 5: Saving to tape

A program saves to tape by switching **port `$FE` bit 3**, MIC, in a pattern of pulses that encodes its
data. The **MIC socket** of the Next carries that pattern to a tape recorder, as the 48K's did.

The MIC line is not bit 3 alone. It is bit 3 combined with the tape input (Part 6) and the EAR bit that
the Raspberry Pi's audio link can produce (article 6) by an **exclusive or**: the line is 1 when an odd
number of the three are 1. The same line feeds both the MIC socket and the mixer's MIC input:

The exclusive or keeps each signal whole. With no tape playing, the MIC line is bit 3, exactly what the
program writes. While a tape plays and the program holds bit 3 still, the line follows the tape: if bit 3
is 1, upside down, which sounds the same and carries the same edges. The consequences:

- **A loading tape is heard**, at MIC's level of 128: the screech of a loading program comes out of the
  speaker, as it did on the 48K.
- **The MIC socket echoes the tape input**: a recorder plugged into it while another plays into EAR
  records what the Next hears.
- **A program saving data** switches bit 3 while no tape plays, and the socket carries its pulses
  unchanged.

## Part 6: Loading from tape

A tape recorder plays its signal into the **EAR socket**. What the Next reads from it is not the raw
voltage but a clean level, 0 or 1, produced by an **input stage** in two steps, both counted on the
master clock.

### Noise rejection

A tape's signal is analogue and noisy: near an edge it may cross the threshold several times in a row. The
first step, **noise rejection**, passes a new level on only when it has held steady for **two ticks of the
1.75 MHz clock**: any change starts the count again. Depending on where the ticks fall, that takes between
0.6 and 1.14 µs, so a pulse shorter than 0.6 µs never reaches the CPU and one longer than 1.14 µs always
does. A real edge arrives about a microsecond late, which no loader notices.

### Relaxation

The second step deals with a tape that stops on a 1. Some recordings, and some tape circuits, leave the
level at 1 after the last pulse instead of returning to 0, and a loader waiting for the next edge would see
the bit stuck. The original machines did not have that problem: the 48K's input relaxed back to 0 after
at most about 800 µs, and the ZX81 expects it to within about 1,300 µs. The Next's **relaxation** step
copies them: a 1 that lasts 64 ticks of a 54,688 Hz clock (512 master clocks each), between 1.15 and
1.17 ms depending on where the ticks fall, turns into a 0, until the input goes low and high again.

The relaxation cuts only highs longer than about 1.15 ms, so a tone with half-periods shorter than
that, above about 430 Hz, passes untouched. A Spectrum tape is far quicker: its pilot tone's pulses are
2,168 T-states long, 619 µs, and its data pulses shorter still. The result of both steps is the **tape
input**, the level that port `$FE` bit 6 reads and that the MIC line carries (Part 5).

### What port `$FE` bit 6 reads

Bit 6 is not the tape input alone. It is 1 when any of these is true at the moment of the read:

1. **The tape input**, combined by exclusive or with the Pi's EAR bit, is 1.
2. **Port `$FE` bit 4**, EAR, was last written as 1.
3. **Port `$FE` bit 3**, MIC, was last written as 1, and the **issue 2 keyboard** is on: the Peripheral 3
   Setting register, NextReg `$08`, bit 0 (0 after power-on).

So a loader must keep EAR at 0 while it reads the tape, or bit 6 reads 1 whatever the tape plays; the
48K ROM's loader does, and keeps MIC at 1. And bit 6 follows the bits at once: the read straight after a write that clears
bit 4 already returns 0. On a 48K, the shared pin of Part 1 is an analogue line that takes a moment to
fall, so a read that comes very soon after the write can still find a 1; the Next's bit 6 is logic, and
has no such delay.

### The issue 2 keyboard

The 48K was built in several board revisions, called issues. On the early **issue 2** boards, writing a 1
to the MIC bit was enough to make bit 6 read 1; from **issue 3** on, only the EAR bit did. Software written
on an issue 2 machine sometimes compared the whole byte read from port `$FE` against a fixed value, with
bit 6 assumed to be 1, instead of testing the key bits alone. On a later machine bit 6 read 0, the
comparison failed, and the program saw keys that were not pressed, or ignored the ones that were.

The Next behaves as issue 3 after power-on, as most Spectrums did. NextReg `$08` bit 0 gives such a program
the issue 2 machine it was written for: with it set, the MIC bit reads back in bit 6 too.

The setting has a price. The 48K ROM's loader holds MIC at 1 the whole time it listens, so with the issue 2
keyboard on, bit 6 reads 1 throughout, the loader never sees an edge, and loading from tape fails. A program
that needs the setting should switch it on after it has loaded, not before.

## Part 8: How Klive does it

Klive's Next core is a C program compiled to WebAssembly; the beeper, the port and the tape input are
three parts of it.

### The beeper

Every write to port `$FE` that changes EAR or the MIC line is recorded with the moment it happened, to the
T-state. When the mixer needs a sample, the beeper works out what share of the sample's time each of the
four levels lasted, so an edge that falls in the middle of a sample gives it a level in between, as the
smoothing after the real converter would. For the HDMI output (Machine › Sound › Output) it takes the level
at the sample's moment instead, as HDMI does (the mixer article's part
[HDMI audio](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-audio/mixer/#part-6-hdmi-audio)).

The mixer adds EAR as 512 units and MIC as 128, and passes them through a filter that removes their
constant level slowly, as the capacitor after the real jack does; that is why the captures in this article
move around zero. With NextReg `$06` bit 6 set and the speaker on, EAR and MIC are left out of the jack's and
HDMI's mix and play on the internal speaker output alone.

### Port `$FE`

A write stores EAR, MIC and the border colour; a read builds bit 6 from the tape input, the Pi's EAR bit,
the stored EAR bit and, with NextReg `$08` bit 0 set, the stored MIC bit, at the moment of the read. There is
no analogue delay: an emulator of the 48K would model the slow fall of its shared pin, but the Next has none.

### The tape input

Klive's tape deck plays a `.tap` or `.tzx` file into the Next's EAR socket (see
[Tapes](/en/guides/media/tapes/)). Its level goes through the input stage of Part 6, counted exactly as the
hardware counts it, one tick of the 1.75 MHz clock at a time; the stage is brought up to date only when
something looks at it (an instruction, a read of port `$FE`, a sample of sound), and skips ahead while
nothing plays. The result feeds port `$FE` bit 6 and the MIC line, so a loading tape is heard as on the real
machine. The deck also loads quickly, by taking over the 48K ROM's loading routine when the program calls
it, and records what the program saves through MIC into a new tape file.

## Summary

- **Port `$FE`** (any even port) holds the beeper: **bit 4, EAR**, and **bit 3, MIC**, the tape output,
  beside the border in bits 2–0. Reset clears them.
- In the mixer, EAR adds **512** and MIC **128** to both sides, 4 to 1 as on the 48K. Their silence is 0,
  so the first switch-on clicks.
- A program plays a note with a **timing loop**: two passes of the loop are one period, so 1,760 T-states
  per pass at 3.5 MHz is **994 Hz**. Pitches come in steps of the loop's smallest change. The **CPU's
  speed** (NextReg `$07`) scales the pitch; **contention** (3.5 MHz only, NextReg `$08` bit 6) slows the
  loop only while the picture's lines are drawn, so the pitch drops and comes back every frame.
- **Two voices** share the bit as trains of **short pulses** in slots that never meet; each voice's pitch
  is how often its pulses come, its loudness their width. The CPU does nothing else meanwhile.
- The **MIC line** is bit 3 exclusive-or the tape input and the Pi's EAR bit; it feeds the **MIC socket**
  and the mixer, so a loading tape is heard and echoed.
- The **EAR socket** passes an **input stage**: pulses shorter than about **a microsecond** are rejected,
  and a 1 held for about **1.15–1.17 ms** relaxes to 0. **Bit 6** reads that tape input (exclusive-or the Pi's EAR bit), or
  the EAR bit, or, with the **issue 2 keyboard** (NextReg `$08` bit 0), the MIC bit; it follows them at once.
- NextReg `$08` bit 4 switches the **internal speaker** on; NextReg `$06` bit 6 moves the beeper to the
  speaker alone.
- **Klive** records each change with its T-state, mixes each sample's share of every level, filters the
  constant level away as the jack's capacitor does, and runs the tape through the same input stage.
