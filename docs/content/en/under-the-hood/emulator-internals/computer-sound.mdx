# How a Computer Makes Sound

A loudspeaker can do only one thing: push its cone out and pull it back in. Everything you have ever
heard from a computer, a beep, a chord, a voice, is a list of positions for that cone. How can a list
of numbers sound like a voice?

Sound is how a program talks to you without taking your eyes off the screen: music sets the mood of a
game, an effect tells you that you were hit, a beep says a key was pressed. On a home computer of the
ZX Spectrum's era it did one more job: programs were saved to and loaded from cassette tape as sound.

This page explains, in plain words, how a computer turns numbers into sound. It is not about any one
machine, and you do not need to know anything about electronics to read it. It is the background for
Klive's articles about how particular machines make their sound, which can then skip these basics.

## Sound is air pushed back and forth

Sound is a change in air pressure that travels. A loudspeaker's cone moves forward and squeezes the
air in front of it a little; it moves back and leaves the air a little thinner. These squeezed and
thinned bands spread out from the speaker and reach your ear, where they push your eardrum in and out
in the same rhythm.

Draw the pressure at one spot over time and you get a curve, the sound's **waveform**. Two things about
it decide most of what you hear:

- **How far the cone moves** is the **amplitude**, which you hear as loudness. A tall waveform is a
  loud sound.
- **How often the cone moves back and forth** is the **frequency**, which you hear as pitch. It is
  counted in **hertz** (Hz), cycles a second. The A that orchestras tune to is 440 Hz; human hearing
  reaches from about 20 Hz to about 20,000 Hz.

The shape of the waveform within each cycle is the sound's **timbre**: a flute and a violin playing the
same 440 Hz differ because their waveforms do.

A speaker moves its cone in proportion to the **voltage** on its wires: more voltage, further out.
So making a sound means making a voltage that changes in the shape of the waveform. The whole job of a
computer's sound hardware is to produce that voltage, and it does it in a chain of steps:

## A sample is one number for one moment

A computer cannot store a smooth curve. It can store only numbers, so it records the waveform's height
at regular moments, one number each time. Each number is a **sample**, and the list of them is the
sound in digital form.

Two settings decide how good the list is:

- The **sample rate** is how many samples are taken each second. More samples follow the curve more
  closely.
- The **resolution** is how many different values one sample can have. It is usually given in bits:
  an 8-bit sample has 256 possible values, a 16-bit sample 65,536. More values mean that each sample
  can be closer to the real height of the curve.

A music CD is the everyday example: it stores 44,100 samples a second for each of its two sides (left
and right), each sample 16 bits. That is enough for every pitch a human can hear.

## From a number to a voltage: the DAC

To play the list back, the computer needs a voltage for each number. The chip that makes it is a
**digital-to-analogue converter**, a **DAC**. You give it a number, and its output becomes the
voltage that belongs to that number: 0 gives the lowest voltage, the largest number the highest, and
every number in between a voltage in between.

A DAC does nothing between two samples. It **holds** its voltage until the next number arrives, then
jumps to the new one. So its output is not a smooth curve but a **staircase**: flat steps, each one sample
long, with a jump at every sample.

The steps are easiest to see when the resolution is low. In the next figure, each sample has only
16 possible values (4 bits), and the wave is played back at 16,000 samples a second, the faster of the
two rates above. The orange staircase is what the DAC delivers:

## From a staircase to a smooth wave: the filter

The steps change far faster than anything in the wanted sound. In the figure, the note goes up and down
once every millisecond, but the DAC jumps 16 times in that millisecond. That difference in speed is
what makes it easy to get rid of the steps.

A **low-pass filter** lets slow changes through and holds back fast ones. In its simplest form it is just
a resistor and a capacitor: the capacitor has to be charged through the resistor, which takes time, so
the voltage across it cannot follow a sudden jump. It moves towards the new level gradually. A slow
change, like the 1,000 Hz note, has time to come through almost whole; the sudden jumps of the steps
are rounded off.

That is the green curve in the figure above: the staircase after a filter of two resistor-and-capacitor
stages. The steps are gone, and what remains is a smooth wave. It comes out a little smaller and about a
tenth of a millisecond behind the staircase, because the filter takes time to follow; neither matters
to the ear.

**This is the step that makes the result analogue.** Before the filter, the voltage can only stand on a
fixed set of steps, at fixed moments. After it, the voltage changes continuously, like the air pressure
it will become. The speaker helps, too: its cone has weight and cannot follow very fast changes, so it
smooths what the filter leaves over, and our ears do not hear very high frequencies at all.

## Fast enough, or it sounds wrong

How many samples a second are enough? The rule is simple: **the sample rate must be more than twice the
highest frequency in the sound**. A wave that goes up and down needs at least one sample on its way up
and one on its way down in every cycle, or the samples cannot tell what it did.

If the rule is broken, the result is not merely a rough copy of the high note. It is a **different,
lower note** that was never in the original. This is called **aliasing**: the samples of the fast wave
happen to lie exactly on a slow one, and the DAC and the filter faithfully play the slow one.

The fast blue wave goes through seven cycles in the millisecond, but the samples catch it only
eight times. Each sample lands seven eighths of a cycle after the one before, which gives the same
height as landing an eighth of a cycle *before* it, so the samples slowly walk backwards through the
wave. The dots therefore describe one slow cycle of 1,000 Hz,
the difference between the sample rate and the tone (8,000 − 7,000), and that is what comes out of the
speaker. No filter after the DAC can undo it: the samples themselves are a correct record of a 1,000 Hz
wave.

So a sound must be free of frequencies above half the sample rate *before* it is sampled. The CD's rate
of 44,100 samples a second was chosen with this in mind: half of it, 22,050 Hz, is just above the
highest pitch anyone hears.

## Sound from a single bit

The simplest sound hardware has no DAC at all: just one bit, which the computer switches on or off,
and which drives the speaker fully out or fully in. The beeper of the ZX Spectrum works like this.
How can that make music?

**A note from switching.** Switch the bit on and off at a steady pace, and the cone goes out and in at
that pace: a **square wave**. The pace is the pitch. To play the 440 Hz A, the program switches the bit
880 times a second, on and off once in each cycle of 2.27 milliseconds. It sounds harsher than a smooth
wave, because the square shape has a strong timbre of its own, but it is clearly a note. The program
has to time every switch itself, so while it plays, it usually does little else.

**Any level from switching fast.** One bit can do more than square waves. Switch it much faster than
any note, faster than the speaker and the ear can follow, and vary *how long it stays on* in each round.
Then the filter from earlier, and the speaker's own sluggishness, average the pulses out: a bit that
is on a quarter of the time gives a quarter of the full level, half of the time gives half.

The green curve is the blue pulse train after a filter, the same kind of smoothing the DAC's staircase
went through. Each time the on-time changes, it moves over to the new average and settles at about a
quarter, a half and three quarters of the full level, wobbling slightly with every pulse. With pulses fast enough, the wobble is far above hearing.

This is called **pulse-width modulation**: the width of each pulse carries the level. Change the widths
in the shape of a waveform, and the averaged output follows that waveform, so one bit can play any
sound a DAC could. Many modern converters are built on the same idea. A **delta-sigma converter**
switches its single output bit millions of times a second, each time checking whether the output so
far has been above or below the wanted level and switching to make up the difference. The
filter after it turns the stream of bits into a smooth voltage. The filter is what makes either kind
work.

## Several sounds at once are added together

Music has many voices at once, and a game plays its effects over its music. The air in front of a
speaker can be in only one state at a time, so several sounds can only reach it as one waveform. That
waveform is simply their **sum**: at every moment, the levels of all the sounds are added together.
In a computer, mixing is adding the numbers (or, in the electronics, adding the voltages).

Adding has one catch. A DAC, an amplifier or a speaker can deliver only a certain range of levels. When
the sum goes past the top or the bottom of the range, the output stays at the limit and the tip of the
wave is cut off flat. This is **clipping**, marked in red in the figure, and it sounds like a harsh
crackle. A designer avoids it by leaving **headroom**: making each source quiet enough that the loudest
possible sum still fits.

Many sound sources never go below zero: a level of 0 is "off", and the wave moves above it. Their sum
then always contains a constant extra level, an **offset**, around which the sound moves. A speaker
cannot use a constant level, it would only push the cone out and keep it there, so a **capacitor** in
the path removes it. A capacitor lets changes through but not a level that stays the same, and what
reaches the speaker is the sound moving around zero.

## Back to the computer

The whole chain, from the first figure, now reads:

**numbers → DAC → filter → amplifier → speaker → air**

The numbers can come from two kinds of place:

- **The CPU writes them.** The program works out each sample and hands it to a DAC (or switches a single
  bit), on time, every time. That can play anything, speech or recorded drums, but it costs the CPU work
  for every sample.
- **A sound chip makes them.** The program tells the chip "play this pitch at this loudness", and the
  chip's own counters produce the waveform from then on, until the program changes the settings. That
  frees the CPU, but the sound is limited to what the chip can generate.

Most computers of the 1980s had one or both. Their sounds go through the same chain: a mixer that adds
them, a filter, an amplifier and a speaker. The articles on particular machines start from here.
