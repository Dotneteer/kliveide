# Putting It Together: Four Layers, One Colour

Four candidates for every dot, and one winner. The rules that pick it fit on a page. Yet they give the
Next layers that can swap places on every line, stencils that cut one picture to the shape of another,
colours that add up across layers, and a single Layer 2 colour that can climb above everything else on
the screen. Not bad for a page of rules, is it?

Why should you care who wins? Because that is what your player sees. Whether your hero walks in front
of the hills or behind them, whether the score panel stays readable over a busy backdrop, whether a
sprite disappears behind the border: every one of those is decided here, dot by dot. Get the rules
right, and the layers work for you. Get them wrong, and you spend an evening wondering where your
spaceship went.

Read the [big picture chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) first: it
introduces the two answers every layer gives for every dot, and the pipeline that carries them. The
[palettes chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/) explains how each
layer's colour and transparency are made. Here we take those answers as given and follow what **stage
2** of the pipeline does with them: the step that turns four answers into one colour.

## The road ahead

We start with a look at stage 2 as a whole: what goes in, and what comes out. Then come the four
reasons a layer can have nothing to show at a dot. Next, the ULA and the tilemap join forces and
become one layer, either one above the other or as a stencil that cuts each to the other's shape.
After that comes the heart of the matter, the six orders of the three remaining layers, seen on the
machine; if you read only one section, make it that one. Two exceptions follow, the Layer 2 priority
bit and sprites over the border. Then blending, where the Next adds colours instead of choosing one,
and what that is good for in a real game. We finish with what happens when nothing at all is opaque,
and how Klive does the same, rule for rule.

## Four answers, one colour

For one dot, stage 2 gets four colours, one from each layer: the ULA (or LoRes), the tilemap, Layer 2
and the sprites. With each comes the layer's answer to "am I transparent here?" and a few flags. The
ULA says whether its dot is border, the tilemap whether the tile is below the ULA, and Layer 2 whether
its palette entry carries the priority bit.

From these it makes one 9-bit colour in two steps. First the ULA and the tilemap are **mixed into one
layer**, called **U**. Then the three remaining layers, **S** (sprites), **L** (Layer 2) and **U**, are
put **in order**, and the first one that is not transparent wins. Two settings replace the ordering
with **blending**, and when nothing at all is opaque, the **fallback colour** fills the dot.

One more thing worth seeing. All the settings stage 2 uses, the order, the stencil and blend bits, the
transparency and fallback colours, are taken once for each dot as it enters the pipeline, and they
travel with it. So your program, or the Copper, can change them between two dots of the same line.
The change takes effect from the next dot on, never half-way through one.

## When a layer is transparent

A layer is transparent at a dot for one of four reasons, and stage 2 does not tell them apart:

| Layer | Switched off by | Its clip window | Its own test: transparent when |
|---|---|---|---|
| ULA (and LoRes) | NextReg `$68` bit 7 | NextReg `$1A` | its colour equals the value in NextReg `$14` |
| Tilemap | NextReg `$6B` bit 7 clear | NextReg `$1B` | its dot equals the value in NextReg `$4C` (text mode: its colour equals the value in NextReg `$14`) |
| Layer 2 | port `$123B` bit 1 clear | NextReg `$18` | its colour equals the value in NextReg `$14` |
| Sprites | NextReg `$15` bit 0 clear | NextReg `$19` | no sprite covers the dot, or its dot equals the value in NextReg `$4B` |

Two things in this table are easy to miss. First, the ULA's colour test applies to its border as much
as to its picture: a border whose colour equals the value in the Global Transparency Colour register,
NextReg `$14`, is transparent too. Second, switching the ULA off with bit 7 of the ULA Control
register, NextReg `$68`, removes it **as a layer**, but not as a source for blending. Your program can
hide the ULA and still blend Layer 2 with it, as you will see in the blending section.

## The ULA and the tilemap become one

The ULA and the tilemap are mixed first, into the single layer U that takes part in the ordering. They
are a pair in the hardware too: they read bank 5 through the same memory port, and their palettes
share one palette memory. A program often uses the tilemap as a text or tile screen in front of the
Spectrum picture, or behind it.

Which of the two is in front is decided **tile by tile**. Bit 0 of a tile's attribute byte puts the
tile **below the ULA**; bit 0 of the Tilemap Control register, NextReg `$6B`, overrides every tile and
puts the whole tilemap on top. (In 512-tile mode bit 0 of the attribute is the ninth bit of the tile
number, so there every tile is below the ULA unless NextReg `$6B` bit 0 says otherwise.)

The rule is then the same as for any two layers: the upper one shows where it is opaque, the lower one
where the upper is transparent, and U is transparent only where both are.

## Stencil mode: each layer cuts the other

Bit 0 of the ULA Control register, NextReg `$68`, turns the mix into something else: a **stencil**.
With both the ULA and the tilemap switched on, U is opaque only where **both** are opaque, and its
colour is their two colours combined bit by bit with **AND**. Each layer cuts the other to its own
shape: a tilemap of solid colour shows only through the ULA's ink, or the ULA's picture only inside
the tiles.

The AND also changes the colours. A white dot (all ones) lets the other layer's colour through
unchanged, a black one (all zeros) gives black, and anything between keeps only the bits both share.
So a stencil can tint as well as cut. Two jobs for the price of one bit.

## Six orders

Three layers are left, S, L and U, and bits 4–2 of the Sprite and Layers System register, **NextReg
`$15`**, choose how they are stacked. The numbers fit neatly. Three bits make **8 combinations**.
Three layers can be stacked in **3 × 2 × 1 = 6 orders**: any of the three on top, either of the other
two in the middle, and the last one at the bottom. So six of the eight values, 000 to 101, are the six
orders, and the **two left over**, 110 and 111, are not orders at all. They are the two blend modes,
which get a section of their own below.

Why six, and not one fixed order? Because games want different things. A game with a Layer 2 backdrop
and a Spectrum-style status bar wants the ULA in front of Layer 2 (SUL, USL). A game with a tile
playfield and Layer 2 effects on top of it wants Layer 2 in front of the tilemap (SLU, LSU). A game
whose sprites walk behind scenery wants the sprites below (LSU, LUS, ULS). And because the order is
taken for every dot, your program can change it at a chosen line, with the line interrupt or the
Copper, and have different orders in different bands of the screen.

Let's see all six on the machine. Four points are marked where two layers overlap: **A** a sprite on a
hill, **B** the ULA's score panel on the edge of a hill, **C** a platform tile on a hill, and **D** a
sprite standing on the ground of tiles.

Here are the winners at the four dots:

| Order | A: sprite / Layer 2 | B: ULA / Layer 2 | C: tile / Layer 2 | D: sprite / tile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SLU | sprite | Layer 2 | Layer 2 | sprite |
| LSU | Layer 2 | Layer 2 | Layer 2 | sprite |
| SUL | sprite | ULA | tile | sprite |
| LUS | Layer 2 | Layer 2 | Layer 2 | tile |
| USL | sprite | ULA | tile | tile |
| ULS | Layer 2 | ULA | tile | tile |

Read a column and you read one pair of layers: at A the sprite wins exactly where S is above L, at D
exactly where S is above U. Read a row and you see that an order is nothing more than three such
decisions. That's all there is to it, really.

## The Layer 2 priority bit

The order has two exceptions, and here is the first. A Layer 2 palette entry can carry a **priority
bit** (bit 7 of the second write to the 9-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$44`, as the
[palettes chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/#nine-bits-of-colour)
explains). A Layer 2 dot whose entry has it, and which is not transparent, goes **above every other
layer**, whatever the order says. It works in every order where Layer 2 is not on top already: SLU,
SUL, USL and ULS, and in the blend modes. In LSU and LUS it changes nothing, because Layer 2 is on top
anyway.

The bit belongs to the **palette entry**, not to the dots, and that is what makes it so precise. One
colour of a Layer 2 picture can be put in front of the sprites, a window frame or a foreground column,
while the rest of the picture stays behind them, without splitting the picture into two layers. I find
this one of the neatest tricks in the whole video system: one bit, and a picture gets a foreground.

## Sprites over the border

The second exception is about the border. In the three orders where U is above the sprites, LUS, USL
and ULS, the ULA would hide every sprite behind its border, because the border is an opaque ULA dot.
Stage 2 makes one exception: where U's dot is the **border**, the tilemap is transparent and a sprite
is opaque, the **sprite** wins. So sprites that are allowed over the border (bit 1 of the Sprite and
Layers System register, NextReg `$15`) stay visible there in every order.

## Blending: adding instead of choosing

Two more values of bits 4–2 of the Sprite and Layers System register, NextReg `$15`, **110** and
**111**, do not order Layer 2 and U at all: they **add their colours**. Each channel of the two
colours, red, green and blue, levels 0 to 7, is added, and the sum, 0 to 14, is brought back into 0
to 7:

- **110: add and clamp.** A sum above 7 becomes 7. Adding brightens: a light source makes Layer 2
  lighter, a black one leaves it as it is.
- **111: add, then subtract 5.** A sum up to 4 becomes 0, a sum of 12 or more becomes 7, and anything
  between loses 5. With a source at level 5, Layer 2 is unchanged; a lighter source lightens it, a
  darker one darkens it. One layer can shade another both ways.

Two things follow from this order, and both surprise programmers who expect a blend mode to be "SLU
with adding". Let me warn you about them before they surprise you too:

- **The blend source shows only through Layer 2.** It is at the bottom, added to Layer 2, and it has no
  place in the order of its own. Where Layer 2 is transparent, the source is not shown at all: the
  dot comes from the layers above or from the fallback colour. With the ULA as the source, the
  Spectrum screen and even its border disappear wherever Layer 2 has nothing.
- **The tilemap can rise above the sprites while Layer 2 stays below them.** With the ULA as the
  source, a tile in front of the ULA is drawn above the sprites, and the blended Layer 2 underneath
  both: a split the six orders cannot make, since there the tilemap and the ULA move together.

Where the source itself is transparent, Layer 2 is shown as it is, in both modes. And a Layer 2 dot with
the priority bit shows its blended colour on top of everything.

## What blending is good for

So, what do you do with it? Blending is the Next's way of **lighting** a picture. Layer 2 holds the
detailed picture, 8 bits a dot, and the blend source holds the light: something coarse and cheap to
change, laid over it.

- **Light and shadow.** Make the ULA the source, with an empty bitmap, and give its attribute cells
  paper colours. In mode 111 a cell at level 5 in all three channels, the Spectrum's normal white,
  leaves the picture under it as it is; BRIGHT white lights it up, and darker colours shade it: a
  blue cell, levels 0, 0 and 5, darkens red and green by five levels and leaves blue as it is. A torch
  in a dark cave, a spotlight following the player, the shadow of a cloud: each is a handful of
  attribute bytes, 768 for the whole screen, instead of a redrawn 48K picture. The tilemap works the
  same way, with a light level for each 8 × 8 tile.
- **Fades and tints of the whole screen.** If every cell of the source shows the same palette entry,
  changing that one entry recolours everything at once. Raise it step by step in mode 110 and the
  picture fades to white; lower it in mode 111 and it darkens, until at black every level has
  dropped by five and only the brightest parts keep a glimmer; give it a little red and the whole
  picture flushes red, the classic flash when the player is hit. One palette write per frame.
- **Bands of colour.** Change the source's colour between lines, with the line interrupt or, better,
  the Copper, and the picture gets a sunset tint at the top and deep-water blue at the bottom, while
  Layer 2 itself is untouched.
- **Glow and haze.** Adding light to a picture is what glow, fog and mist look like: a pale source over
  part of the screen in mode 110 makes it look seen through haze.

The limits come with the format. Each channel has only eight levels, so steps are coarse, and the
clamps at 0 and 7 lose detail in the darkest and brightest parts. But for the cost of a few register or
attribute writes, the CPU never touching the 48K or 80K of Layer 2, nothing else on the Next comes
close. Here is my advice: whenever you are about to redraw a whole picture just to make it darker,
reach for blending first.

## When nothing is opaque

When every layer is transparent at a dot, in any mode, stage 2 shows the **fallback colour**, held in
the Fallback Colour register, NextReg `$4A`: an 8-bit colour made into nine bits like a write to the
8-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$41`. The machine starts with `$E3` there. Programs usually set
it to black, or, as the pictures here do, to a sky blue that then needs no layer at all. Free sky, no
memory required.

## How Klive does it

Klive's Next core has stage 2 as one function, `zxnextComposePixel`, written from the hardware's rules
branch for branch: the ULA and tilemap mix with its below flag and stencil mode, the six orders with
the priority bit and the border exception, both blend modes with their sources and order, and the
fallback. It takes the four values of a dot from the layer buffers that the big picture chapter's How
Klive does it describes, and the settings of NextRegs `$15`, `$68`, `$6B`, `$14` and `$4A`, and gives
one 9-bit colour.

The settings are recorded each time they change during a frame, so a frame whose order or blend mode
the Copper changes between bands is composed with the right settings in each band.

The same function can also tell you **which rule** chose a dot's colour: one of ten, the fallback, the
ULA or the tilemap through U, Layer 2 by the order or by its priority bit, the sprites by the order or
by the border exception, the stencil, or one of the two blends. That answer is what the IDE's
[Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view and its pixel probe show when you point at a dot: each layer's
value there, the settings in force on that line, and the rule that won. When a dot comes out the
wrong colour, that is the first place I would look. And if you want to read the code yourself, here
is where it lives:

## What to take home

Let's sum up the referee's rules. Stage 2 takes four colours, one from each layer, with their transparency answers
and flags, and makes one 9-bit colour. A layer is **transparent** at a dot when it is switched off,
clipped, or fails its own test: the colour against the value in NextReg `$14` for the ULA and Layer 2,
the dot against the values in NextRegs `$4B` and `$4C` for the sprites and the tilemap.

First the ULA and the tilemap become one layer, **U**: the tile on top, unless its attribute or
512-tile mode puts it below, and NextReg `$6B` bit 0 can force every tile on top. In **stencil mode**,
NextReg `$68` bit 0, U is opaque only where both are, and its colour is the AND of the two.

Then NextReg **`$15` bits 4–2** put S, L and U in one of **six orders**, values 000 to 101, and the
first opaque layer wins. The setting is taken for every dot, so it can change in the middle of a
frame. Two exceptions bend the order: a Layer 2 palette entry with the **priority bit** lifts its dots
above everything, and in the orders with U above S, sprites still show over the **border**.

The two values left over, 110 and 111, **blend** Layer 2 with a source chosen by NextReg `$68` bits
6–5, clamped at 7 or minus 5. The source shows only through Layer 2, and the tilemap can rise above
the sprites. When nothing at all is opaque, the **fallback colour** in NextReg `$4A` fills the dot.

Klive composes every dot with a C copy of these rules, and for any dot it can tell you which of the ten
rules chose its colour. Four candidates, one winner, and now you know how the referee thinks.
