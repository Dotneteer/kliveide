# Colours and Palettes: From an 8-Bit Index to a 9-Bit Colour

The Next can show 512 colours, yet not one of its layers can name a single one of them. Every layer
speaks in 8-bit **indices**, and a palette turns each index into a colour only at the very last moment.
Change one palette entry, and every dot that uses it changes colour at once, without a byte of the
picture being touched. And one value, `$E3`, means "nothing here" almost everywhere, so you can fill a
whole screen with it and see straight through. How can a colour mean "no colour"?

Why should you care? Because on an 8-bit CPU, the palette is the cheapest paintbrush you have. Rewriting a
picture costs thousands of bytes; rewriting a palette entry costs one register write. Water that flows, a
sky that darkens into night, a sprite that comes in eight team colours from one pattern, a picture that
fades to black: all of them are palette tricks. And the same mechanism decides what is transparent, so
sooner or later every Next program meets it, usually at the moment a hole in its graphics stops being a hole.

Read [the first screen chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) first: it
explains the layers and the pipeline the palettes sit in, including why the eight palettes live in two
memories. [The previous chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/) explains the
frame and how the picture leaves the machine; we pick up its thread at the very end.

## The road ahead

We start with a single colour: nine bits of it, and the 8-bit shortcut most programs write instead, with a
small trick that keeps full blue within reach. Then come the eight palettes, which layer owns which, and the
three registers that write them. Next we ask where each layer's index comes from: the ULA reads it from its
attributes in three different ways, and the other layers build it from their dots and a palette offset. The
key idea follows, and it is the one to read if you read nothing else: the Next has two quite different rules
for transparency, and `$E3`, the colour of nothing, sits at the meeting point of both. After that, the colours
the palettes hold at power-on, the effects palettes make cheap, shown on the machine, and how a 9-bit colour
becomes a signal on the cable. We end with how Klive does the same.

## Nine bits of colour

Every colour the Next shows is **RGB333**: three bits each of red, green and blue, levels 0 to 7, 512
colours in all. The first screen chapter shows every one of them. Three bits a channel is what the video
output carries, and nine bits is what a palette entry stores.

Nine bits is awkward for an 8-bit machine: one byte is one bit short. So most of the time your program
writes colours in an **8-bit form**, `RRRGGGBB`: three bits of red, three of green, and only two of blue,
the upper two. The missing lowest bit of blue is made by the hardware: it is **B1 OR B0** of the 8-bit value.

Why OR, and not simply 0? Because of the four blue levels it gives. With a 0, two blue bits would reach the
levels 0, 2, 4 and 6, and never full blue. With the OR they reach **0, 3, 5 and 7**: no blue and full blue
both, and two steps between them, nearly even. Neat, isn't it? One gate, and an 8-bit colour can be as
bright as a 9-bit one, which is what matters most.

Want all nine bits? Then write them in two goes, through the 9-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$44`. The
first write is the upper eight bits, the same `RRRGGGBB`, and the second write carries the lowest blue bit in
bit 0. Bit 7 of the second write has another job, for Layer 2 only: the **priority bit** that lifts a Layer 2
dot above every other layer, whatever their order.

## Eight palettes

There are **eight palettes** of 256 entries: a first and a second one for each of the ULA (which LoRes
shares), Layer 2, the sprites and the tilemap. Each layer shows one of its two at a time, and your program
can edit any of the eight, shown or not, with two independent choices in the registers:

Two of every layer is not luxury. Your program can prepare the second palette while the first is on

in the middle of one. Think of it as a stage with two sets of lights: the audience sees one while the crew
quietly rigs the other.

## Writing and reading a palette

Writing a palette takes three steps. Bits 6–4 of the Palette Control register, NextReg `$43`, pick the
palette. The Palette Index register, NextReg `$40`, sets the **index** of the first entry to write. Then each
write to the 8-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$41`, stores one 8-bit colour into the entry at the index
and moves the index on by one, so a run of colours goes into a run of entries without setting the index again:

Let's set four shades of red into Layer 2's first palette:

```z80klive
; Set Layer 2's first palette, entries 16 to 19, to four shades of red
    nextreg $43,%00010000   ; edit Layer 2's first palette, keep moving the index on
    nextreg $40,16          ; start at entry 16
    nextreg $41,%01000000   ; red 2
    nextreg $41,%10000000   ; red 4
    nextreg $41,%11000000   ; red 6
    nextreg $41,%11100000   ; red 7
```

With the 9-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$44`, it works the same way, with two writes per entry; the
index moves on after the second. A write to NextReg `$40`, `$41` or `$43` in between restarts the pair, so
your program can never leave half a colour behind.

You can read the entries back, too. Reading NextReg `$41` gives the upper eight bits of the entry at the
index, and reading NextReg `$44` gives its second byte, the lowest blue bit and the priority bit. Reads never
move the index on.

## The ULA: three maps of one palette

The palettes are only half of the story; the other half is the index each layer looks up. Every layer makes
its 8-bit index in its own way, and the ULA's way is the oddest, so let's start there.

The ULA's dots are not bytes but **attributes**: a bitmap bit says "ink" or "paper", and the cell's attribute
says which colours those are. The Next can read the same attribute byte three ways, and each uses different
entries of the ULA palette:

- **Standard**, as on the Spectrum: INK uses entries 0–7, BRIGHT INK 8–15, PAPER 16–23 and BRIGHT PAPER
  24–31. The border uses the PAPER entries. FLASH swaps ink and paper, as it always did. Change these 32
  entries, and you change the Spectrum's colours: old software keeps working, in new clothes.
- **ULANext** (bit 0 of the Palette Control register, NextReg `$43`): the attribute byte is split by a
  **mask** in the ULANext Attribute Byte Format register, NextReg `$42`. The bits the mask covers are the INK,
  an index from 0 upward; the rest are the PAPER, an index from 128 upward, and the border uses the PAPER
  entries too. A mask of `$07` gives 8 inks and 32 papers, `$FF` gives 256 inks and takes paper and border
  from the fallback colour. FLASH and BRIGHT are gone: their bits are colour now.
- **ULA+** (bit 3 of the ULA Control register, NextReg `$68`, or ULA+'s own ports): a standard from other
  Spectrum clones, with 64 colours in entries 192–255. FLASH and BRIGHT choose one of four groups of 16, each
  with 8 inks and 8 papers. ULA+ software writes its colours to port `$FF3B` in its own bit order, green
  first; the Next reorders them into `RRRGGGBB` and stores them in the ULA palette like any other colour.

## The other layers: a dot and an offset

The other layers do not have attributes; they have real pixels, here called **dots**. Each dot is a number
stored in memory, and the layer turns it into a palette **index**, a number from 0 to 255. How it does that
depends on how big a dot is.

**Think of an index as two hex digits.** An index is one byte, 0 to 255, or `$00` to `$FF` in hex. Each hex
digit is four bits, and you can draw the palette's 256 entries as **16 rows of 16**: the first digit is the
row, the second the column. Entry `$59` is in row 5, column 9.

**Dots come in two sizes.**

- An **8-bit dot** is a whole byte, `$00` to `$FF`: two hex digits, a complete index. Layer 2's dots in its
  256 × 192 and 320 × 256 modes are 8-bit, one byte per dot, and so are the dots of 8-bit sprite patterns.
- A **4-bit dot** is half a byte, `0` to `F`: one hex digit, so one byte holds two dots, the left dot in the
  first digit and the right dot in the second. 4-bit sprite patterns, tiles, Layer 2's 640 × 256 mode and
  LoRes in Radastan mode store their dots this way. They save half the memory, but a single digit can name
  only 16 entries.

**The palette offset fills the gap.** The offset is one more hex digit, 4 bits, `0` to `F`, that each of these
layers keeps beside its dots. What the layer does with it depends on the dot:

- **A 4-bit dot** has no first digit, so the offset **becomes the first digit**. Offset 5 and dot 9 make index
  `$59`. So the offset picks the **row** and the dot picks the column: a 4-bit sprite with offset 5 uses the 16
  entries of row 5, `$50` to `$5F`, and the same sprite with offset 6 uses row 6 and appears in a different
  set of 16 colours. The pattern does not change; only its row of the palette does.
- **An 8-bit dot** already has both digits, so the offset is **added to its first digit**, and the second
  digit is left alone. Layer 2 byte `$A8` with offset 1 shows entry `$B8`: one row down, the same column. If
  the first digit passes `F` it wraps round to `0`: `$F3` with offset 1 shows `$03`. With offset 0, nothing
  changes, which is why Layer 2 normally shows its bytes as they are.

The two rules serve two different purposes. For 4-bit dots the offset is what makes them useful at all: 16
colours per sprite or tile, but up to 16 different sets of them in one palette. For 8-bit dots it is a way to
shift a whole picture's colours to another part of the palette without rewriting a byte of it, for an effect
or to share one palette between pictures.

Every layer with dots keeps its offset somewhere else:

| Layer | Its dots | Its offset | The index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Layer 2, 256 × 192 and 320 × 256 | 8-bit | NextReg `$70` bits 3–0, one for the whole layer | the byte, offset added to the first digit |
| Layer 2, 640 × 256 | 4-bit, two per byte | NextReg `$70` bits 3–0 | offset, then the dot |
| Sprites, 8-bit patterns | 8-bit | in each sprite's attributes, one per sprite | the dot, offset added to the first digit |
| Sprites, 4-bit patterns | 4-bit, two per byte | in each sprite's attributes | offset, then the dot |
| Tilemap | 4-bit, two per byte | in each tile's attribute byte, one per tile | offset, then the dot |
| LoRes | 8-bit | NextReg `$6A` bits 3–0, one for the whole screen | the byte, offset added to the first digit |
| LoRes, Radastan mode | 4-bit, two per byte | NextReg `$6A` bits 3–0 | offset, then the dot |

The tilemap's **text mode** is the extreme case. Its dots are a single bit, on or off, so nearly the whole
index comes from the tile's attribute: a 7-bit offset, with the dot's one bit as the last bit of the index.
Every tile of text can have its own pair of colours.

Hey, keep one consequence in mind for what follows: an offset moves **every** dot, and for an 8-bit layer
that includes the byte your program uses for "nothing here". The next sections, on transparency and on what
palettes make cheap, show what that does.

there are two different rules for it:

The first rule, the **index rule**, makes a hole in a shape. A **sprite** dot is transparent when its pattern
value equals the value in the Sprite Transparency Index register, NextReg `$4B`, and a **tile** dot when its
4-bit value equals the value in the Tilemap Transparency Index register, NextReg `$4C` (for 4-bit sprites, the
lower four bits of NextReg `$4B`). The test is made on the dot itself, **before** the palette offset is
applied. That is what makes a pattern reusable: the hole is part of the shape, and it stays a hole whichever
offset a sprite or tile is drawn with, and whatever the palette holds.

The second rule, the **colour rule**, makes a colour that means nothing. The **ULA**, **LoRes**, **Layer 2**,
and tiles in text mode use it: a dot is transparent when its **colour**, after the palette, equals the
**global transparency colour**, held in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg `$14`. NextReg `$14`
is an 8-bit colour, and it is compared with the upper eight bits of the 9-bit colour, so the lowest blue bit
does not matter: two colours that differ only in it are both transparent.

These layers draw whole screens of colours, and for a picture "this colour means see-through" is the natural
rule. But because the test is made on the colour, it has consequences, and let me warn you: every one of them
will surprise you at least once.

- **Any index can be transparent.** Whatever entry holds the colour in NextReg `$14` makes its dots
  transparent, and two entries with that colour make two transparent indices.
- **A palette change can make dots appear or vanish.** Give an entry the colour in NextReg `$14` and every dot
  using it disappears; give the transparent entry another colour and the "empty" parts of the layer suddenly
  show. The section on what palettes make cheap shows both on the machine.
- **The offset moves transparency too.** The offset is added before the lookup, so with an offset the "empty"
  byte `$E3` points at another entry, with another colour.
- **Changing NextReg `$14` changes the meaning of every layer that uses it.** Set it to some other colour and
  that colour vanishes from the ULA, LoRes and Layer 2 alike, while every dot that was `$E3` becomes visible.

## `$E3`: the colour of nothing

NextReg `$14` starts at `$E3`, and so do the sprites' NextReg `$4B` and the fallback colour, while the
tilemap's NextReg `$4C` starts at `$F`. `$E3` is `111 000 11`, a bright magenta, a colour a picture rarely
wants. And in the default Layer 2 and sprite palettes, entry `$E3` holds the colour `$E3` (see the colours at
power-on, below). So out of the box, a Layer 2 byte `$E3` and a sprite pattern value `$E3` are both
invisible, by two different rules that happen to agree. Two locks, one key.

The same choice explains a detail of the ULA's default palette. Pure bright magenta, red 7, green 0, blue 7,
would be the colour `$E3` itself, and every bright magenta dot of the Spectrum screen would be a hole. So the
default ULA palette gives bright magenta one step of green. Spectrum fans, rest easy: your magenta is still
there, just a touch greener.

## The fallback colour

When every layer answers "transparent" for a dot, the picture still needs a colour. It is the **fallback
colour**, held in the Fallback Colour register, NextReg `$4A`: an 8-bit colour made into nine bits the same
way as a write to the 8-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$41`. The ULA uses it in two more places: in
ULANext with the mask `$FF`, as the paper and the border, and for any mask that is not one of the eight it
accepts.

## The colours at power-on

The palette memories have no reset: a reset of the Next leaves their colours as they were. It is the Next's
firmware that fills them when the machine is switched on:

- The **ULA palettes** get the Spectrum's colours: the eight colours at level 5 in entries 0–7, and BRIGHT at
  level 7 in entries 8–15, bright magenta with its step of green. The same 16 colours repeat in every block of
  16 entries, so PAPER (16–31) has the same colours as INK, and any offset or mode that reaches another block
  still finds Spectrum colours there.
- The **Layer 2, sprite and tilemap palettes** get the **identity**: entry *n* holds the 8-bit colour *n*. So
  until your program writes a palette, a Layer 2 byte or a sprite pattern value is simply an `RRRGGGBB`
  colour, and `$E3` is the transparency colour.

## What palettes make cheap

A palette sits between the picture's memory and the screen, so changing it changes the screen without
touching the memory. On an 8-bit CPU, that is the difference between one register write and thousands of
bytes:

- **Palette cycling.** Rotate the colours of a run of entries, and water flows, lights chase, a waterfall
  falls, while the bitmap stands perfectly still.
- **Fades.** Darken every entry a step at a time, and the whole picture fades to black in a few frames, at
  the cost of 256 writes per palette.
- **Switching.** Prepare the second palette and switch to it with one bit: day becomes night.
- **Recolouring.** One sprite pattern in eight colourings needs eight offsets, not eight patterns.
- **Changing it mid-frame.** A palette entry rewritten at every line gives one entry a different colour on
  each line: the sky gradients and colour bars of Next games.

For that last kind, and for most palette effects that follow the beam, the **Copper** is the best and the
cheapest tool. It is a small co-processor of its own, beside the CPU, that runs a list of up to 1,024
instructions in step with the beam: **WAIT** until the beam reaches a line and a position, and **MOVE** a
value into a NextReg. The palette registers are NextRegs, so a Copper list can rewrite a palette entry at any
line, or at several places along one: WAIT for the line, MOVE the index into the Palette Index register,
NextReg `$40`, MOVE the colour into the 8-bit Palette Value register, NextReg `$41`. With bit 7 of the Palette
Control register, NextReg `$43`, set, the index does not move on after a NextReg `$41` write, so a gradient
that recolours one entry needs a single MOVE per line.

Everything about it is cheaper than doing the same with the CPU. Once the list is written, it costs the CPU
**nothing**: no interrupt, no instruction, not a T-state, every frame, by itself. And it is **on time**: the
CPU answers a line interrupt only when its current instruction ends, a few T-states earlier or later from
frame to frame, while the Copper makes its MOVE at the position it waited for, every frame alike. The chapter
on [changing the picture mid-frame](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/raster-effects/#the-coppers-two-instructions)
explains the Copper in detail, and [the Copper guide](/en/guides/next/copper/) shows how to see a Copper list
at work in Klive.

## From palette to screen

The colour that leaves the pipeline is nine bits, and the outputs of [the previous
chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#from-counters-to-cables) carry it
on. The VGA and RGB outputs have three bits a channel, so the colour goes out as it is, through a small
digital-to-analogue converter for each channel. HDMI carries eight bits a channel, and the Next makes eight
from three by **repeating the bits**: `101` becomes `10110110`.

Repeating is a cheap and good way to stretch a value: it needs no arithmetic, only wires, and it gives the same
result as scaling by 255/7 and rounding. Level 0 stays black, level 7 becomes full 255, and the steps between
are within a unit of even. I find this one of the prettiest tricks in the whole machine: free, exact, and made of
nothing but wires.

## How Klive does it

Klive's Next core keeps the **eight palettes** as 256 entries each, nine bits of colour plus the priority bit,
and handles the palette registers, NextRegs `$40`–`$44`, and the Fallback Colour register, NextReg `$4A`, as the
machine does: the 8-bit form with the OR, the two writes of NextReg `$44`, the moving index and its NextReg
`$43` bit 7, and reads that do not move it. ULA+ colours written to port `$FF3B` are reordered and stored in
the ULA palette's top 64 entries. A reset clears the palette registers and leaves the colours; switching the
machine on fills them with the firmware's defaults described above.

Each layer's renderer, described in the first screen chapter, turns its dots into indices the way the ULA and
offset sections describe, looks them up in the palette the layer shows, applies its transparency rule, and
writes the 9-bit colour to the layer's buffer. The ULA's index function handles the three maps and FLASH;
Layer 2 and the sprites add their offsets; the sprites and the tilemap test their index before the offset. The
9-bit colours become screen pixels at the very end, through a table that repeats the bits exactly as HDMI does.

You can watch all of this in the IDE. The [Layer 2 inspector](/en/guides/next/layer2-inspector/) shows the
palette Layer 2 is using, its offset and the priority entries, and resolves any pixel to its byte, its index
and its colour. The [Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view explains why a dot has its colour, transparency
included. Next palette files (`.pal`, `.npl`) open in the IDE's
[palette editor](/en/guides/next/sprite-palette-editors/). And if you want to read the code yourself, here is

## What to take home

Here is the whole story in a few lines. Every colour the Next shows is **RGB333**, 512 colours. Programs usually write the 8-bit
form `RRRGGGBB`, whose missing blue bit is **B1 OR B0**, so blue still reaches levels 0, 3, 5 and 7; NextReg
`$44` writes all nine bits, and the Layer 2 priority bit, in two writes.

There are **eight palettes**, two each for the ULA, Layer 2, the sprites and the tilemap. NextReg `$43` bits
6–4 pick the one you edit, and separate bits pick the one each layer shows. NextReg `$40` sets the index, and
each NextReg `$41` write stores a colour and moves the index on.

The ULA makes its indices from attributes in three maps, **standard, ULANext and ULA+**. The other layers use
their dots with a 4-bit **palette offset**: it becomes the first hex digit of a 4-bit dot, and is added to the
first digit of an 8-bit one.

Transparency has **two rules**. The sprites and the tilemap test their **index**, against NextRegs `$4B` and
`$4C`, before the offset; the ULA, LoRes and Layer 2 test their **colour**, against NextReg `$14`, after the
palette, so for those three a palette change or an offset can make dots appear or vanish. **`$E3`** is the
colour of nothing by default, which is also why the default bright magenta has one step of green, and when
every layer is transparent, the **fallback colour** in NextReg `$4A` shows.

At power-on the firmware fills the ULA palettes with the Spectrum's colours and the others with the identity;
a reset keeps whatever they hold. Palettes make colour changes cheap: cycling, fades, switching, recolouring
and per-line changes, which the **Copper** makes at exact beam positions without costing the CPU a thing.
And at the very end, the 9-bit colour goes out as three bits a channel on VGA and RGB, and with its bits
repeated to eight on HDMI. Five hundred and twelve colours, and you now know the way to every one of them.
