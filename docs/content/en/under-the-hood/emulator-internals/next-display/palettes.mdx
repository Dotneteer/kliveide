# ZX Spectrum Next: Colours and Palettes

The Next can show 512 colours, yet no layer of it ever names one. Every layer speaks in 8-bit
**indices**, and a palette turns each index into a colour only at the last moment. Change a palette
entry and every dot that uses it changes colour at once, without a byte of the picture being touched.
And one value, `$E3`, means "nothing here" almost everywhere, so a whole screen can be filled with it
and stay invisible.

This article is about the palettes: how a colour is stored, how a program writes it, which palette
each layer reads and with which index, and why the Next has two different ideas of transparency. It is
the third article of the series. [The first](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/)
explains the layers and the pipeline the palettes sit in, including why the eight palettes live in
two memories; [the second](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/) explains the
frame and how the picture leaves the machine.

## Part 1: Nine bits of colour

Every colour the Next shows is **RGB333**: three bits each of red, green and blue, levels 0 to 7,
512 colours. The first article shows all of them. Three bits a channel is what the video output
carries, and nine bits is what a palette entry stores.

Nine bits is awkward for an 8-bit machine: one byte is one bit short. So most of the time a program
writes colours in an **8-bit form**, `RRRGGGBB`: three bits of red, three of green, and only two of
blue, the upper two. The missing lowest bit of blue is made by the hardware: it is **B1 OR B0** of the
8-bit value.

Why OR, and not simply 0? Because of the four blue levels it gives. With a 0, two blue bits would
reach the levels 0, 2, 4 and 6, never full blue. With the OR they reach **0, 3, 5 and 7**: no blue
and full blue both, and two steps between them, nearly even. An 8-bit colour can be as bright as a
9-bit one, which is what matters most.

A program that wants all nine bits writes them in two goes, through NextReg `$44`: the first write is
the upper eight bits, the same `RRRGGGBB`, and the second write carries the lowest blue bit in bit 0.
Bit 7 of the second write has another job, for Layer 2 only: the **priority bit** that lifts a Layer 2
dot above every other layer, whatever their order.

## Part 2: Eight palettes

There are **eight palettes** of 256 entries: a first and a second one for each of the ULA (which LoRes
shares), Layer 2, the sprites and the tilemap. Each layer shows one of its two at a time, and a
program can edit any of the eight, shown or not, with two independent choices in the registers:

```z80klive
; Set Layer 2's first palette, entries 16 to 19, to four shades of red
    nextreg $43,%00010000   ; edit Layer 2's first palette, keep moving the index on
    nextreg $40,16          ; start at entry 16
    nextreg $41,%01000000   ; red 2
    nextreg $41,%10000000   ; red 4
    nextreg $41,%11000000   ; red 6
    nextreg $41,%11100000   ; red 7
```

With `$44` it is the same, with two writes per entry; the index moves on after the second. A write to
`$40`, `$41` or `$43` in between restarts the pair, so a program cannot leave half a colour behind.

A program can read the entries back too. Reading `$41` gives the upper eight bits of the entry at the
index, and reading `$44` gives its second byte, the lowest blue bit and the priority bit. Reads never
move the index on.

## Part 3: Where the indices come from

The palettes are only half of the story; the other half is the index each layer looks up. Every layer
makes its 8-bit index in its own way.

### The ULA: three maps of one palette

The ULA's dots are not bytes but **attributes**: a bitmap bit says "ink" or "paper", and the cell's
attribute says which colours those are. The Next can read the same attribute byte three ways, and each
uses different entries of the ULA palette:

- **Standard**, as on the Spectrum: INK uses entries 0–7, BRIGHT INK 8–15, PAPER 16–23 and BRIGHT
  PAPER 24–31. The border uses the PAPER entries. FLASH swaps ink and paper, as it always did. A
  program that changes these 32 entries changes the Spectrum's colours, and old software keeps
  working in new colours.
- **ULANext** (NextReg `$43` bit 0): the attribute byte is split by a **mask** in NextReg `$42`. The bits
  the mask covers are the INK, an index from 0 upward; the rest are the PAPER, an index from 128
  upward, and the border uses the PAPER entries too. A mask of `$07` gives 8 inks and 32 papers, `$FF`
  gives 256 inks and takes paper and border from the fallback colour. FLASH and BRIGHT are gone: their
  bits are colour now.
- **ULA+** (NextReg `$68` bit 3, or ULA+'s own ports): a standard from other Spectrum clones, with 64
  colours in entries 192–255. FLASH and BRIGHT choose one of four groups of 16, each with 8 inks and
  8 papers. ULA+ software writes its colours to port `$FF3B` in its own bit order, green first; the
  Next reorders them into `RRRGGGBB` and stores them in the ULA palette like any other colour.

The offsets live in different places. Layer 2's is NextReg `$70` bits 3–0, one for the whole layer.

### The index rule: a hole in a shape

A **sprite** dot is transparent when its pattern value equals NextReg `$4B`, and a **tile** dot when
its 4-bit value equals NextReg `$4C` (for 4-bit sprites, the lower four bits of `$4B`). The test is
made on the dot itself, **before** the palette offset is applied. That is what makes a pattern
reusable: the hole is part of the shape, and it stays a hole whichever offset a sprite or tile is
drawn with, and whatever the palette holds.

### The colour rule: a colour that means nothing

The **ULA**, **LoRes**, **Layer 2**, and tiles in text mode use the other rule: a dot is transparent
when its **colour**, after the palette, equals the **global transparency colour** in NextReg `$14`.
`$14` is an 8-bit colour, and it is compared with the upper eight bits of the 9-bit colour, so the
lowest blue bit does not matter: two colours that differ only in it are both transparent.

These layers draw whole screens of colours, and for a picture "this colour means see-through" is the
natural rule. But it has consequences a programmer must know, because the test is made on the
colour:

- **Any index can be transparent.** Whatever entry holds the colour `$14` makes its dots
  transparent, and two entries with that colour make two transparent indices.
- **A palette change can make dots appear or vanish.** Give an entry the colour `$14` and every dot
  using it disappears; give the transparent entry another colour and the "empty" parts of the layer
  suddenly show. Part 6 shows both on the machine.
- **The offset moves transparency too.** The offset is added before the lookup, so with an offset the
  "empty" byte `$E3` points at another entry, with another colour.
- **Changing `$14` changes the meaning of every layer that uses it.** Set it to some other colour and
  that colour vanishes from the ULA, LoRes and Layer 2 alike, while every dot that was `$E3` becomes
  visible.

### `$E3`: the colour of nothing

`$14` starts at `$E3`, and so do the sprites' `$4B` and the fallback colour, while the tilemap's `$4C`
starts at `$F`. `$E3` is `111 000 11`, a bright magenta, a colour a picture rarely wants. And in the
default Layer 2 and sprite palettes, entry `$E3` holds the colour `$E3` (Part 5). So out of the box, a
Layer 2 byte `$E3` and a sprite pattern value `$E3` are both invisible, by two different rules that
happen to agree.

The same choice explains a detail of the ULA's default palette: pure bright magenta, red 7, green 0,
blue 7, would be the colour `$E3` itself, and every bright magenta dot of the Spectrum screen would be a
hole. So the default ULA palette gives bright magenta one step of green.

### The fallback colour

When every layer answers "transparent" for a dot, the picture still needs a colour. It is the
**fallback colour**, NextReg `$4A`, an 8-bit colour made into nine bits the same way as a `$41` write.
The ULA uses it in two more places: in ULANext with the mask `$FF`, as the paper and the border, and
for any mask that is not one of the eight it accepts.

## Part 5: The colours at power-on

The palette memories have no reset: a reset of the Next leaves their colours as they were. It is the
Next's firmware that fills them when the machine is switched on:

- The **ULA palettes** get the Spectrum's colours: the eight colours at level 5 in entries 0–7, and
  BRIGHT at level 7 in entries 8–15, bright magenta with its step of green. The same 16 colours repeat
  in every block of 16 entries, so PAPER (16–31) has the same colours as INK, and any offset or mode
  that reaches another block still finds Spectrum colours there.
- The **Layer 2, sprite and tilemap palettes** get the **identity**: entry *n* holds the 8-bit colour
  *n*. So until a program writes a palette, a Layer 2 byte or a sprite pattern value is simply an
  `RRRGGGBB` colour, and `$E3` is the transparency colour.

## Part 6: What palettes make cheap

A palette sits between the picture's memory and the screen, so changing it changes the screen without
touching the memory. On an 8-bit CPU, that is the difference between one register write and thousands
of bytes:

- **Palette cycling.** Rotate the colours of a run of entries, and water flows, lights chase, a
  waterfall falls, while the bitmap stands still.
- **Fades.** Darken every entry a step at a time, and the whole picture fades to black in a few
  frames, at the cost of 256 writes per palette.
- **Switching.** Prepare the second palette and switch to it with one bit: day becomes night.
- **Recolouring.** One sprite pattern in eight colourings needs eight offsets, not eight patterns.
- **Changing it mid-frame.** A palette entry rewritten at every line, by the line interrupt or the
  Copper, gives one entry a different colour on each line: the sky gradients of Next games. The last
  article of the series shows how.

## Part 7: From palette to screen

The colour that leaves the pipeline is nine bits, and the outputs of [the second
article](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#part-5-from-counters-to-cables)
carry it on. The VGA and RGB outputs have three bits a channel, so the colour goes out as it is, through
a small digital-to-analogue converter for each channel. HDMI carries eight bits a channel, and the Next
makes eight from three by **repeating the bits**: `101` becomes `10110110`.

Repeating is a cheap and good way to stretch a value: it needs no arithmetic, only wires, and it gives

the steps between are within a unit of even.

## Part 8: How Klive does it

### Eight palettes of nine bits

Klive's Next core keeps the **eight palettes** as 256 entries each, nine bits of colour plus the
priority bit, and handles `$40`–`$44` and `$4A` as the machine does: the 8-bit form with the OR, the
two writes of `$44`, the moving index and its `$43` bit 7, reads that do not move it. ULA+ colours
written to port `$FF3B` are reordered and stored in the ULA palette's top 64 entries. A reset clears
the palette registers and leaves the colours; switching the machine on fills them with the firmware's
defaults of Part 5.

### Lookup in the renderers

Each layer's renderer, described in the first article, turns its dots into indices the way Part 3
says, looks them up in the palette the layer shows, applies its transparency rule, and writes the
9-bit colour to the layer's buffer. The ULA's index function handles the three maps and FLASH; Layer 2
and the sprites add their offsets; the sprites and the tilemap test their index before the offset.
The 9-bit colours become screen pixels at the very end, through a table that repeats the bits
exactly as HDMI does.

### Seeing the palettes in the IDE

The [Layer 2 inspector](/en/guides/next/layer2-inspector/) shows the palette Layer 2 is using, its
offset and the priority entries, and resolves any pixel to its byte, its index and its colour. The
[Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view explains why a dot has its colour, transparency included. Next
palette files (`.pal`, `.npl`) open in the IDE's [palette editor](/en/guides/next/sprite-palette-editors/).

## Summary

- Every colour is **RGB333**, 512 colours. Programs usually write the 8-bit form `RRRGGGBB`, whose
  missing blue bit is **B1 OR B0**, so blue still reaches levels 0, 3, 5 and 7; `$44` writes all nine
  bits, and the Layer 2 priority bit, in two writes.
- There are **eight palettes**, two each for the ULA, Layer 2, the sprites and the tilemap. NextReg
  `$43` bits 6–4 pick the one a program edits; separate bits pick the one each layer shows. `$40` sets
  the index, and each `$41` write stores a colour and moves it on.
- The ULA makes indices from attributes in three maps, **standard, ULANext and ULA+**. The other
  layers use their dots with a 4-bit **palette offset**, added to the upper four bits of the index.
- Transparency has **two rules**: the sprites and the tilemap test their **index** (`$4B`, `$4C`)
  before the offset, the ULA, LoRes and Layer 2 test their **colour** (`$14`) after the palette. So for
  those three, palette changes and offsets can make dots appear or vanish.
- **`$E3`** is the colour of nothing by default, which is also why the default bright magenta has one
  step of green. When every layer is transparent, the **fallback colour** `$4A` shows.
- At power-on the firmware fills the ULA palettes with the Spectrum's colours and the others with the
  identity; a reset keeps whatever they hold.
- Palettes make colour changes cheap: cycling, fades, switching, recolouring, and per-line changes.
- The 9-bit colour goes out as three bits a channel on VGA and RGB, and with its bits repeated to eight
  on HDMI.
