# Layer 2: A Byte for Every Dot

Layer 2 is the simplest idea on the Next: one byte, one dot, any colour. No attributes, no colour
clash, no scrambled addresses. And yet, uhm, it has the most surprising memory layout of all the
layers. In two of its three resolutions the bytes do not run across the screen, line after line, as
in every other bitmap you have met. They run **down** it, column after column. Why on earth would
anyone build it that way?

Why should you care? Because Layer 2 is where a Next program goes when it wants a picture without
compromises: a backdrop painted in many colours, a photo, a drawing program, a screen the CPU can
fill dot by dot without ever thinking about cells. If you have ever fought the ULA's two colours per
cell, you know how freeing that is. But the freedom costs memory, lots of it, and knowing where those
bytes are, and in which order, is what lets your code draw fast.

Everything here builds on the
[big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) (the layers, the wide
area, clip windows and scrolling in general) and on
[colours and palettes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/) (how a byte
becomes a colour, and when it is transparent). If those are fresh in your mind, you are ready.

## The road ahead

We start with the idea itself, one byte for every dot, and what it costs, and then find where those
bytes live: in ordinary 16K banks, with a second, hidden picture beside the one on screen. Then comes
the heart of the matter. Layer 2 has three resolutions, and two of them store their bytes down the
screen; once you see how the hardware turns a position into an address, the odd order turns out to be
the obvious one, so if you read only one section, read that one. After that we squeeze two dots into a
byte at 640 × 256, and look at how your program reaches the picture, through port `$123B` with four
practical recipes, or through the MMU. Scrolling, clipping and colours follow, then the question of
how Layer 2 reads a byte for every dot without slowing the CPU down. We end with how Klive does the
same.

## One byte, one dot

The ULA spends one bit on each dot and one attribute byte on every 8 × 8 cell, so 6,912 bytes paint
the whole Spectrum screen, at the price of two colours per cell. **Layer 2** spends a whole byte on
each dot. The byte is a palette index, so every dot can have any of the 256 colours of the Layer 2
palette, which are any 256 of the Next's 512.

The price is seven times the memory, and seven times the work for a CPU that redraws the picture.
That is why Layer 2 could not simply be one more ULA mode. The ULA's memory is bank 5, 16K kept
inside the FPGA, as the [ULA chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/ula/#the-same-ula-new-memory)
explains; a 48K or 80K picture does not fit there. So Layer 2 is a layer of its own, with its own way
into the Next's main memory.

## Where Layer 2 lives

Layer 2 lives in the Next's ordinary RAM, counted in **16K banks**: bank 5 is the ULA's screen, bank 0
the Spectrum's usual top 16K, and a 2 MB Next has 112 of them. Bits 6–0 of the Layer 2 Active RAM
bank register, **NextReg `$12`**, name the bank where the picture starts; it continues in the banks
after it. A 256 × 192 picture takes **3 banks** (48K), the 320 × 256 and 640 × 256 pictures **5 banks**
(80K).

Beside it, the Layer 2 Shadow RAM bank register, **NextReg `$13`**, names a second picture: the
**shadow**. Layer 2 always shows the picture of NextReg `$12`; the shadow is never shown, and exists
only for the CPU, which can map either one into memory, as you will see in a moment. Your program
draws the next frame into the shadow while the active picture is on screen, then swaps the two
numbers: **double buffering**, with not a single byte copied. Neat, isn't it?

Two details, and the first one is a trap. The defaults, banks 8 and 11, suit the 256 × 192 mode: in
the wide modes the active picture runs into the shadow's first two banks, so if you want double
buffering there, set NextReg `$13` to 13 or beyond. And a picture placed so that it runs past the end
of the memory the Next can address has **no dots** there: those parts of Layer 2 are transparent.

## Three resolutions

Bits 5–4 of the Layer 2 Control register, **NextReg `$70`**, choose the resolution:

| NextReg `$70` bits 5–4 | Resolution | Bits a dot | Where | Memory | Byte order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `00` | 256 × 192 | 8 | the display area | 48K, 3 banks | across: line after line |
| `01` | 320 × 256 | 8 | the wide area | 80K, 5 banks | **down**: column after column |
| `10` | 640 × 256 | 4 | the wide area | 80K, 5 banks | **down**: column after column |

The 256 × 192 mode covers the ULA's display area exactly. The two wide modes cover the 320 × 256 wide
area, the ULA's display area with 32 dots of border on every side. Notice the last column of the
table: two orders. That is the surprise from the opening, and it deserves a section of its own.

## Why the bytes run down the screen

Look at how the hardware turns a position into an address. In 256 × 192, the line *y* and the dot *x*
are each a byte, 0–255 (the line only up to 191), and the address of the dot within the picture is
the two bytes **side by side**: *y* as the high byte, *x* as the low byte. No arithmetic at all, and
the dots run across the screen, because *x* is the part that counts up first.

Now try the same with 320 dots across. The dot no longer fits in a byte: it needs 9 bits, 0–319. So
"line high, dot low" would leave a gap, and the address of line 1 would have to start at 320, which
takes a multiplication, *y* × 320 + *x*. Hardware hates multiplying in a hurry. So turn the picture on
its side, and the problem disappears. The wide modes have exactly **256 lines**, so the *line* is the
coordinate that fills a byte, and the address is again two coordinates side by side: the column *x* as
the high part, the line *y* as the low byte. The dots now run **down** the screen, and each column of
the picture is one 256-byte page of memory.

So the rule is the same in every mode, and I find it easiest to remember it like this: whichever
coordinate is 256 long goes in the low byte. Here it is on the machine, with the same 80K of bytes in
both modes, eight colours, each painted over 2,048 bytes in a row of memory:

scrambled lines, moving down was the expensive one, so the tables have turned rather nicely.

## The CPU's view through port `$123B`

Layer 2 reads its picture from banks the CPU does not see unless it asks to. There are two ways to
ask: port `$123B`, which this section and the next one are about, and the MMU.

The Layer 2 control port, **port `$123B`**, can lay the picture over the bottom of the CPU's memory:

| Bits of port `$123B` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 7–6 | which part: `00`, `01`, `10` the first, second or third 16K at `$0000`–`$3FFF`; `11` the first 48K at `$0000`–`$BFFF` |
| 5 | not used (0) |
| 4 | 0 for this layout; 1 for the bank offset below |
| 3 | 0 maps the active picture (NextReg `$12`), 1 the shadow (NextReg `$13`) |
| 2 | 1: CPU reads come from Layer 2 |
| 1 | 1: Layer 2 is shown (also NextReg `$69` bit 7) |
| 0 | 1: CPU writes go to Layer 2 |

A write with bit 4 set stores a **bank offset** in bits 2–0 instead, 0–7 banks, added to the part
chosen in bits 7–6. The 48K window shows only three of a wide picture's five banks; with an offset,
any of them can be reached.

## Four ways to use port `$123B`

The separate switches for reads and writes are what make the port useful. With **writes only**, the
CPU still reads, and runs, whatever is normally at those addresses, the ROM at `$0000`–`$3FFF` for a
start, while every write there lands in Layer 2. Here are four common uses, each with the port value
that sets it up.

**1. Plot a dot, one third at a time** (layout 1, writes only, 256 × 192). The three thirds of a
256 × 192 picture are 64 lines each, and bits 7–6 of the line number *are* the third's number. So the
line's top two bits go straight into the port, and the rest of the line and the dot become the
address, H the line within the third, L the dot:

```z80klive
; Plot x = E, y = D (0-191) in the colour at L2Ink
Plot:   ld a,d
        and $c0                 ; y's bits 7-6 pick the third: port bits 7-6
        or $03                  ; bit 1: Layer 2 shown; bit 0: writes mapped
        ld bc,$123b
        out (c),a
        ld a,d
        and $3f                 ; the line within the third
        ld h,a
        ld l,e                  ; the dot
        ld a,(L2Ink)
        ld (hl),a               ; into Layer 2; reads of $0000-$3FFF still see the ROM
        ret
```

The routine can live anywhere, even in the area it draws through, because only its writes are
redirected, and only those below `$4000`: its stack can be anywhere from `$4000` up.

**2. Work on the whole picture at once** (layout 2, reads and writes, 256 × 192). With port value `$C7`
the whole 48K picture sits at `$0000`–`$BFFF` with no gaps: **H is the line and L the dot**, and your
program can read a dot, change it and write it back. This replaces one colour with another over the
whole screen:

```z80klive
        di                      ; the ROM's interrupt handler at $0038 is not there any more
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$c7                ; layout 2, reads (bit 2) and writes (bit 0) mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ld hl,$0000
Next:   ld a,(hl)
        cp $e0                  ; red...
        jr nz,Skip
        ld (hl),$1c             ; ...becomes green
Skip:   inc hl
        ld a,h
        cp $c0
        jr nz,Next
        ld a,$02                ; unmap again, Layer 2 still shown
        out (c),a
```

Let me warn you about this one: with reads mapped, everything the CPU fetches below `$C000` comes from
Layer 2, its own instructions included. So the code, its stack and its data must be at `$C000` or
above, and interrupts off. Forget that, and the CPU cheerfully runs your picture as a program.

**3. Reach all 80K of a wide picture** (layout 2 with a bank offset, 320 × 256). In the wide modes the
48K window holds columns 0–191, again with no gaps, but now **H is the column and L the line**: moving
down a column is `INC L`. For the columns from 192 on, a write with bit 4 set moves the window: with
bank offset 2 it shows banks 2–4, columns 128–319, and column *x* is at H = *x* − 128. This draws the
whole of column 300 in green:

```z80klive
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$c3                ; layout 2, writes mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ld a,$12                ; bit 4 set: bank offset 2
        out (c),a
        ld h,300-128            ; column 300
        ld l,0                  ; from line 0...
        ld b,0
Col:    ld (hl),$1c
        inc l                   ; ...down all 256 lines
        djnz Col
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$10                ; the offset back to 0
        out (c),a
        ld a,$02                ; unmap
        out (c),a
```

Port value `$C3` maps **writes only**, so this code can run **anywhere**, below `$C000` too: the
CPU's instruction fetches and other reads are not redirected and still see the normal memory. What
moves is every write below `$C000`: while the port is set, those go to Layer 2, so the stack, which
`CALL`, `PUSH` and an interrupt write to, must be at `$C000` or above, and so must any variable the
code updates.

**4. Draw off screen, then show** (bit 3, writes only). Port value `$CB` maps the **shadow** picture,
NextReg `$13`, while NextReg `$12`'s stays on screen, untouched. Your program draws the whole next
frame into the shadow, then swaps the two bank numbers at the frame interrupt, and the new frame
appears at once, never half drawn:

```z80klive
        ld bc,$123b
        ld a,$cb                ; layout 2, the shadow (bit 3), writes mapped, shown
        out (c),a
        ; ... draw the next frame with ordinary writes to $0000-$BFFF ...
        ld a,$02
        out (c),a               ; unmap
        halt                    ; the frame interrupt: the beam is past the picture
        nextreg $12,11          ; the shadow becomes the picture shown...
        nextreg $13,8           ; ...and the old picture the next shadow
```

With writes mapped, every write below `$C000` goes to Layer 2, the CPU's own pushes included, so the
stack must be at `$C000` or above while the port is set.

## Through the MMU

The other way in is the Next's MMU, the eight MMU slot registers, **NextRegs `$50`–`$57`**, which page
any 8K page of RAM into any 8K slot of the CPU's 64K. A 16K bank *b* is 8K pages 2*b* and 2*b* + 1,
so the default picture, banks 8–12, is pages 16–25. In the wide modes an 8K page is exactly 32 columns,
so your program can page in the columns it is drawing and leave the rest of memory alone.

## Scrolling, clipping and colours

Layer 2 scrolls by its own offsets: the Layer2 X Scroll LSB register, **NextReg `$16`**, with bit 0 of
the Layer 2 X Scroll MSB register, **NextReg `$71`**, as a ninth bit, and the Layer2 Y Scroll register,
**NextReg `$17`**. The picture wraps round at its own size:

| Mode | X offset | Wraps across at | Y offset | Wraps down at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 256 × 192 | 0–255 dots (NextReg `$71` is not used) | 256 | 0–191 | 192 |
| 320 × 256 | 0–319 dots | 320 | 0–255 | 256 |
| 640 × 256 | 0–319, in steps of two dots | 640 | 0–255 | 256 |

Its clip window, the Clip Window Layer 2 register, **NextReg `$18`**, counts in the area the mode
covers: in 256 × 192, the display area, one unit a dot; in the wide modes, the wide area, X in units of
**two** 7 MHz dots (four dots in 640 × 256) so that 0–159 spans the 320 positions, and Y one unit a
line. The clip window starts as 0–255, 0–191, which in a wide mode leaves the bottom 64 lines clipped
until your program writes 0–159, 0–255. Yes, that one catches people: switch to 320 × 256, forget the
clip window, and the bottom of your picture is missing. The
[big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#clip-windows-a-rectangle-for-each-layer)
has the details of both.

Its colours come through the two Layer 2 palettes; the palette offset of NextReg `$70` is added to the
high four bits of an 8-bit dot and becomes the high four bits of a 4-bit one. Layer 2 is
**transparent** where its colour, after the palette, equals the value in the Global Transparency
Colour register, NextReg `$14`, `$E3` after a reset. And a palette entry can carry the **priority
bit** that lifts its dots above every other layer, as the
[composition chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#the-layer-2-priority-bit)
shows.

Every one of these settings, and the bank and the resolution, is taken again for every dot, so a
change made in the middle of a line, by the CPU or the Copper, shows from the next dot.

## Fetching a byte for every dot

The ULA reads block RAM inside the FPGA. Layer 2 reads the Next's main memory, an external static
RAM shared with the CPU and the DMA. It needs **one byte for every 7 MHz dot** while it draws, a
read about every 140 nanoseconds, and the CPU at 28 MHz wants the same memory too. So how do they not
get in each other's way?

The memory is fast enough to be shared by time. It completes one access in every tick of the 28 MHz
clock, **four accesses per dot**, and each tick goes to whoever asks, in a fixed order of priority:
the **CPU** first, **Layer 2** second, and the **DMA** with whatever is left.

Layer 2 can afford second place because it is never in a hurry: it asks for each byte a dot ahead
of the dot it draws, so a request that waits a tick still arrives in time. The CPU never waits for
Layer 2 at all, at any speed; unlike the ULA's memory on a Spectrum, Layer 2's has **no contention**.
And the one byte per dot is also what sets the 640 mode's four bits: that is the answer the section
on two dots in a byte promised.

## How Klive does it

Klive's Next core draws Layer 2 in C with one renderer for each resolution:
`zxnextUlaRenderLayer2_256x192Screen`, `..._320x256Screen` and `..._640x256Screen`. Each works
through its area dot by dot, forms the address as the section on why the bytes run down describes,
line high in 256 × 192 and column high in the wide modes, applies the scroll with the same wrap at
192, 256 or 320, reads the byte from the banks of NextReg `$12`, and turns it into a colour through the
Layer 2 palette with the offset. It then writes either nothing, for a transparent dot, or the colour
with the priority bit into Layer 2's buffer, for stage 2 to compose. A bank past the end of memory
gives no dot, as on the hardware. The 640 renderer reads one byte per 7 MHz position and writes two
dots from it.

Klive does not model the memory sharing you met in the fetching section: on the hardware it never
delays the CPU or the picture, so there is nothing to imitate.

Port `$123B`'s mapping is part of Klive's memory decoding, `zxnextMemoryResolveLayer2Offset`, which
lays the chosen bank over the CPU's view for reads, writes or both, as the section on the CPU's view
describes. In the IDE, the [Layer 2 Inspector](/en/guides/next/layer2-inspector/) shows the picture's
banks, registers and palette, and the [Layers](/en/guides/next/layers/) view shows Layer 2 alone. And
if you want to read the code yourself, here is where it lives:

## What to take home

In short: **Layer 2** is a bitmap with **one byte for every dot**, a palette index, kept in
the Next's main memory: 48K or 80K in 16K banks, starting at the bank in NextReg `$12`. NextReg `$13`
names a **shadow** picture for double buffering, and only NextReg `$12`'s is ever shown. After a reset
they point at banks 8 and 11, which suits 256 × 192; move the shadow to 13 or beyond for the wide modes.

Bits 5–4 of NextReg `$70` choose **256 × 192** over the display area, or **320 × 256** and **640 × 256**
over the wide area. The address of a dot is its two coordinates side by side, with the one that
is 256 long in the low byte. That is why the bytes run **across** in 256 × 192 and **down** in the wide
modes, one column per 256-byte page. In **640 × 256** a byte holds **two 4-bit dots**, high nibble
first, because Layer 2 gets one byte per 7 MHz dot in every mode, and the palette offset in bits 3–0 of
NextReg `$70` gives those dots their high four bits.

Your program reaches the picture through **port `$123B`**, one 16K bank at `$0000` or 48K at
`$0000`–`$BFFF`, for reads, writes or both, with a bank offset to reach the rest; or through the
**MMU**, NextRegs `$50`–`$57`, 8K at a time. Layer 2 has its own **scroll** (NextRegs `$16`, `$71` and
`$17`), wrapping at its own size, and its own **clip window** (NextReg `$18`) in the units of its area;
it is transparent where its colour equals the value in NextReg `$14`.

Its bytes come from the main memory, which makes four accesses in every 7 MHz dot. The CPU gets first
pick, Layer 2 needs only one of the four, and so Layer 2 never slows the CPU down. Klive draws each
resolution with its own renderer, rule for rule as the hardware does. One byte, one dot, any colour:
the rest is up to your brush.
