# Raster Effects: Changing the Picture Mid-Frame

Every screen chapter so far made one quiet assumption: the registers stay still while a frame is
drawn. Well, they do not have to. The machine draws the picture one dot at a time, and every dot uses the
registers as they are at that very moment. Change a palette entry, a scroll offset or the layer order at
the right line, and one screen becomes several. So what happens when you refuse to sit still? I have
kept this chapter for last on purpose, because I think it is where the Next's screen stops being a set
of modes and becomes an instrument.

You get the effects that make a game look like more than its hardware. A sky with more colours than the
palette holds. A playfield that scrolls under a status bar that does not budge. More sprites than the
machine has. The CPU can do all of this, if somebody tells it when the beam reaches a line. But the Next
also has a small processor whose only job is to do it on time: the **Copper**, which waits for a position
of the beam and then writes a register, as exactly on every frame as the clock allows. The big picture
chapter treats the screen as a small theatre company; in that company, I would make the Copper **the
stage manager**. It never appears on stage itself, it does not paint a single dot, but it calls every cue
at exactly the same moment, night after night.

This chapter builds on the line numbers and the line interrupt of the frame-and-beam chapter,
[The Frame and the Beam: Two Counters, Four Timings](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/), and on the pipeline of
the overview, [The Big Picture: Five Producers, Four Layers, One Dot](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/). Keep them at
hand; I will point back at them more than once.

## The road ahead

We start with the one idea that the rest grows from: the picture is made at the moment the beam gets
there, so the beam is a clock. Then we look at how exactly a write shows, because not every part of the
picture takes its settings at every dot. Next comes the CPU's way of splitting the screen, the line
interrupt, and the shimmer that it cannot shake off. That brings us to the Copper, the heart of the
chapter: its two instructions, where seven of its WAITs stop on the screen, its memory, how a list ends,
its four modes, and why it lands on the same dot every frame. Three effects then put it to work: a sky
gradient, a split scroll and thirty-two ships made from eight sprites. Then we close the series by
following one dot through the whole machine, and I gather the whole screen up one last time.

## The beam as a clock

The picture is not stored anywhere. Every dot is made at the moment the beam reaches it, from the
layers' memory and from the registers as they are **then**. So a register written in the middle of a
frame changes the picture **from where the beam is**: the part of the frame drawn before the write keeps
the old value, the rest gets the new one. I find it the simplest idea in the whole series, and the
one with the longest reach.

That makes the beam a clock, and every position on the screen a time: 456 dots a line, 311 lines a
frame, the same every frame. Write a register at the same position in every frame, and you get a steady
picture with a band in it. Write it at a slightly different position each time, and the band's edge
flickers. Believe it or not, that is the whole game in two sentences; everything that follows is about
hitting the same position every time.

layer next reads it, which for a byte the beam has already passed is the next frame.

## The line interrupt and its jitter

The Next can interrupt the CPU before a line of your program's choosing: the **line interrupt**, switched
on by bit 1 of the Line Interrupt Control register, **NextReg `$22`**, with the line in bit 0 of
NextReg `$22` and the Line Interrupt Value LSB register, **NextReg `$23`**. It is raised on the line
before, near the end of its display area, in the line numbers that the Vertical Line Count Offset
register, NextReg `$64`, sets (the frame-and-beam
chapter's [line interrupt](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/timing/#the-line-interrupt)).

Your handler can then write the registers for the next band. That works, but it has a weakness: the CPU
answers an interrupt only when the instruction it is running has finished. At 3.5 MHz an instruction
takes up to 23 T-states, so the handler starts anywhere from 0 to 22 T-states later than it could, and
one T-state is two dots. A split made this way lands up to **44 dots** later in one frame than in
another (more, if contention or a wait state stretches the instruction), and its edge shimmers like a road in summer. Think of it as an actor who waits for the
director to shout his cue, and then first has to finish his sentence:

You can remove most of the jitter by waiting in `HALT`: a halted CPU runs NOP cycles of 4 T-states and
answers at the end of one, so only 0 to 3 T-states, up to 6 dots, are left. But then your program can do
nothing else while it waits. At 28 MHz the jitter is smaller, but it is still there, and
every split costs the CPU an interrupt and a handler. I am not a fan of making the CPU do this.
Wouldn't it be nice to hand the whole job to someone who has nothing else to do?

once it is started. If you read nothing else here, read this section and the four after it. The stage
manager knows just two instructions:

- **WAIT** (bit 15 = 1) waits until the beam is on **line** *n*, bits 8–0, at or past the horizontal
  position *h*, bits 14–9. The line is counted in the numbers that the Vertical Line Count Offset
  register, NextReg `$64`, sets, the same as the
  line interrupt's: line 0 is the first line of the display area, or of the wide area with an offset of
  32. The position *h* is matched at **dot 8 × *h*** of the display area, so a WAIT can stop at any 8-dot
  boundary; *h* = 0 is the first dot of the line's display area.
- **MOVE** (bit 15 = 0) writes the **value** in bits 7–0 to the **NextReg** in bits 14–8. Seven bits
  reach NextRegs `$00`–`$7F`: the palettes, the layer settings, the scroll registers, the sprite
  attributes and the MMU are all there. A MOVE to NextReg `$00` writes nothing; it is the Copper's NOP.

That's it. Two instructions, and no jumps, no loops, no arithmetic. I find it charming how much a
machine can do with so little.

Example 6 needs a word, because it catches almost everyone out, and I would rather you met it here than
in your own list. The Copper's horizontal count starts
**12 dots before** the display area, and its line number moves on there, at dot −12. So the border to
the left of line 100, dots −48 to −13, is still part of line 99 for the Copper: dot 408 of line 99 and
dot −48 of line 100 are the same place.

Example 7 reaches the top-left corner of the **wide area**, the 320 × 256 picture of the tilemap, the
sprites and the wide Layer 2 modes. It falls in the same left border, and its WAIT takes three steps to
find, and I suggest you follow them once with a pencil:

1. **Where the corner is.** The wide area begins 32 dots left of the display area and 32 lines above
   it. Line 0 is the display area's first line, and in a 311-line frame the line 32 before it is line
   279. So the corner is dot −32 of line 279.
2. **Which line the Copper counts it in.** Dot −32 is left of dot −12, where the Copper's line number
   moves on, so for the Copper it is part of the line above: line 278, at dot −32 + 456 = 424.
3. **Which *h*.** A WAIT stops at dot 8 × *h*, and 424 = 8 × 53, so *h* is 53. The WAIT is line 278,
   *h* 53: `$EB16`.

Both numbers depend on the timing. The line follows from the length of the frame, *h* from the length of
a line: on a 448-dot line, dot −32 is dot 416 of the line above, and *h* is 52. NextReg `$64` at 32 makes
the wide area's first line the Copper's line 0, so the corner is then on the last line of the frame,
whatever the timing:

| Timing | Frame | The corner | WAIT, NextReg `$64` = 0 | WAIT, NextReg `$64` = 32 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 128K/+3 | 456 × 311 | dot −32, line 279 | line 278, *h* 53: `$EB16` | line 310, *h* 53: `$EB36` |
| 48K | 448 × 312 | dot −32, line 280 | line 279, *h* 52: `$E917` | line 311, *h* 52: `$E937` |
| Pentagon | 448 × 320 | dot −32, line 288 | line 287, *h* 52: `$E91F` | line 319, *h* 52: `$E93F` |

And since a 128K line has 456 dots, *h* = 55, dot 440, is the last position a WAIT can reach; a WAIT with
*h* of 56 or more never matches. On the 448-dot lines of 48K and Pentagon timing the last one is 54.

Uhm, one more thing to keep in mind: a WAIT compares the line only for **equality**. If the Copper reaches a
WAIT for a line the beam has already passed, it does not shrug and go on; it waits for that line in the
next frame. A WAIT for a line that never comes, such as `$FFFF`, stops the list, and that is how a list
ends, as Where a list ends explains below.

## The Copper's memory

The Copper has **2K** of its own memory, **1,024 instructions**, with each instruction's high byte at the
even address. Your program writes it through NextRegs:

- The Copper Address LSB register, **NextReg `$61`**, and bits 2–0 of the Copper Control register,
  **NextReg `$62`**, set the byte address, 0–`$7FF`.
- The Copper Data 16-bit Write register, **NextReg `$63`**, takes an instruction in two writes, the high
  byte first, and writes **both bytes at once** on the second. A list can be rewritten while it runs
  without the Copper ever reading half an instruction.
- The Copper Data 8-bit Write register, **NextReg `$60`**, writes one byte at a time, each at once.

Both move the address on after every write. Notice how neat the 16-bit one is: you never have to stop the
Copper just to change a value in its list. My advice is to use NextReg `$63` for every list you write,
and keep NextReg `$60` for the rare byte you really mean to write alone.

## Where a list ends

Here is a surprise, and not one I would have designed: the Copper has **no end instruction**, and it does not know how long a list is. It
goes on to the next instruction in its memory, and after instruction 1,023 it goes on with
instruction 0. What lies beyond the last instruction your program wrote is whatever the memory held
before: an older list, or zeros, which are `MOVE $00, $00`, the NOP. A list that just stops being written
would run on through them, wrap round, and start again from instruction 0 at once, wherever the beam
happens to be, instead of at the start of the next frame.

So a list ends itself the only way the Copper allows: with a **WAIT that never comes true**. A WAIT for
line 511 never matches, because no frame has that many lines, and neither does one with *h* of 56 or more,
which no line is long enough to reach. `$FFFF`, WAIT line 511, *h* 63, is the usual choice. The Copper
then sits on that WAIT, patient as a cat at a mouse hole, until something else moves it on:

- in mode 11, the start of the next frame, which sends it back to instruction 0 (see the four modes below);
- a write to the Copper Control register, NextReg `$62`, that changes the mode, from the CPU;
- in modes 01 and 10, nothing: the list ran once and the Copper waits for good.

Stopping the Copper itself is a write of 00 to NextReg `$62` bits 7–6.

## The Copper's four modes

Bits 7–6 of the Copper Control register, **NextReg `$62`**, choose what the Copper does:

- **00, stopped.** The Copper does nothing, and keeps its place in the list.
- **01, run from the start.** The Copper starts at instruction 0 and runs on through its memory, wrapping
  from instruction 1,023 back to 0. It never restarts by itself: a list that ends with a WAIT that never
  comes runs **once**.
- **10, carry on.** The Copper runs on from the instruction where it stopped, and wraps the same way.
- **11, run from the start, every frame.** Like 01, but the list also starts again from instruction 0 at
  **line 0** of every frame, in the line numbers the Vertical Line Count Offset register,
  NextReg `$64`, sets. It runs once a frame, in step with the
  beam, whatever the CPU does.

Writing the mode the Copper already has changes nothing. Changing to 01 or 11 always starts from
instruction 0; changing to 10 does not.

So which one do you want? My rule of thumb is simple:

- **Mode 11 is the one for effects** that repeat every frame: the gradient, the split scroll and the
  sprites below all use it. The CPU starts the list once, and from then on only changes the values in
  it, for example the scroll value a split's first MOVE writes.
- **Mode 01** suits work that has to happen once. A list without WAITs writes its registers as fast as the
  Copper can, two clocks a MOVE: 256 palette entries in 128 dots, far faster than the CPU could. A list
  with WAITs and a final WAIT that never comes makes one change at one place on the screen, for example to
  switch a layer on at a given line in the frame after a game has loaded its graphics.
- **Mode 00** is for the times the list must not run: before a new list is written, or when a game turns
  its effects off.
- **Mode 10** resumes a list that was stopped, from where it stopped: a program that stops the Copper with
  00 for a moment, to rewrite instructions further down the list, can let it carry on with the same frame.

## Why the Copper is exact

The Copper runs at **28 MHz**, four clocks for every dot, and checks its WAIT at **every clock**. The
clock it sees the beam reach the position, it moves on, and a MOVE takes two clocks: half a dot. Its
writes go through the same path as the CPU's NextReg writes, but **ahead** of them, so the CPU can never
delay a Copper write. Nothing in it depends on what the CPU is doing, so a list does the same thing at the
same dot in every frame: the Copper's strips in the figure of The line interrupt and its jitter. No shimmer,
no handler, no interrupt. The CPU is free to run the game. That is why I trust the stage manager with
every split I care about.

The list is five instructions, run in mode 11:

```text
WAIT 151, 33           ; line 151, dot 264: right border
MOVE $26, 0            ; no scroll: the bar, lines 152-191
WAIT 191, 33           ; line 191, dot 264: after the bar
MOVE $26, 40           ; playfield scroll, for next line 0
WAIT 511, 63           ; stop until the next frame
```

How it works, one frame at a time:

1. At line 0 the scroll register already holds 40, written at the end of the previous frame, so the
   playfield is drawn scrolled 40 dots to the left.
2. At dot 264 of line 151, in the right border just after the playfield's last dot, the Copper writes 0.
   Line 152, the bar's first line, starts with the new value, and so does every line of the bar.
3. At dot 264 of line 191, after the bar, it writes 40 again. Nothing more is drawn with it in this
   frame, but it is there when line 0 of the next one begins.
4. The final WAIT never comes true, so the Copper stops there; mode 11 starts the list again at line 0.

Why not simply WAIT for line 0 and line 152 themselves, at *h* 0? Because the ULA takes its scroll per
8-dot cell, and reads each cell's scroll a little **before** it shows the cell (When a write shows). A
MOVE made at dot 0 comes too late for the line's first cell, which would keep the old scroll: an 8-dot
notch at the left edge of the bar. Writing in the border of the line before has the whole width of the
border to spare. Here is my advice: whenever a split has to be clean from the very first dot, make the
write in the border of the line before.

To scroll the playfield, the CPU changes only one byte: the value of the second MOVE, the instruction at
address 3, which it rewrites through the Copper Address LSB, Copper Control and Copper Data 16-bit Write
registers, NextRegs `$61`, `$62` and `$63`, once a frame. The bar is never
touched. I like how little the CPU has to know: one byte in someone else's list.

The list, run in mode 11 with the Vertical Line Count Offset register, NextReg `$64`, set to 32:

```text
WAIT 0, 0              ; the top of the wide area
MOVE $34, 0            ; select sprite 0
MOVE $35, 8            ; X = 8
MOVE $36, 24           ; Y = 24: band 0
MOVE $37, $00          ; palette offset 0: the grey hull
MOVE $34, 1            ; sprite 1 ...
MOVE $35, 38
MOVE $36, 24
MOVE $37, $00
…                      ; the same for sprites 2-7
WAIT 44, 0             ; band 0 ended at line 39: free
MOVE $34, 0
MOVE $35, 12           ; X = 12: a little further right
MOVE $36, 80           ; Y = 80: band 1
MOVE $37, $10          ; palette offset 1: the blue hull
…                      ; sprites 1-7
WAIT 100, 0            ; band 2: Y = 136, palette offset 2
…
WAIT 156, 0            ; band 3: Y = 192, palette offset 3
…
WAIT 511, 63           ; stop until the next frame
```

How it works:

- **One line numbering for both.** NextReg `$64` = 32 makes the Copper's line 0 the first line of the
  wide area, so a Copper line number is a wide-area row, and a wide-area row is a sprite Y. You can then
  write the WAITs in the sprites' own coordinates.
- **Four MOVEs a sprite.** NextReg `$34` selects the sprite; NextRegs `$35`, `$36` and `$37` then write
  its X, its Y and its third attribute byte, whose bits 7–4 are the palette offset. The pattern and the
  "visible" bit, in the fourth byte, were set once by the CPU and never change. Eight sprites take 32
  MOVEs, 64 clocks: 16 dots, a small part of one line.
- **When to move a band.** The sprite engine draws each line during the line before it. Band 0 covers
  lines 24–39, so the engine has finished with its sprites once line 39 has been drawn, and band 1, from
  line 80, needs them from line 79. Any WAIT from line 40 to line 78 works; this list uses line 44.
- **Each frame from the top.** Mode 11 restarts the list at line 0, where its first block puts the
  sprites back into band 0 before the engine reaches line 24.

The table has 128 sprites, so 32 ships do not need this; they are here only to show the trick. You need
it when a game wants more sprites on the screen than the table holds, or more on one line than the time
budget allows. My advice: reach for it only then, because a list that moves sprites is one more thing
to keep in step with your game.

## What to take home

Well, that is the whole screen, end to end, and I hope it feels like one machine now rather than ten. The picture is made dot by dot as the beam reaches it, from the registers **as they
are then**, so a write in the middle of a frame changes the picture **from where the beam is**. Most
settings are taken at every dot. The ULA's scroll, Timex mode and border wait for the next 8-dot cell, the
tilemap's settings for the next tile it fetches, the sprites' attributes for a later line, and NextReg
`$64` for the next frame.

The **line interrupt**, NextRegs `$22` and `$23`, lets the CPU split the screen, but the CPU answers only
when its current instruction ends, and that leaves up to **44 dots** of jitter at 3.5 MHz.

The **Copper** has none of it. It runs a list of up to **1,024** instructions from its own 2K: **WAIT**
for a line and an 8-dot position, **MOVE** a value to NextReg `$00`–`$7F`. It checks its WAIT at every
28 MHz clock and goes ahead of the CPU, so it writes at the same dot every frame. A list ends with a WAIT
that never comes, usually `$FFFF`; NextReg `$63` writes an instruction whole, and mode **11** of NextReg
`$62` restarts the list at line 0 of every frame. With it, one palette entry becomes **311 colours**, a
status bar stands still under a scrolling playfield, and **eight sprites** become 32. My parting advice
for your own programs: give every split to the stage manager,
and write in the border of the line before.

And that is the screen of the Next: ten chapters, one dot. Every effect you will ever see on it is that
dot, made at the moment the beam gets there, from whatever the registers hold right then. The rest is
up to your timing.
