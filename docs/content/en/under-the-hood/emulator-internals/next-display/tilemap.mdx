# The Tilemap: A Screen of 8 × 8 Tiles

Look closely at almost any scrolling game, and you will notice that the scenery repeats. The same brick,
the same patch of grass, the same pipe, over and over. Game consoles were built on exactly that
observation: store each little graphic once, and keep the screen as a list of which one goes where. So
what does the Next do with the idea, and how small can a full-width picture get?

It matters for time as much as for memory. When the scenery scrolls, the hardware moves the whole picture,
and your program only adds what comes into view: a new entry in the list for each cell, instead of every
dot of the new graphics. The Next's tilemap is that idea. A 320 × 256 picture would take 80K as a Layer 2
bitmap; the tilemap draws one from **2.5K**: a grid of 40 × 32 small numbers, each naming an 8 × 8 tile to
draw there. Each tile can be mirrored, rotated, given its own 16 colours and put above or below the
Spectrum's screen, tile by tile, and the whole grid scrolls by the dot. Bringing a new column of scenery
onto the screen means writing 32 map entries, 64 bytes; drawing the same 8-dot column into a 320 × 256
Layer 2 picture means writing 2,048 bytes, 32 times as many. Not a bad deal, is it?

Read [the big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) and
[colours and palettes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/) first: the tilemap
uses the wide area, the clip windows and the palette offsets they explain.

## The road ahead

We start with the bytes: the map that says which tile goes where, the tile definitions that say what each
tile looks like, and the small piece of memory, bank 5 and half of bank 7, where both must live. That
corner is cramped, so we work out how big each table is, which layouts fit, and one trap that catches a
program moving its tables into bank 7. Then we take a map entry apart: its attribute byte, a tile's 4-bit
dots, and the key idea, the eight orientations that mirroring and rotation give every tile for free. After
that come text mode, 80 columns, and how a tile decides whether it sits above or below the ULA. We finish
with scrolling and clipping, how the tilemap fetches its bytes one character ahead of the beam, and how
Klive draws the same.

## The map and the tiles

The tilemap covers the 320 × 256 **wide area** with a grid of **40 columns by 32 rows** of 8 × 8
cells. What it draws comes from **two separate areas of memory**, each with a start address of its
own:

1. The **map** says *which* tile goes where. It has one entry per cell, row after row, 40 entries to a
   row: the number of the tile to draw in the cell and, in a second byte, how to draw it (you meet that
   byte in the attribute byte's section). The map holds no dots at all.
2. The **tile definitions** say what each tile *looks like*. They hold the tiles' dots, 32 bytes for
   each tile, tile 0 first, then tile 1, and so on. They say nothing about where a tile appears.

The map refers to the tile definitions only by number, so any number of cells can name the same tile.
See what that gives you? change one tile's 32 bytes, and every cell that shows it changes at
once; change one map entry, and only that one cell changes. A whole meadow of grass can sway in the wind
from a single 32-byte write.

## Bank 5 and bank 7: where the tables can live

The map and the tile definitions cannot be anywhere in the Next's memory. The tilemap reads its bytes
through the same memory port as the ULA, and that port reaches only one kind of memory: the **block
RAM inside the FPGA**, which holds exactly **24K**: all 16K of **bank 5**, and the first 8K of **bank
7**. The rest of the Next's RAM, the main memory outside the FPGA, is out of the tilemap's reach; that
is why its areas are so small. (The ULA chapter's
[The same ULA, new memory](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/ula/#the-same-ula-new-memory)
explains why the screen memory is kept inside the FPGA.)

For the CPU, this block RAM simply *is* banks 5 and 7. Counted in the 8K pages of the Next's MMU,
bank 5 is pages 10 and 11, and bank 7's first half is page 14. Whenever the CPU reads or writes one
of those three pages, wherever it has paged them in, it uses the block RAM's other port;
there is no copy of these pages in the main memory. Bank 7's second half, page 15, is ordinary main
memory, and the tilemap cannot use it.

Each area is placed on its own. The Tilemap Base Address register, **NextReg `$6E`**, says where the
map starts, and the Tile Definitions Base Address register, **NextReg `$6F`**, where the tile
definitions start. Both have the same layout:

| Bits of NextReg `$6E` / `$6F` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 7 | 0: the area is in bank 5; 1: in bank 7 (its first 8K) |
| 6 | not used (0) |
| 5–0 | where in the bank the area starts, in steps of 256 bytes: 0–63 in bank 5, 0–31 in bank 7 |

So an area always starts at a multiple of 256 bytes. As the CPU normally sees bank 5 at `$4000`, a
value of `$20` puts an area at `$6000`; with bit 7 set, `$80` puts it at the start of bank 7, which is
`$C000` when port `$7FFD` pages bank 7 in at the top of memory, or wherever an MMU slot shows page 14.
The two areas choose their bank independently: the map can be in bank 7 and the tiles in bank 5, or the
other way round. An area that runs past the end of its bank continues from the bank's start: past 16K
in bank 5, past 8K in bank 7.

## How big the two tables are

The size of each area is not fixed: it follows from four choices your program makes in the Tilemap
Control register, **NextReg `$6B`**. Each gets its own section further on; here is only what each does to
the memory.

- **A map entry is 2 bytes or 1.** Normally an entry is two bytes: the tile number, and an
  **attribute** byte that says how to draw the tile (its colours, mirroring, rotation). When every
  tile can be drawn the same way, a program can drop the attribute byte and use one shared attribute
  for all tiles; each entry is then **1 byte**, the tile number alone (see the attribute byte's section).
- **The grid is 40 or 80 columns.** 40 × 32 is 1,280 cells. In **80-column** mode the cells are half as
  wide and there are 80 × 32 = 2,560 of them, so the map holds twice as many entries (see 40 or 80
  columns).
- **A tile is 32 bytes or 8.** A normal tile has 8 × 8 dots of **4 bits** each, two dots in a byte: 8
  rows of 4 bytes, **32 bytes**. In **text mode** a dot is a single bit, as in the Spectrum's font: 8
  rows of 1 byte, **8 bytes** a tile (see Text mode).
- **There are 256 tiles or 512.** A tile number is a byte, 0–255; in **512-tile** mode the attribute
  lends a ninth bit, and the tile definitions can hold twice as many tiles (see the attribute byte's
  section).

The size of each area is then a product:

| Area | One item | How many | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| The map | an entry: 2 bytes, or 1 | a cell: 1,280, or 2,560 in 80 columns | 1,280 to 5,120 bytes |
| The tile definitions | a tile: 32 bytes, or 8 in text mode | 256 tiles, or 512 | 2,048 to 16,384 bytes |

That has to fit in bank 5, which is only 16K, and the ULA's screen already uses 6.75K of it:

The sums are tight. A map of 2,560 bytes and 256 tiles of 8,192 come to 10,752 bytes, and with the
ULA's 6,912 that is 17,664: more than bank 5 holds. This is where bank 7 earns its place: its 8K is
exactly the size of 256 tiles. Coincidence? I doubt it.

## Layouts that fit

Here are four common ways to share the 24K, each with the values for NextRegs `$6E` and `$6F`. Bank 5's
addresses are those the CPU normally sees; bank 7's assume it is paged in at `$C000`. The bytes right
after the ULA's screen, from `$5B00`, are where 48K BASIC keeps its system variables, so these layouts
suit a program that does not return to BASIC.

| Layout | The map | The tile definitions | NextRegs `$6E`, `$6F` | The ULA's screen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **256 tiles beside the ULA** | bank 5, `$5B00`–`$64FF` (40 × 32, 2-byte entries) | bank 7, `$C000`–`$DFFF` (256 tiles) | `$1B`, `$80` | kept |
| **All in bank 5, fewer tiles** | bank 5, `$5B00`–`$64FF` | bank 5, `$6500`–`$7FFF` (216 tiles) | `$1B`, `$25` | kept |
| **512 tiles** | bank 7, `$C000`–`$C9FF` | all of bank 5, `$4000`–`$7FFF` (512 tiles) | `$80`, `$00` | gone: switch the ULA off |
| **80 columns of text** | bank 5, `$5B00`–`$6EFF` (80 × 32, 2-byte entries) | bank 5, base `$6F00`: characters 32–127 at `$7000`–`$72FF` | `$1B`, `$2F` | kept |

Two of these give something up. The first uses bank 7's first half, which is also where a 128K program
keeps its **shadow screen** (port `$7FFD` bit 3): the two cannot be used at once. The third fills bank
5 with tiles, so the ULA, which reads bank 5 for its own screen, would show tile bytes as a scrambled
picture; your program switches it off with bit 7 of the ULA Control register, NextReg `$68`. The text
layout's tile definitions start at `$6F00` but hold only characters 32–127: a character's tile number is
its code, so tiles 0–31 are never used, and their 256 bytes may hold anything.

## A trap: moving a setup from bank 5 to bank 7

Let me warn you about this one. The 8K limit of bank 7 is easy to forget, because to the CPU bank 7
looks like any other 16K bank. Here is how it catches a program out.

Suppose your program has the map at `$4000` and the tile definitions at `$6000`, both in bank 5: NextReg
`$6E` is `$00` (bank 5, offset 0) and NextReg `$6F` is `$20` (bank 5, offset 32 × 256 = 8K). It works.
Now the program copies all of bank 5 into bank 7, byte for byte, to free bank 5 for something else, and
switches both areas to bank 7 with the same offsets: `$6E` = `$80` and `$6F` = `$A0`. What could possibly
go wrong?

The map still works: offset 0 in bank 7 is the start of page 14, where the map's copy now is. The tiles
do not. The copy put bank 5's first 8K, the map, into page 14, the block RAM, but bank 5's second 8K,
the tiles, into **page 15**, the main memory the tilemap cannot see. And asking for offset 32, 8K into
bank 7, does not reach page 15 anyway: only the low 13 bits of the address are used in bank 7, so 8K
wraps round to 0, the start of page 14. The tilemap reads its tile definitions **from the map's bytes**:
tiles 0–79 are made of the 2,560 bytes of the map, and tiles 80–255 of whatever else was copied from
bank 5's `$4A00`–`$5FFF`, often the ULA's screen. The screen shows the right arrangement of cells, each
drawn as noise. Every tile is in the right place, and every one of them is garbage. (Klive's Next draws
exactly this: with `$A0`, the picture is identical to the one with `$80`.)

What works instead is to put everything the tilemap reads into bank 5 or page 14, never page 15:

- **The tiles in bank 7, the map in bank 5.** Copy the 8K of tile definitions to the start of bank 7
  and set NextReg `$6F` to `$80`; leave the map in bank 5 with NextReg `$6E` at `$00`. This is the
  "256 tiles beside the ULA" layout of Layouts that fit.
- **Both in bank 7, if they fit in 8K together.** A map of 2,560 bytes and 176 tiles of 32 bytes come
  to exactly 8,192: for instance the map at offset 0 (`$6E` = `$80`) and the tiles at offset 10, right
  after the map's 2,560 bytes (`$6F` = `$8A`).

After a reset, NextReg `$6E` is `$2C` and NextReg `$6F` is `$0C`: the map at `$6C00` and the tiles at
`$4C00`, inside the ULA's bitmap. So set both yourself before you switch the tilemap on.

When every tile can be drawn the same way, the attribute is wasted space. Bit 5 of the Tilemap Control
register, **NextReg `$6B`**, removes it: each entry is then one byte, the map halves to 1,280 bytes, and
every tile uses the Default Tilemap Attribute register, **NextReg `$6C`**.

Need more than 256 tiles? Bit 1 of NextReg `$6B` switches on **512-tile mode**: bit 0 of the attribute
becomes the ninth bit of the tile number, 0–511, and 512 tiles take the whole of bank 5. The bit no longer
says "ULA over this tile", so in 512-tile mode every tile is below the ULA unless bit 0 of NextReg `$6B`
puts the whole tilemap on top (see Above or below the ULA).

## A tile's dots

A tile is **8 × 8 dots of 4 bits**: 32 bytes, 4 bytes for each row, two dots in a byte, the high nibble
on the left. The dot at column *x* of row *y* of tile *t* is in byte *t* × 32 + *y* × 4 + *x* ÷ 2 of the
tile definitions.

A 4-bit dot is a number 0–15, and the attribute's **palette offset** becomes the high four bits of
the index into the tilemap palette, as the palettes chapter's
[A dot and an offset](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/#the-other-layers-a-dot-and-an-offset)
explains. So one tile can be drawn in any of 16 sets of 16 colours: a red and a blue enemy, or a
lit and an unlit window, are the same 32 bytes with a different offset. Bit 4 of NextReg `$6B` chooses
which of the two tilemap palettes the tilemap uses.

The tilemap's **transparency** is decided on the 4-bit dot before the palette: a dot equal to bits
3–0 of the Tilemap Transparency Index register, **NextReg `$4C`**, `$F` after a reset, is transparent,
whatever the offset. So the empty parts of a tile let the layers below it show.

The rotate bit always turns the tile **90 degrees clockwise**: there is no setting for the other
direction or for half a turn, because the mirrors provide those. The rule is: **rotate 90 degrees
clockwise first, then mirror**. Rotation moves the tile's dot (*u*, *v*) to (7 − *v*, *u*); then X mirror
takes the column *x* to 7 − *x*, and Y mirror the row *y* to 7 − *y*. The two mirrors together are a half
turn, 180 degrees, so rotate plus X and Y (`$0E`) is 90 + 180 = 270 degrees clockwise: 90 degrees
anticlockwise. I always remember it as "turn, then flip".

Now notice how neat the hardware is about it: it does all this without moving a single dot. To draw the
dot at column *x*, row *y* of the cell, it reads a different dot of the tile: it turns *x* into
7 − *x* for X mirror (and, because a rotation also flips across, for rotate too, the two cancelling when
both are set), turns *y* into 7 − *y* for Y mirror, and for rotate swaps the two before it forms the
address. Three bits of the attribute change three bits of the address: the transformation costs nothing.

Why eight? Because game graphics are full of symmetry. A pipe, a wall, a corner, a ladder, a character
facing left or right: one tile per shape covers every direction, and the tile set, which is the
expensive part of bank 5, shrinks by up to eight times. A corner piece, after all, does not care which
corner it is in.

## Text mode

Bit 3 of NextReg `$6B` switches the tilemap to **text mode**. A tile becomes a character in the way
the Spectrum's font is: **1 bit a dot, 8 bytes a tile**, one byte a row, bit 7 on the left. Two
hundred and fifty-six characters take 2K instead of 8K, and a copy of the Spectrum's font fits in 768
bytes.

A dot of one bit cannot pick a colour by itself, so the attribute does more of the work: its bits 7–1
become a **7-bit palette offset**, and the dot is bit 0 of the index. So every character picks its own
**pair** of palette entries, even for paper and odd for ink, from 128 pairs. The price is the other
attribute bits: in text mode there is no mirroring and no rotation, and bit 0 of the attribute is
colour too. Fair enough: few people want their letters upside down.

Transparency changes with it: a text-mode dot is transparent when its **colour**, after the palette,
equals the value in the Global Transparency Colour register, NextReg `$14`, the rule the ULA and
Layer 2 use, and not when its index matches NextReg `$4C`. Here it is on the machine, 80 columns of text
mode, with four colour pairs:

## Above or below the ULA

The tilemap and the ULA are mixed into one layer before the layer order is applied, and the attribute's
bit 0 decides, tile by tile, which of the two is in front: 0 puts the tile above the ULA, 1 puts the
ULA above the tile. Bit 0 of NextReg `$6B` overrides every tile and puts the whole tilemap on top. The
composition chapter's
[The ULA and the tilemap become one](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/#the-ula-and-the-tilemap-become-one)
explains the mix, and its stencil mode.

This is how a game puts a tile-based playfield behind a Spectrum-style status panel, or a text window
in front of the Spectrum's screen. It is also why the tilemap and the ULA share one memory: they are, in
the end, one layer.

## Scrolling and clipping

The tilemap has its own scroll: an X offset of 10 bits, bits 1–0 of the Tilemap X Scroll MSB register,
**NextReg `$2F`**, above the Tilemap X Scroll LSB register, **NextReg `$30`**, and a Y offset in the
Tilemap Offset Y register, **NextReg `$31`**. The picture wraps round at its own size:

| Mode | X offset | Wraps across at | Y offset | Wraps down at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 40 columns | 0–319 dots | 320 | 0–255 | 256 |
| 80 columns | 0–639 dots of 14 MHz | 640 | 0–255 | 256 |

The grid covers exactly the wide area, so a scroll never shows anything but the map: what leaves on one
side comes back on the other. A game that scrolls endlessly writes the next column of tiles into the
map column that has just left the screen, one byte or two per row, 32 rows: 64 bytes for a whole new
column. The landscape is a treadmill, and the player never sees the belt.

The tilemap's clip window, the Clip Window Tilemap register, **NextReg `$1B`**, works in the wide
area: X in units of two 7 MHz dots (four dots in 80 columns), so 0–159 covers the width, and Y one unit a
line. It starts as 0–159, 0–255, the whole grid, as
[the big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/#clip-windows-a-rectangle-for-each-layer)
describes.

## Fetching a character ahead

The tilemap reads bank 5 through the **same port of the block RAM as the ULA**. The ULA, which needs its
bytes at exact moments, goes first; the tilemap takes every cycle the ULA does not use. The block RAM
runs at 28 MHz, and the ULA uses only a few of its cycles, so there is plenty left.

To be ready in time, the tilemap works **one character ahead**. While the dots of one cell are being
drawn from a small buffer, it fetches the next cell into the buffer's other half: the map entry, one or
two bytes, then the tile's row, a read for each dot. When the beam reaches the next cell, its dots are
waiting, and the halves swap.

## How Klive does it

Klive's Next core draws the tilemap with one function, `zxnextUlaRenderTilemapScreen`, which works
through the wide area dot by dot. For each dot it applies the scroll and the wrap at 320 or 640, finds
the map entry at row × 40 (or 80) + column, reads the tile number and attribute (or uses NextReg `$6C`),
and reads the dot. In standard mode it goes through the orientation rule of Eight orientations, takes the
high or low nibble of byte *t* × 32 + *y* × 4 + *x* ÷ 2, and treats the dot as transparent when it equals
NextReg `$4C`; in text mode it takes bit 7 − *x* of byte *t* × 8 + *y*, transparent by its colour. It
writes the colour with the below flag into the tilemap's buffer for stage 2. Bank 7's 8K wraps as on the
hardware.

The fetch one character ahead is not modelled: on the hardware it never delays anything, so there is
nothing to imitate. Sometimes the best emulation of a detail is to leave it out.

## What to take home

To wrap up: the tilemap draws the 320 × 256 wide area from a grid of **40 × 32** cells of
8 × 8 dots, or 80 × 32 cells of half the width. It needs two tables: a **map**, one entry per cell, and the
**tile definitions**, both in the 24K of block RAM, bank 5 or the first 8K of bank 7, at the multiples of
256 bytes set in NextRegs **`$6E`** and **`$6F`**. Bank 7's second half is out of reach, and an offset past
8K wraps back to its start, which is the trap waiting for anyone who copies bank 5 into bank 7 wholesale.

A map entry is a **tile number** and an **attribute**: a 4-bit palette offset, X mirror, Y mirror, rotate,
and the ULA-over-tile bit, which becomes the ninth tile bit in **512-tile** mode. Bit 5 of NextReg `$6B`
drops the attribute and uses the one in NextReg `$6C` for every tile, halving the map. A tile is 32 bytes,
**4 bits a dot**; the palette offset adds the high four bits, and a dot equal to NextReg **`$4C`** is
transparent. Rotate 90 degrees clockwise, then mirror, and every tile has **eight orientations**, at no cost
at all: the hardware simply changes which dot it reads.

**Text mode** makes tiles 1 bit a dot, 8 bytes each, with a 7-bit palette offset that gives every character
its own colour pair and no mirroring; **80 columns** halves the width of a cell. The tilemap scrolls by its
own offsets in NextRegs `$2F`, `$30` and `$31`, wrapping at its own size, and clips in wide-area units with
NextReg `$1B`. It reads bank 5 after the ULA, **one character ahead**, and takes its settings afresh for
every cell, so the Copper can change them in the middle of a line. Klive draws it with one renderer, dot by
dot, by the same rules. The rest is your level design.
