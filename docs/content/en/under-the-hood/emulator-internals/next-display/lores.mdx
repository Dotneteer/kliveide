# LoRes and Radastan: Big Pixels in the ULA's Place

A 128 × 96 screen sounds like a step backwards. The Spectrum's own screen has four times as many dots, so why
would anyone want fewer? Well, on the Spectrum every 8 × 8 cell of the screen can show only two colours, and
you have to draw your picture around that. On a LoRes screen, **every pixel can be any of 256 colours**, the
whole screen fits in **12K**, and it scrolls in both directions without your program copying a single byte.

Why does that matter? Think of a game or a demo that wants colour more than detail, on a CPU that has to
redraw the screen 50 times a second. Fewer, fatter pixels, each in a colour of its own, is a very good trade
for it. The Next has two such modes. **LoRes** stores a byte per pixel. **Radastan**, a mode first made for
the ZX-Uno, stores 4 bits per pixel: 16 colours in half the memory. Neither is a layer of its own. Switch one
on, and it takes the ULA's place, and the rest of the machine treats its picture as the ULA's.

Read [the big picture](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/overview/) and
[colours and palettes](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/palettes/) first: everything here
leans on the layers and the palettes they introduce. The
[ULA chapter](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/ula/) helps when we get to the ULA's
clip window and border.

## The road ahead

We start with what a LoRes pixel is, four dots of one colour, and how 128 × 96 of them cover the display
area. Then we look for the bytes in bank 5, in two neat halves, and see how LoRes reads them without ever
getting in the CPU's way. Next, a byte becomes a colour through the ULA's palette and a palette offset.
Radastan comes after that, with its 16 colours in 6K and the two screens it can flip between. The key idea
is the one that follows: LoRes is not a layer of its own but the ULA in disguise, with the ULA's clip window,
border and transparency. We end with scrolling by half a pixel, with what a full screen costs to redraw, and
with how Klive does the same.

**128 pixels across and 96 down**, and it fills the same **display area** as the Spectrum's screen,
256 × 192 dots. So each LoRes pixel covers **2 dots across and 2 lines down**, four dots of one colour:

- The **top half**, rows 0–47, is at `$4000`–`$57FF`.
- The **bottom half**, rows 48–95, is at `$6000`–`$77FF`.
- Inside a half the rows are stored one after the other, **128 bytes a row**, left to right. Pixel
  (*x*, *y*) of the top half is at `$4000` + 128*y* + *x*; of the bottom half, at `$6000` + 128(*y* − 48) + *x*.

Did you spot what is missing? the scrambling. Unlike the ULA's bitmap, the next byte is the next pixel, and
the next 128 bytes are the next row. The gap between the halves is where the ULA keeps its attributes, at
`$5800`, and the two halves are exactly where the Timex modes keep their two bitmaps.

LoRes always reads bank 5, even while a 128K program shows the shadow screen in bank 7.

## How LoRes reads bank 5

Bank 5 is block RAM inside the FPGA with two ports. The ULA and the tilemap share one; **the CPU and
LoRes share the other**. LoRes reads one byte for every dot, through the CPU's port, and the CPU always
goes first. In each dot there is time for the CPU's access and for LoRes's, so LoRes never delays the
CPU and always gets its byte. Polite, and on time.

The ULA keeps running underneath. It still fetches its screen through its own port, and only its picture
is replaced by LoRes. So the timing of the machine, contention included, is the same with LoRes on or
off.

## From a byte to a colour

A LoRes byte is a **palette index**. It goes through the **ULA's palette**, the first or the second one
as bit 1 of the Palette Control register, **NextReg `$43`**, selects, and so any of the 256 entries can
be used. The ULA's ways of reading an attribute, ULANext and ULA+, do not apply: a LoRes byte is never
an attribute.

The LoRes Control register, **NextReg `$6A`**, holds a **palette offset** in bits 3–0. It is added to the
byte's high nibble before the lookup, as for an 8-bit Layer 2 dot: offset 1 turns byte `$25` into index
`$35`. With offset 0, the byte is the index. Radastan uses the same offset differently, as the next section
shows:

- A row is **64 bytes**. The left pixel of a pair is the byte's high nibble, the right one its low
  nibble.
- The rows follow one another with no gap: 96 rows of 64 bytes, **6,144 bytes**, in one block.
- The pixel's 4 bits are the low half of the palette index. The high half is the palette offset,
  NextReg `$6A` bits 3–0, so the offset picks which **16 entries** of the ULA palette the picture uses.
- With **ULA+** switched on (NextReg `$68` bit 3, or port `$FF3B`) and ULANext off, the high half is `11`
  followed by the offset's bits 1–0, so the picture takes one of the four 16-colour groups of the ULA+
  palette, entries 192–255.

## Two Radastan screens

A Radastan screen takes 6K, so bank 5 holds two, at `$4000` and at `$6000`: **display file 0** and
**display file 1**. Which one is shown is decided by bit 0 of the Timex control port, **port `$FF`**,
the same bit that picks the Timex modes' second display file, exclusive-or'ed with bit 4 of NextReg `$6A`.

That makes double buffering simple. Draw the next picture into the screen that is not shown, then flip
one bit, and the new picture is there at once. The XOR bit is there so that your program can keep port
`$FF` set the way other code expects, and still choose either screen.

Plain LoRes does not look at port `$FF`: its two halves are one screen, and the Timex mode does not move
them.

## In the ULA's place

If you read only one section, read this one. LoRes is not a sixth layer. When NextReg `$15` bit 7 is set,
LoRes's dot **replaces the ULA's** on its way into the ULA layer, and from then on the machine cannot tell
the two apart. It is the ULA wearing a different coat:

- **The clip window.** LoRes has no clip window of its own; it uses the ULA's, the Clip Window ULA
  register, **NextReg `$1A`**, in display-area coordinates. Outside the window the ULA layer is
  transparent, and whatever is below shows through.
- **The border** is the ULA's: LoRes covers only the display area, and the border around it keeps the
  colour port `$FE` gives it.
- **Transparency**: NextReg `$14` applies to LoRes colours as to ULA colours, as the section From a byte
  to a colour says.
- **Switching off**: bit 7 of the ULA Control register, **NextReg `$68`**, which switches the ULA off,
  switches LoRes off with it.
- **Layer order, stencil and blending** treat the LoRes picture as the ULA's
  ([putting it together](/en/under-the-hood/emulator-internals/next-display/composition/)).

## Scrolling by half a pixel

LoRes has its own scroll registers: the LoRes X Scroll register, **NextReg `$32`**, 0–255, and the LoRes
Y Scroll register, **NextReg `$33`**, 0–191. They count in **dots and lines**, not in LoRes pixels, so one
step is half a pixel, and a scroll moves smoothly. The picture **wraps round** at 256 dots across and 192
lines down: what leaves on one side comes back on the other, and your program never copies a byte to
scroll.

stay the same.

## How Klive does it

Klive draws LoRes in `zxnextUlaRenderLoResScreen`, in `zxnext-ula.c`, the C file of the Next's WASM core
that draws the ULA layer. When NextReg `$15` bit 7 is set, it replaces the ULA's renderer for the
display area, after the border has been drawn, which is why the border stays the ULA's. For every dot it
takes the ULA's clip window, adds the scroll, wraps Y at 192, and reads the byte from bank 5 at the
address the section Where the bytes are gives, or, in Radastan mode, the nibble from the display file the
section Two Radastan screens chooses. The index then goes through the ULA's palette and the NextReg `$14`
test, like any ULA dot.

The registers, NextReg `$15` bit 7, `$32`, `$33` and `$6A`, are kept in `zxnext-layer2.c`, with the two
address functions.

## What to take home

Time to pack up. **LoRes**, switched on by NextReg `$15` bit 7, draws **128 × 96 pixels** of 2 × 2
dots across the display area, a **byte per pixel**, any of 256 colours, in **12K**. The bytes sit in
**bank 5** in rows of 128 bytes, the top half at `$4000`–`$57FF` and the bottom half at `$6000`–`$77FF`, with
nothing scrambled. LoRes reads a byte per dot through the CPU's port of bank 5, and never delays the CPU.

A LoRes byte is an index into the **ULA's palette**, and NextReg `$6A` bits 3–0 add a **palette offset** to
its high nibble. **Radastan**, NextReg `$6A` bit 5, stores **two pixels a byte**: 16 colours in **6K**, with
the offset as the index's high nibble, or `11` and the offset's bits 1–0 with ULA+. Bank 5 holds **two
Radastan screens**, and port `$FF` bit 0 XOR NextReg `$6A` bit 4 chooses the one you see.

Both modes **take the ULA's place**: the ULA's clip window, border, transparency test, switch and position
among the layers. NextReg `$32` and `$33` **scroll** them by dots, half a pixel a step, wrapping at
256 × 192. Fat pixels, lots of colour, little memory: not a bad deal for a screen that looks like a step
backwards.
